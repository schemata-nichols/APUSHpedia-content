> **Patriots and Loyalists** were colonists who, respectively, supported resistance and independence or continued allegiance to the British crown during the [[American Revolution]]. Their choices reflected ideology, local conditions, family and commercial ties, religion, ethnicity, military events, and calculations of safety rather than two uniform social categories. Many colonists remained neutral, shifted positions, or tried to avoid the conflict. For APUSH, the division reveals that the Revolution was also a civil war within communities and that support for independence was created through persuasion, organization, coercion, and wartime experience rather than shared automatically by all Americans.

## APUSH Significance

Patriots defended consent, representative government, and eventually republican independence, organizing committees, militias, boycotts, and new state governments. Loyalists argued that reform within the empire was safer than rebellion, feared disorder, or maintained strong ties to Britain. Loyalism appeared in every colony and among varied classes, though its strength differed regionally. Patriot governments sometimes confiscated Loyalist property, required loyalty oaths, or punished dissent, showing that a movement for liberty also used coercive power.

## Choices and Consequences

Enslaved people and Native nations evaluated the war through their own struggles for freedom and land. Thousands of enslaved people sought British protection after royal officials offered liberty to some who escaped Patriot owners, while others served the Patriot cause. Many Native nations saw settler expansion as the greater danger and allied with Britain, although strategies varied. After victory, tens of thousands of Loyalists left for other parts of the British Empire while many remained. The categories therefore illuminate competing interests but should not erase neutrality, change over time, or groups whose objectives differed from both white Patriot independence and British imperial rule.
