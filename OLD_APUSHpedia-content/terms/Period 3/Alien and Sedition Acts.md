> The **Alien and Sedition Acts** were four laws passed by the Federalist-controlled Congress in 1798 during the undeclared naval conflict with France. They lengthened the residency requirement for citizenship, expanded presidential power over noncitizens deemed dangerous, and criminalized certain false or malicious criticism of the federal government. Federalists defended the measures as national-security protections; Democratic-Republicans viewed them as attacks on immigrants, opposition newspapers, and free expression. For APUSH, the acts reveal how international crisis intensified the [[First Party System]] and tested whether a government born in revolution would tolerate organized dissent.

## APUSH Significance

The Sedition Act was used to prosecute Republican editors and politicians, demonstrating its partisan application. The naturalization law weakened a growing immigrant constituency often sympathetic to Jefferson's party, while the alien laws enlarged executive discretion even when used less frequently. The measures exposed the limited early reach of the [[Bill of Rights]], since judicial doctrine protecting political speech had not yet developed and Federalist judges enforced the law.

## Resistance and Legacy

[[Thomas Jefferson]] and James Madison secretly authored the [[Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions]], arguing that the federal compact did not authorize unconstitutional restrictions and that states could challenge them. Their exact remedies differed, but later advocates of nullification drew on the language. Public opposition helped Democratic-Republicans win the election of 1800; the Sedition Act expired and Jefferson pardoned those convicted. The episode established no immediate Supreme Court precedent, yet it became lasting evidence that claims of security can suppress opposition and that partisan competition can both threaten and defend civil liberties.
