> The **Boston Tea Party** was a protest on December 16, 1773, in which colonists disguised in Native clothing boarded ships and destroyed East India Company tea in Boston Harbor. The [[Tea Act]] made company tea cheaper but preserved Parliament's tax and gave favored sellers a commercial advantage. Protesters feared that purchasing it would accept parliamentary taxation without colonial consent. For APUSH, the Tea Party mattered less for the price of tea than for the constitutional principle at stake and Britain's punitive response, which helped turn a local act of property destruction into broader intercolonial resistance.

## APUSH Significance

Boston activists organized by the [[Sons of Liberty]] prevented the tea from being unloaded or returned. Their action combined economic resistance, political theater, and disciplined destruction; participants targeted tea rather than carrying goods away for personal use. Reactions within the colonies varied, and some critics who opposed the tax also condemned the destruction of private property. The episode therefore did not reflect instant colonial unity, but it forced colonists to choose how far resistance could legitimately go.

## Coercion and Unity

Parliament answered with the Coercive Acts, called the Intolerable Acts by colonists, closing Boston's port and altering Massachusetts government. Other colonies sent supplies and delegates to the [[Continental Congress]], interpreting punishment of Massachusetts as a threat to their own liberties. This consequence made the Tea Party a major step toward the [[American Revolution]]. It also shows escalation on both sides: colonists defended local consent through direct action, while Britain defended imperial sovereignty through collective punishment, leaving less room for compromise.

## The Tea Act and Colonial Strategy

The Tea Act did not impose the first tax on tea or simply raise its retail price. It allowed the financially troubled East India Company to ship tea more directly to the colonies, reducing costs while retaining the existing Townshend duty. British officials expected legal tea to compete effectively with smuggled Dutch tea and demonstrate Parliament's accepted right to tax.

Opponents recognized that purchasing the cheaper tea could establish a precedent of consent. Merchants also resented the privileged distribution arrangement. In several ports, pressure forced ships to turn back or prevented unloading. Massachusetts governor Thomas Hutchinson refused to let the Dartmouth depart without meeting customs requirements, creating a standoff in Boston.

## Destruction and Political Meaning

On December 16, 1773, after a large public meeting, men boarded three ships and emptied 342 chests of tea into the harbor. Some disguised themselves in ways intended to evoke Native identity. The action was disciplined destruction of a targeted commodity rather than an uncontrolled attack on people, but it still destroyed valuable private property and divided colonial opinion.

Participants protected anonymity because their action was illegal. Later national memory turned the event into a simple patriotic festival, obscuring contemporary disagreement over tactics. Benjamin Franklin and other colonial critics of Parliament could still believe compensation was owed. The episode shows that resistance contained debates over law, property, popular action, and the limits of protest.

## Coercion and Colonial Unity

Parliament answered with the Coercive Acts, called the Intolerable Acts by colonists. These measures closed Boston's port, altered Massachusetts government, and strengthened mechanisms of imperial enforcement. Rather than isolating Massachusetts, punishment encouraged other colonies to send aid and view the attack on one colony's institutions as a general threat.

That reaction helped produce the First [[Continental Congress]]. The Tea Party therefore mattered less for the tea itself than for the chain of action and response: a dispute over parliamentary authority prompted property destruction, imperial coercion, intercolonial solidarity, and organized resistance. Independence still was not the declared goal, but compromise became more difficult as both sides defended incompatible understandings of sovereignty.
