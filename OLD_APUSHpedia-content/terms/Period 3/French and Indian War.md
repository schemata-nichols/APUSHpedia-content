> The **French and Indian War** was the North American phase of the wider Seven Years' War, fought from 1754 to 1763 among Britain, France, their colonists, and allied Native nations. Competition over the Ohio River Valley began the conflict, while Native peoples pursued their own security and territorial interests. British victory removed France as a major mainland power and transferred Canada to Britain, but wartime debt and western administration led Parliament to tax and regulate the colonies more aggressively. For APUSH, the war was therefore both an imperial victory and the central context for the [[American Revolution]]: it changed colonial expectations, Native diplomacy, British policy, and the political cost of empire.

## APUSH Significance

The war reorganized power throughout British North America:

1. **The Ohio Valley triggered imperial conflict.** French forts challenged British land claims and colonial speculation.
2. **Native nations shaped the war.** Alliances reflected local interests, not automatic loyalty to one empire.
3. **British victory expanded responsibility.** Britain gained territory while assuming greater defense and administrative costs.
4. **Colonists gained military experience and confidence.** Cooperation remained limited, but the war created shared grievances and leaders.
5. **The postwar settlement produced resistance.** The [[Proclamation of 1763]] and parliamentary taxation challenged colonial expectations.

The war should not be treated as a simple Britain-versus-France contest. Native nations were independent diplomatic and military actors whose choices often determined frontier power.

## Causes and Early Fighting

Britain and France both claimed the Ohio River Valley, where Native communities traded and governed territory. Virginia land speculators sought western expansion, while France built forts connecting Canada to the Mississippi Valley. In 1754, a young [[George Washington]]'s clash with French forces helped begin open war.

At the Albany Congress, colonial delegates discussed defense and relations with the Haudenosaunee. Benjamin Franklin's [[Albany Plan of Union]] proposed intercolonial coordination, but colonial assemblies and the crown rejected it because each feared losing authority.

Early British campaigns suffered from divided command and unfamiliarity with frontier warfare. General Edward Braddock's defeat demonstrated the limits of European tactics and the importance of Native alliances and local knowledge.

## Imperial and Native War

The conflict widened into the Seven Years' War across Europe, the Caribbean, Africa, and Asia. Britain committed greater resources under William Pitt, captured Quebec in 1759, and defeated French power in Canada.

Native alliances shifted with military conditions. Many nations sought to preserve autonomy by balancing empires; the removal of France reduced that leverage. British officials also changed gift-giving and trade practices that had helped sustain diplomatic relationships.

After the main war, [[Pontiac's Rebellion]] challenged British forts and settlement in the Great Lakes and Ohio regions. The uprising showed that the Treaty of Paris could transfer European claims without obtaining Native consent.

## Settlement and Consequences

The Treaty of Paris of 1763 gave Britain Canada and territory east of the Mississippi, while Spain received Louisiana west of the river. France lost most of its mainland North American position.

To reduce frontier warfare and administrative expense, Britain issued the Proclamation of 1763, restricting settlement west of the Appalachians. Colonists and land speculators resented the boundary and often ignored it. Native peoples viewed it as inadequate protection rather than a permanent guarantee.

Britain's debt encouraged new revenue measures, including the [[Sugar Act]] and [[Stamp Act]]. Parliament believed colonists should help pay for imperial defense; colonists argued that taxation without elected representation violated their rights.

## From Victory to Revolution

Before 1763, many colonists identified proudly as British subjects. The war strengthened that identity through victory but also exposed disagreements. British officers criticized colonial troops, colonists resented military discipline, and assemblies defended control over taxation and recruitment.

Postwar policy transformed those tensions into a constitutional dispute. Enforcement, standing troops, western restrictions, and taxes suggested that Britain intended to reduce colonial autonomy. Resistance escalated through petitions, boycotts, intercolonial congresses, and crowd action.

The war did not make revolution inevitable. Compromise remained possible, and most colonists initially sought rights within the empire. Its importance lies in changing the conditions under which later disputes occurred.

## Imperial Victory and Colonial Crisis

The French and Indian War is best understood as a turning point rather than a direct cause acting alone. Victory enlarged Britain's empire but made governing it more expensive and politically difficult.

The conflict also marks a loss of Native diplomatic leverage. With France largely removed, British settlers and officials faced fewer imperial limits on westward expansion, deepening the struggle over Native sovereignty that continued after independence.
