> The **Land Ordinance of 1785** created a federal system for surveying and selling western land north of the Ohio River. It divided territory into rectangular townships and sections, establishing the grid visible across much of the United States, and reserved land in each township to support public education. Congress hoped land sales would raise revenue because the [[Articles of Confederation]] denied it an effective power to tax. For APUSH, the ordinance was a significant achievement of the Confederation government, but its orderly map treated Native homelands as transferable public property and made survey and sale instruments of [[Westward Expansion]].

## APUSH Significance

The ordinance required survey before settlement, replacing irregular colonial claims with a standardized national system. Townships six miles square were divided into thirty-six sections, which could then be sold. Large initial purchase requirements tended to favor speculators and companies rather than poorer individual farmers, though parcels were later subdivided. Reserving Section 16 for schools established an enduring link between federal land policy and public education.

## Land, Revenue, and Dispossession

The policy addressed two Confederation problems: overlapping state western claims and the national government's shortage of revenue. Combined with the [[Northwest Ordinance of 1787]], it provided a process for turning territory into organized settlement and eventually states. Yet surveying did not establish uncontested ownership. Native nations remained on much of the land and resisted U.S. expansion, so military force and unequal treaties accompanied the geometric system. The ordinance demonstrates that a weak central government could still shape national development powerfully when it controlled western land.
