> The **Declaration of Independence** was the document adopted by the Second [[Continental Congress]] on July 4, 1776, announcing that the thirteen colonies were independent states. Drafted chiefly by [[Thomas Jefferson]] and revised by Congress, it drew on Enlightenment natural rights and republican consent to justify revolution, then listed grievances against King George III. The declaration did not create a government, abolish slavery, or establish equal citizenship. For APUSH, its importance lies in both immediate diplomacy and long-term political language: claims that all people possess rights and may alter unjust government became standards used by abolitionists, women's-rights advocates, civil-rights activists, and independence movements.

## APUSH Significance

The declaration transformed rebellion into a claim of legitimate nationhood:

1. **It explained separation to several audiences.** Congress addressed colonists, foreign powers, and the British public.
2. **Natural rights justified resistance.** Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness existed before government.
3. **Consent defined legitimate authority.** Government derived “just powers” from the governed.
4. **Grievances made the case concrete.** The document accused the king of violating colonial government and rights.
5. **Universal language exceeded immediate practice.** Later movements applied its principles to people excluded in 1776.

The declaration differs from the [[United States Constitution]]. It justified independence; the Constitution later designed a federal government.

## Context and Decision

Fighting had continued for more than a year after [[Lexington and Concord]], but many colonists initially sought reconciliation. The king rejected the [[Olive Branch Petition]], Britain declared the colonies in rebellion, and military conflict widened.

Thomas Paine's [[Common Sense]] attacked hereditary monarchy and argued that independence offered political and commercial opportunity. In June 1776, Richard Henry Lee proposed that the colonies become free and independent states.

Congress appointed a committee including Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, and Robert Livingston. Jefferson drafted the text, drawing on colonial grievances, George Mason's Virginia Declaration of Rights, and Enlightenment ideas.

## Principles and Grievances

The opening asserted human equality and unalienable rights. Government existed to secure those rights and could be altered or abolished when it became destructive of them. This argument combined [[Natural Rights]], [[Popular Sovereignty]], and the social contract.

The document then accused George III of obstructing laws, manipulating legislatures and courts, maintaining troops, imposing taxes without consent, and waging war against the colonies. It presented independence as a response to a pattern of tyranny rather than one disputed tax.

Congress directed its charges toward the king even though Parliament had enacted many policies. Focusing blame on the monarch helped justify rejection of the entire imperial system.

## Revision and Exclusion

Congress edited Jefferson's draft to produce collective agreement. It removed a passage blaming the king for the slave trade, partly because delegates from slaveholding and commercial colonies objected. The final declaration did not condemn slavery.

“All men are created equal” did not mean that political and civil rights became universal. Enslaved people remained property, women lacked equal political authority, and Native peoples were described in hostile language among the grievances.

These exclusions do not make the principles meaningless. They reveal a conflict between universal claims and a social order that restricted who could invoke them effectively.

## Immediate Purposes

Independence allowed the states to pursue formal alliances and commerce. The declaration helped present the American cause as a legitimate struggle rather than domestic disorder, contributing to the diplomatic setting for the [[French Alliance]].

It also required colonists to choose political allegiance. Patriots celebrated independence, Loyalists rejected it, and many people made decisions under local pressure and wartime uncertainty.

The document itself did not win independence. Military endurance, state-building, foreign assistance, and the [[American Revolution|Revolutionary War]] determined whether its claim would survive.

## Long-Term Uses

Abolitionists invoked the declaration against [[Slavery]], arguing that human equality contradicted bondage. At the [[Seneca Falls Convention]], the Declaration of Sentiments adapted its structure to demand women's equality.

Abraham Lincoln treated the declaration's equality principle as central to national purpose during the sectional crisis and [[Civil War]]. Later civil-rights leaders likewise used it to contrast American ideals with segregation and disfranchisement.

Its international influence extended to movements that adopted declarations of rights and national independence, though American foreign policy did not consistently support self-determination.

## Universal Principles and Unequal Application

The declaration is simultaneously a diplomatic act, a revolutionary argument, and an unfinished promise. Reading only its ideals ignores its exclusions; reading only its exclusions misses why those ideals became tools for change.

Its continuing power comes from that tension. Americans have repeatedly disagreed over whether the document merely described independence in 1776 or established a moral standard for the nation afterward.
