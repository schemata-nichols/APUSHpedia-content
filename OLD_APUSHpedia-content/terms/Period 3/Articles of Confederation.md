> The **Articles of Confederation** were the first national constitution of the United States, adopted by Congress in 1777 and fully ratified in 1781. They created a confederation in which states retained sovereignty and a one-house Congress managed war, diplomacy, western lands, and other shared concerns. Congress could request money but could not tax individuals, regulate interstate commerce, or enforce laws through separate executive and judicial branches. The system successfully directed independence and organized western settlement, yet fiscal weakness and interstate conflict encouraged constitutional reform. For APUSH, the Articles show that the founders first treated centralized power—not weak government—as the greater threat to republican liberty.

## APUSH Significance

The Articles reveal both the achievements and limits of confederation:

1. **State sovereignty dominated.** Each state retained extensive authority and one vote in Congress.
2. **Congress lacked independent revenue.** It depended on state requisitions and borrowing.
3. **Amendment was extremely difficult.** Changes required unanimous state approval.
4. **Western policy was a major success.** Land ordinances created rules for sale, government, and statehood.
5. **Economic and political crises encouraged replacement.** Reformers argued that national problems required enforceable national power.

The Articles did not create no government. Congress possessed important powers, but it lacked reliable means to carry them out.

## Origins and Structure

Revolutionary Americans associated centralized authority with British taxation, standing armies, and executive abuse. Their first national framework therefore protected states and avoided a separate president or national judiciary.

Congress could conduct diplomacy, declare war, make treaties, coin money, and resolve some disputes. Major decisions required nine states, while amendments required all thirteen. States could ignore requests for funds or troops.

The arrangement reflected practical conditions as well as ideology. States already possessed governments and tax systems, while national institutions were new and politically suspect.

## Wartime Government

The [[Continental Congress]] and Confederation government coordinated the [[American Revolution]], negotiated the [[French Alliance]], and secured the [[Treaty of Paris of 1783]]. Victory demonstrated that the system could accomplish significant goals under pressure.

Yet Congress struggled to pay soldiers and creditors. It printed currency, borrowed, and requested state contributions, producing inflation and weak public credit. Robert Morris and other nationalists urged stronger fiscal authority without obtaining unanimous consent.

Military success depended on state cooperation, foreign loans, and French support rather than a strong national administrative system.

## Western Land Policy

States with western claims ceded much of them to the national government, giving Congress a valuable resource. The [[Land Ordinance of 1785]] surveyed land into townships and sections for orderly sale. It also reserved land for public education.

The [[Northwest Ordinance of 1787]] created territorial government and a process for equal statehood. It prohibited slavery in the Northwest Territory while protecting property and civil liberties.

These laws established a durable model for continental expansion. They also treated Native homelands as federal land available for settlement, contributing to conflict and dispossession.

## Economic Weakness

Congress could not impose national taxes or regulate commerce among states and foreign countries. States adopted their own tariffs, currencies, and debtor policies, creating friction. British merchants restricted American trade, while Britain retained western forts partly because the United States failed to meet treaty obligations.

Debt divided creditors, taxpayers, farmers, and governments. Some states printed paper money or delayed collection; commercial elites feared instability and attacks on contracts. Nationalists believed only a stronger central government could establish credit and coordinate economic policy.

## Shays' Rebellion and Reform

In 1786–1787, indebted Massachusetts farmers closed courts to prevent foreclosures and tax collection during [[Shays' Rebellion]]. The state raised a privately funded force after Congress could not provide an effective response.

The rebellion alarmed many elites, who feared social disorder and weak protection for property. Others sympathized with farmers burdened by taxes and debt. The episode did not alone cause the Constitution, but it strengthened the movement for revision.

The Annapolis Convention had already called for a broader meeting. Delegates arriving at the [[Constitutional Convention]] chose to replace rather than amend the Articles.

## Republican Liberty and Governmental Capacity

Judging the Articles only as a failure imposes later expectations on a wartime confederation designed to restrain central power. It preserved union, won recognition, and established western policy.

Its weaknesses were nevertheless structural. National decisions depended on voluntary state compliance. The [[United States Constitution]] responded by granting taxation, commerce, executive, judicial, and enforcement powers while retaining [[Federalism|federalism]].
