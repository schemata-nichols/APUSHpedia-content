> The **United States Constitution** is the governing framework drafted in Philadelphia in 1787 to replace the [[Articles of Confederation]]. It created a stronger federal government with legislative, executive, and judicial branches while dividing authority through [[Federalism]], separation of powers, and checks and balances. Delegates compromised over representation, commerce, and slavery, then submitted the document to state conventions. The Constitution expanded national capacity without creating direct or equal democracy: senators, presidents, and judges were selected indirectly, and slavery received substantial protection. For APUSH, it is both a durable structure and a contested text whose meaning has changed through amendments, elections, war, courts, political practice, and social movements.

## APUSH Significance

The Constitution reorganized political power:

1. **National authority became enforceable.** Congress could tax, regulate commerce, and act directly on individuals.
2. **Power was divided.** Branches and levels of government could limit one another.
3. **Compromise made agreement possible.** Representation and slavery produced the most consequential bargains.
4. **Ratification required public argument.** Federalists and Anti-Federalists debated power, liberty, and representation.
5. **Amendment allowed change.** Later generations altered citizenship, voting, federal power, and institutional rules.

The Constitution differs from the [[Declaration of Independence]], which justified separation but did not design a government.

## Constitutional Convention

Economic conflict, fiscal weakness, diplomacy, and [[Shays' Rebellion]] convinced nationalists that the confederation needed reform. Delegates met at the [[Constitutional Convention]] under instructions to revise the Articles but instead drafted a new system.

James Madison's Virginia Plan favored representation by population and a strong national government. The New Jersey Plan preserved equal state voting. The [[Great Compromise]] created a House based on population and a Senate with equal state representation.

Delegates worked in secrecy and did not agree on one political theory. The final document combined popular authority, state power, national capacity, and restraints on immediate majorities.

## Branches and Powers

Congress received powers over taxation, borrowing, commerce, currency, war, and laws necessary and proper for executing enumerated responsibilities. Bicameralism required agreement between House and Senate.

The president executed laws, conducted diplomacy, commanded the military, and could veto legislation. The federal judiciary resolved cases under national law. The Constitution did not explicitly establish judicial review, which developed through practice and _[[Marbury v. Madison]]_.

Checks and balances distributed appointments, treaties, legislation, impeachment, and funding among institutions. The design sought effective government without concentrating all authority in one branch.

## Federalism and Supremacy

The Constitution granted specified powers to the national government while leaving states responsible for many local matters. The Supremacy Clause made valid federal law superior to conflicting state law, and the Necessary and Proper Clause supported implied powers.

The Tenth Amendment later reserved undelegated powers to states or the people. These provisions did not fix one permanent boundary; federalism developed through political conflict and court interpretation.

## [[Slavery]] and Exclusion

The [[Three-Fifths Compromise]] counted three-fifths of enslaved populations for representation and direct taxation, increasing slave-state political power without recognizing enslaved people's rights. Congress could not prohibit the international slave trade before 1808, and a fugitive-slave provision required return across state lines.

The document avoided the word slavery but protected the institution. It also left voting qualifications mainly to states, which generally excluded women and imposed property or racial restrictions.

The [[Reconstruction Amendments]] later abolished slavery, established national citizenship and equal protection, and prohibited racial discrimination in men's voting.

## Ratification and the Bill of Rights

The Constitution would take effect after nine state conventions approved it, bypassing unanimous amendment under the Articles. [[Federalists]] argued that national stability and prosperity required greater authority. [[Anti-Federalists]] feared consolidated power, distant officials, and the absence of explicit liberties.

The [[Federalist Papers]] defended the proposed system. Promises to add amendments helped secure ratification, and the [[Bill of Rights]] was adopted in 1791.

## Durable Framework and Contested Meaning

The Constitution was democratic relative to monarchy and inherited rule, but it also filtered participation and protected unequal power. Its durability comes partly from general language that permits adaptation, though that flexibility creates conflict over interpretation.

Constitutional change occurs through more than formal amendment. Courts, parties, executive practice, legislation, war, and movements have all reshaped how its powers and rights operate.
