> The **Anti-Federalists** were diverse opponents or critics of ratifying the [[United States Constitution]] in 1787–1788. They feared that the proposed national government was too distant from ordinary citizens, that broad taxing and military powers could threaten liberty, and that the document lacked an explicit bill of rights. Many preferred smaller republics and stronger state authority, though they did not all reject union or agree on an alternative. For APUSH, the Anti-Federalists lost the immediate [[Ratification Debate]] but shaped the constitutional order by forcing supporters to promise the amendments that became the [[Bill of Rights]].

## APUSH Significance

Writers using names such as Brutus and Federal Farmer warned that the Necessary and Proper Clause, federal courts, standing army, and executive could expand beyond their stated limits. Critics also argued that the House was too small to represent a large and varied population. Anti-Federalist support often drew strength from rural communities and people suspicious of creditor and commercial elites, but the coalition differed across states and cannot be reduced to one class.

## Defeat and Influence

The [[Federalists]] possessed stronger national coordination and argued that checks and balances would control power. In closely divided conventions, promises of amendments helped win ratification. James Madison then introduced protections for speech, religion, due process, and other liberties. Anti-Federalism should be distinguished from the later Democratic-Republican Party, although suspicion of centralized power influenced [[Thomas Jefferson]] and his allies. Its lasting importance lies in making civil liberties and reserved powers part of the new government's political legitimacy while preserving a recurring critique of concentrated national authority.
