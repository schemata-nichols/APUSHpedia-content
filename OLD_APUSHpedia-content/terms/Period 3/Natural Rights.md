> **Natural rights** are rights understood to belong to human beings by nature rather than as gifts from rulers or governments. Enlightenment thinkers, especially John Locke, argued that legitimate government rests on consent and exists to protect life, liberty, and property; a government that systematically violates those ends may forfeit obedience. American revolutionaries adapted these ideas in the [[Declaration of Independence]], which proclaimed equality and unalienable rights. For APUSH, natural rights supplied a powerful justification for revolution and later reform, while the exclusion of enslaved people, women, Native peoples, and others exposed the gap between universal language and unequal practice.

## APUSH Significance

Natural-rights theory connected individual liberty to the [[Social Contract]]: people establish government to secure rights, and political authority must remain accountable to them. Colonial writers combined this philosophy with English constitutional traditions and their experience of [[Colonial Self-Government]]. Thomas Jefferson's formulation of “life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness” transformed a long imperial dispute into a general claim about legitimate government, allowing independence to be presented as a defense of principle rather than simply a contest for power.

## Promise and Contradiction

Revolutionary governments did not immediately apply natural rights universally. Slavery survived, political participation remained limited, and Native sovereignty was threatened by expansion. Yet the broad language could be claimed by people excluded from its first application. Abolitionists, women's-rights activists, and the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] repeatedly used founding ideals to criticize unequal law and citizenship. Natural rights therefore differ from specific constitutional rights: they are moral claims understood to exist before government, while constitutions translate some of those claims into enforceable legal protections.
