> The **Albany Plan of Union** was Benjamin Franklin's 1754 proposal for a unified intercolonial government to coordinate defense, diplomacy with Native nations, and western affairs during a growing imperial crisis. Delegates at the Albany Congress approved a version of the plan, but colonial assemblies and British authorities rejected it because each feared surrendering power. The plan never took effect and was not an independence movement; its supporters sought stronger cooperation within the British Empire. For APUSH, it reveals both an early effort at continental coordination and the strength of colonial particularism before the [[French and Indian War]].

## APUSH Significance

British officials called the Albany Congress partly to repair relations with the [[Iroquois Confederacy]] as conflict with France approached. Franklin's plan proposed a president-general appointed by the crown and a grand council selected by colonial assemblies, with authority to raise revenue for common defense and manage frontier policy. Its design attempted to balance imperial supervision with colonial representation. Rejection showed that colonial governments guarded their taxing and governing powers, while British officials distrusted a body that might become too independent.

## Cooperation and Limits

The Albany Plan anticipated later problems faced by the [[Continental Congress]] and [[Articles of Confederation]]: how could separate colonies or states coordinate shared needs without losing local authority? It did not directly create those later institutions, but it provides evidence of continuity in debates over union and sovereignty. Franklin's “Join, or Die” image captured the argument for collective security, yet cooperation remained difficult until imperial policies after 1763 created broader common grievances. The plan's failure is therefore as instructive as its design, showing how weak intercolonial identity remained in 1754.

## The Albany Congress

Imperial officials called the Albany Congress partly to repair relations with the [[Iroquois Confederacy]] as war with France approached. Representatives from seven colonies attended, but many colonies sent no delegates. Native diplomacy was therefore central to the meeting's context even though later memory often treats the congress chiefly as a step toward American union.

Franklin's proposal placed a president-general appointed by the Crown alongside a grand council selected by colonial assemblies. This structure attempted to balance imperial oversight with colonial representation. The union could raise money, organize defense, regulate some western affairs, and negotiate with Native nations. It was not a proposal for independence or a fully democratic national government.

## Rejection and Significance

Colonial assemblies feared surrendering taxation and authority to a central body, while imperial officials could view the plan as granting too much coordinated power to colonists. No government adopted it. Britain instead directed wartime policy through existing imperial institutions, and colonies continued to guard their separate interests.

The rejection reveals the limits of common identity in 1754. Colonists faced a shared military threat and participated in connected trade and communication networks, but political loyalty remained strongly provincial. Wartime cooperation often required subsidies, negotiation, or direct British coordination. The [[French and Indian War]] would deepen shared experience without immediately eliminating rivalry among colonies.

The plan later became significant because the revolutionary crisis changed the incentives for union. The [[Continental Congress]] coordinated resistance when Parliament appeared to threaten every colony, and the [[Articles of Confederation]] created a permanent but weak union after independence. Those institutions did not simply implement Franklin's design, yet the Albany Plan supplied an earlier example of delegates debating how local governments might share limited powers.

Its APUSH value is therefore diagnostic rather than prophetic. It demonstrates awareness that defense and diplomacy could require intercolonial institutions, while its failure shows why unity required more than a clever constitutional plan: colonies needed a common political crisis strong enough to outweigh fear of centralized authority.
