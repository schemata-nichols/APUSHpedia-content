> The **Northwest Ordinance of 1787** established government for territory north of the Ohio River and created a process by which western territories could enter the Union as states equal to the original thirteen. It protected selected civil liberties, encouraged education, and prohibited slavery in the territory, although enforcement and the legal status of enslaved people remained contested. Passed under the [[Articles of Confederation]], it became a durable framework for national expansion. For APUSH, the ordinance joined republican institution-building to Native dispossession and introduced the question of slavery's western expansion long before the [[Sectional Crisis]].

## APUSH Significance

The ordinance provided stages of territorial government followed by statehood once population thresholds were met, rejecting permanent colonial subordination of western settlers. Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, and Wisconsin, as well as part of Minnesota, eventually emerged from the territory. Its declaration of rights included religious liberty, jury trial, and due process. The slavery prohibition helped shape a regional free-labor pattern, though indentures and evasions allowed forms of unfree labor to persist.

## Expansion and Contradiction

The law promised that Native property would not be taken without consent, yet the United States claimed authority over lands Native nations had not ceded. Settlement therefore produced warfare, treaty pressure, and land loss. Together with the [[Land Ordinance of 1785]], the measure demonstrated that the Confederation Congress could manage territory even while lacking strong fiscal and enforcement powers. Its central contradiction is essential to APUSH: the nation extended representative institutions and eventual equal statehood for settlers through a process that denied equal sovereignty to Indigenous peoples and made slavery's geographic future a federal concern.
