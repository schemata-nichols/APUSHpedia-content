> The **Federalists** were supporters of ratifying the [[United States Constitution]] during the 1787–1788 debate. They argued that the [[Articles of Confederation]] left the nation unable to tax effectively, regulate interstate commerce, enforce treaties, or maintain stability. A larger republic with divided powers, they contended, could control factions while preventing any one institution from dominating. For APUSH, ratification-era Federalists should be distinguished from the later [[Federalist Party]]: the coalitions overlapped, but supporting the Constitution did not automatically mean endorsing every policy associated with [[Alexander Hamilton]] in the [[First Party System]].

## APUSH Significance

Federalists included figures such as Hamilton, James Madison, and [[George Washington]], as well as merchants, creditors, urban artisans, and other supporters who varied by state. They coordinated newspaper essays, conventions, and personal networks more effectively than many opponents. The [[Federalist Papers]] defended the proposed government's structure, explaining how representation, checks and balances, and an extended republic could restrain both majority and minority factions.

## Ratification and Concession

Critics feared centralized authority and the absence of an explicit declaration of rights. Federalists answered that the national government possessed only delegated powers, but political necessity led them to promise amendments. That concession helped secure ratification and produced the [[Bill of Rights]]. Federalist success created a stronger government without ending disputes over constitutional meaning. The movement's central claim was that liberty required capable institutions as well as limits on power; the [[Anti-Federalists]] warned that the same capacity could become distant, consolidated, and unaccountable.
