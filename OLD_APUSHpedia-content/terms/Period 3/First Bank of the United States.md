> The **First Bank of the United States** was a federally chartered but privately owned bank operating from 1791 to 1811. Proposed by Treasury Secretary [[Alexander Hamilton]], it held government deposits, transferred federal funds, issued reliable notes, and extended credit. Supporters believed it would stabilize national finance and commerce; opponents feared concentrated privilege and argued that the [[United States Constitution]] did not authorize Congress to create it. For APUSH, the Bank made an economic policy dispute into an enduring conflict over implied powers, strict versus broad [[Constitutional Interpretation]], and who should benefit from national development.

## APUSH Significance

Hamilton defended the Bank as a convenient means of executing enumerated powers to tax, borrow, regulate commerce, and manage revenue. [[Thomas Jefferson]] argued that “necessary” did not mean merely useful and that undelegated authority remained outside federal power. [[George Washington]] accepted Hamilton's reasoning and signed the charter. The disagreement helped organize Federalist and Democratic-Republican coalitions in the [[First Party System]].

## Finance and Legacy

The Bank's national reach helped discipline state banks and provide credit, but its stock ownership and connections to merchants reinforced criticism that the government favored wealthy interests. Congress allowed its twenty-year charter to expire in 1811, shortly before wartime financing difficulties revived demands for a national institution. The later [[Second Bank of the United States]] inherited many functions and controversies. The First Bank was not the modern Federal Reserve or a wholly public central bank; it was a mixed public-private institution whose constitutional defense laid groundwork for *[[McCulloch v. Maryland]]* and later expansions of implied national power.
