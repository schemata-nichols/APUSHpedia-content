> The **Three-Fifths Compromise** was the constitutional agreement that counted three-fifths of the enslaved population when allocating seats in the House of Representatives and direct federal taxes among states. It did not declare that an enslaved person was “three-fifths human”; it determined representation and taxation while enslaved people had no political rights. The formula increased the power of slaveholding states in Congress and presidential elections. For APUSH, the compromise shows that the [[United States Constitution]] did not merely postpone conflict over [[Slavery]]—it embedded protections and political advantages for slavery in the new national system.

## APUSH Significance

At the [[Constitutional Convention]], delegates from slaveholding states wanted enslaved people counted for representation but resisted counting them for taxation; opponents highlighted the contradiction of treating people as population for power and property for law. The compromise applied an existing Confederation-era ratio to both purposes. Together with clauses concerning fugitive labor and the continuation of the international slave trade until at least 1808, it helped gain southern support for the Constitution.

## Power Without Representation

The added representation strengthened slaveholders' influence in the House, Electoral College, and selection of national officials, contributing to what critics later called “Slave Power.” Enslaved people generated political power for those who denied them freedom rather than receiving representation themselves. The Civil War amendments made the clause obsolete: the Thirteenth abolished slavery and the Fourteenth based representation on whole persons while imposing a potential penalty for denying male citizens the vote. The compromise is essential evidence of how constitutional structures can distribute power unequally even when their language appears numerical or procedural.
