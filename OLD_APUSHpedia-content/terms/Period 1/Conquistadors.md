> **Conquistadors** were Spanish military adventurers who led expeditions of conquest in the Americas during the sixteenth century under authority granted by the crown. Figures such as [[Hernán Cortés]] and Francisco Pizarro sought wealth, status, converts, and royal favor, but their victories depended heavily on Indigenous allies, local political divisions, and epidemic disease rather than on small Spanish forces alone. Conquest destroyed states, redirected tribute and labor, and laid foundations for the [[Spanish Empire]]. For APUSH, conquistadors reveal how private ambition and imperial authority combined while Native choices and biological exchange shaped outcomes Europeans could not control by themselves.

## APUSH Significance

Cortés entered Mexico in 1519 and built alliances with peoples hostile to Aztec rule before capturing Tenochtitlán. Pizarro exploited conflict within the Inca Empire in the Andes. Steel weapons, horses, and firearms mattered, but unfamiliar diseases caused mass death and political disruption, while translators, guides, porters, and thousands of Indigenous fighters made Spanish campaigns possible. Describing conquest as the work of a few exceptional Europeans erases the coalitions and contingencies that determined victory.

## Conquest and Colonial Rule

Conquistadors claimed land, treasure, and Indigenous labor through institutions such as the [[Encomienda System]], often committing extreme violence. The crown later attempted to restrain independent conquerors and replace expeditionary rule with royal courts, officials, and laws. Critics such as [[Bartolomé de las Casas]] challenged abuses, while defenders appealed to conversion and imperial right. Conquistadors should be distinguished from later settlers, missionaries, and administrators, although their roles overlapped. Their importance lies in initiating colonial power that the monarchy then tried to regularize and in provoking Native resistance that continued long after dramatic military victories.

## Alliances, Disease, and Military Power

Conquistadors succeeded through coalitions as well as weapons. Cortés entered a region in which many peoples resented or resisted Mexica demands and assembled thousands of Indigenous allies, especially Tlaxcalans. Pizarro exploited conflict within the Inca Empire following a civil war. Native translators and intermediaries, including Malintzin, made diplomacy possible. Describing conquest as a victory of a few Europeans over entire empires erases the Indigenous political choices that shaped events.

Steel weapons, horses, firearms, and siege tactics offered advantages, but none guaranteed victory. Epidemic disease caused catastrophic mortality among populations without prior exposure to pathogens such as smallpox and disrupted leadership and defense. Disease was not a conscious ally under complete Spanish control; it interacted with warfare, famine, displacement, and existing conflict. Conquest resulted from this combination rather than one technological superiority.

## Profit, Religion, and Royal Authority

Expeditions were risky private ventures operating under royal authorization. Participants expected land, office, captives, tribute, and precious metals, while the Crown demanded sovereignty and a share of wealth. Conquistadors also described their campaigns as spreading Christianity. Material ambition and religious conviction reinforced one another without making conquest orderly or centrally planned.

After victory, conquerors sought rewards through [[Encomienda System|encomiendas]] and local authority. The monarchy responded by building courts and viceroyalties that limited their independence. Missionaries disputed abuses, and Indigenous litigants used Spanish law to challenge some demands. Military conquest therefore opened colonial rule but did not determine every later institution.

Conquistadors encountered continuing resistance. The fall of a capital did not give Spain immediate control of distant regions, and revolts challenged colonial settlements for centuries. In the northern borderlands that later became part of the United States, Spanish soldiers and colonists possessed fewer resources and depended heavily on missions, alliances, and negotiation. The term is most useful when it identifies armed agents of expansion while distinguishing their initial campaigns from the larger [[Spanish Empire]] built afterward.
