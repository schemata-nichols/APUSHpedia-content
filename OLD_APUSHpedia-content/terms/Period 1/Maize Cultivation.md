> **Maize cultivation** was the growing of domesticated corn, a crop first developed in Mesoamerica and spread through exchange and adaptation across much of North America before European contact. Because maize could produce a dependable surplus in varied environments, it supported population growth, permanent settlements, specialized labor, trade, and complex political organization. Native peoples combined it with other crops and adapted farming to local climates rather than following one uniform system. For APUSH, maize is evidence that [[Native American Societies Before European Contact]] actively transformed their environments and built diverse economies long before colonization.

## APUSH Significance

Maize agriculture spread northward over centuries and became especially important in the Southwest, Southeast, and Eastern Woodlands. Irrigation helped [[Pueblo Societies]] farm in arid environments, while maize surpluses supported large population centers associated with [[Mississippian Culture]]. In many eastern communities, corn grew alongside beans and squash in complementary agricultural systems. Farming encouraged settled villages and denser populations, but cultivation coexisted with hunting, fishing, gathering, and seasonal movement; it did not make all Native societies alike.

## Exchange and Historical Change

Maize moved through Indigenous trade and knowledge networks, demonstrating innovation before European arrival. After 1492, it became part of the [[Columbian Exchange]] and spread to Europe, Africa, and Asia, where its high yields supported population growth. Europeans adopted the crop while colonial warfare, land seizure, and disease disrupted many of the communities that had developed it. Maize cultivation therefore connects geography and environment to social organization and global exchange. Its importance lies not simply in the crop itself but in the accumulated knowledge required to domesticate, adapt, plant, process, and distribute it.

## Domestication and Adaptation

Maize was not a naturally occurring staple waiting to be collected. Indigenous farmers in Mesoamerica selectively bred teosinte over generations, producing plants with larger, more accessible kernels. As seeds and knowledge moved northward, communities adapted varieties to different elevations, growing seasons, rainfall patterns, and soils. This process demonstrates scientific observation and cumulative innovation without relying on European technology.

Cultivation methods varied by environment. Southwestern communities captured scarce water through irrigation, runoff farming, and careful placement of fields. Eastern farmers often planted maize with beans and squash: maize offered a stalk for climbing beans, beans contributed nitrogen to soil, and broad squash leaves helped suppress weeds and retain moisture. These techniques were not universal or unchanged, but they show deliberate management of ecological relationships.

## Surplus, Settlement, and Power

Stored agricultural surplus could support larger permanent settlements and people engaged in political, ceremonial, defensive, or craft work rather than full-time food production. At Cahokia and other Mississippian centers, maize helped sustain dense populations and mound construction. In the Southwest, farming supported multiroom settlements and wide trade networks. Agricultural success did not automatically create one political system: societies distributed food, labor, and authority differently.

Dependence on a staple also created vulnerability. Drought, soil depletion, conflict, or shortened growing seasons could encourage migration and political reorganization. Communities responded by diversifying food sources, trading, moving fields, or relocating. Environmental limits influenced history without mechanically determining decline.

After European contact, colonizers depended on Native crops and knowledge while warfare, disease, mission systems, and land seizure disrupted agricultural communities. Maize traveled through Atlantic networks and became a major crop in Africa and Europe. Its global spread was part of the [[Columbian Exchange]], but its origin should remain clear: the crop embodied thousands of years of Indigenous domestication and regional adaptation before Europeans encountered it.
