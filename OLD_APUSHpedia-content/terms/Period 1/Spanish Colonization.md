> **Spanish Colonization** was the conquest, settlement, and administration of American territory by the Spanish monarchy beginning after 1492. Spanish expeditions sought wealth, Christian converts, strategic control, and social advancement. Conquistadors allied with Indigenous enemies of large empires, while disease weakened Native populations. Colonial officials then organized cities, missions, mining, agriculture, and coerced labor within a hierarchical imperial system. Native peoples resisted, negotiated, and adapted, and African slavery became increasingly important. For APUSH, Spanish colonization established enduring patterns in the present-day United States Southwest and Florida: cultural exchange and mixed communities developed alongside violence, Native dispossession, forced conversion, and racial classification.

## APUSH Significance

Spanish colonization provides an important comparison with later British settlement:

1. **Conquest combined several forces.** Disease, steel weapons, horses, Native alliances, and political divisions aided relatively small Spanish forces.
2. **The crown sought controlled empire.** Officials regulated land, labor, religion, trade, and urban government more directly than Britain initially did.
3. **Labor extraction drove settlement.** Encomienda, mining, missions, and plantations coerced Native and African workers.
4. **Religion was an imperial institution.** Missions pursued conversion and territorial control while becoming sites of cultural exchange and resistance.
5. **A racial hierarchy developed.** The [[Casta System]] classified ancestry and status while remaining more complex in practice than its formal labels suggest.

Spanish colonization was not uniform. Mexico, the Caribbean, Florida, New Mexico, Texas, and California developed under different environments, Native relationships, and strategic purposes.

## Conquest and Native Alliances

[[Christopher Columbus]]'s voyages initiated sustained contact and Spanish claims, but conquest depended on more than European technology. [[Hernán Cortés]] defeated the [[Aztec Empire]] with the aid of thousands of Indigenous allies who opposed Aztec rule. Smallpox and political disruption further weakened resistance.

The Spanish used the title of [[Conquistadors|conquistador]] for participants in conquest expeditions. They sought tribute, land, office, and wealth, often acting with considerable initiative before the crown imposed firmer administration.

Native peoples made strategic choices among dangerous alternatives. Alliance with Spain could serve local rivalries, while flight, revolt, legal petitions, and cultural adaptation offered other forms of resistance. Conquest replaced some rulers but did not eliminate Native communities or political action.

## Labor, Wealth, and Government

The [[Encomienda System]] granted colonists claims to Indigenous tribute and labor while formally requiring protection and Christian instruction. In practice, coercion, overwork, violence, and disease caused immense suffering. The crown later attempted reforms but retained other systems of compulsory Native labor.

Silver mining in Mexico and South America connected the colonies to a global economy. American silver financed imperial government and entered European and Asian trade. Agriculture and ranching supplied towns and mines, while sugar plantations increased reliance on the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]].

Spanish cities followed planned grids and became centers of government, commerce, and the Catholic Church. Royal councils, viceroys, governors, and local officials attempted to regulate colonial life. Distance and local interests nevertheless limited central control.

## Missions and Borderlands

In northern borderlands where mineral wealth and dense settlement were less available, Spain used forts, towns, and the [[Spanish Mission System]] to claim territory. Missionaries sought to convert Native peoples and reorganize work, residence, family life, and religious practice.

Missions could provide food, tools, or defense, but they also imposed labor and discipline and exposed communities to disease. Native converts did not necessarily abandon older beliefs; people combined practices or maintained traditions beyond missionaries' view.

Spanish settlements in Florida, New Mexico, Texas, and California attempted to block rival empires and secure routes as much as to generate immediate profit. Their limited populations made alliances and Native labor particularly important.

## Resistance and the Pueblo Revolt

Native resistance ranged from everyday refusal and flight to organized rebellion. In 1680, Pueblo communities coordinated the [[Pueblo Revolt]], driving Spanish settlers from New Mexico for more than a decade. The revolt attacked forced labor, religious suppression, and colonial violence.

When Spain returned, officials exercised somewhat greater tolerance toward Pueblo religious practices and modified some labor demands. The episode demonstrates that Native resistance could change imperial policy even when it did not permanently end colonization.

## Race, Culture, and Social Hierarchy

Spanish America developed populations of Native, European, and African ancestry. Colonial categories such as peninsular, creole, mestizo, mulatto, Native, and African attempted to connect ancestry with legal privilege and social status.

The casta system was hierarchical but not perfectly fixed. Wealth, occupation, reputation, marriage, and local practice could affect status. Still, Europeans generally held the greatest political and economic power, and racial classification helped organize unequal labor and taxation.

Cultural mixing produced new languages, foods, religious practices, architecture, and communities. This process, sometimes described as mestizaje, occurred under unequal power and should not be treated as evidence that conquest was harmonious.

## Debate and Interpretation

Some Spaniards criticized conquest. [[Bartolomé de las Casas]] documented abuses and defended Native humanity, while [[Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda]] justified conquest through claims of cultural superiority. The [[Valladolid Debate]] revealed disagreement within empire without ending coercive rule.

Spanish colonization differed from British colonization in its earlier conquest of dense Native populations, stronger royal and church administration, and more formal racial categories. Both empires pursued land, labor, and profit and depended on Native dispossession. Comparison should explain degrees and methods rather than portray one empire as tolerant and the other as uniquely violent.
