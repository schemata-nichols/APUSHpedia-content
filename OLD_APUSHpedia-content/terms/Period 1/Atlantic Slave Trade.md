> The **Atlantic slave trade** was the forced transportation of millions of Africans across the Atlantic from the sixteenth through nineteenth centuries, primarily to labor in plantation and mining economies in the Americas. European merchants, African political and commercial actors, and American colonists participated in a system driven by demand for labor and commodities, but enslaved people experienced capture, family separation, the [[Middle Passage]], sale, and hereditary bondage. For APUSH, the trade explains the growth of [[Chattel Slavery]], the [[Atlantic Economy]], and racial ideology while connecting colonial North America to a much larger African and American history.

## APUSH Significance

European colonizers initially used multiple labor systems, including Indigenous labor and [[Indentured Servitude]], but disease, resistance, plantation expansion, and profit encouraged growing reliance on enslaved Africans. The largest numbers were transported to Brazil and the Caribbean, while a smaller share arrived directly in mainland British North America. That distinction does not lessen the trade's importance to the future United States: North American merchants, ports, consumers, and plantations participated in Atlantic networks, and a self-reproducing enslaved population made slavery expand even after legal importation ended in 1808.

## Commerce, Culture, and Resistance

The trade transformed African societies through warfare, political disruption, and demographic loss while generating wealth for merchants and imperial states. Survivors carried languages, agricultural knowledge, religions, music, and family practices that shaped cultures throughout the Americas. Enslaved people resisted aboard ships, escaped, preserved community, and rebelled, demonstrating agency under extreme constraint. The Atlantic slave trade should be distinguished from slavery itself: the trade transported and sold people across the ocean, while slavery was the broader labor and legal system that continued within the United States after the transatlantic trade was prohibited.

## Capture, Commerce, and the Middle Passage

European traders usually acquired captives through African commercial and political networks rather than conducting most inland raids themselves. Warfare, kidnapping, judicial enslavement, and debt could place people into the trade. African rulers and merchants made strategic choices, but European demand, firearms, shipping, and credit enlarged the scale and profitability of enslavement. Participation by some African states does not make the system equal or reduce European responsibility for the plantation markets driving it.

The [[Middle Passage]] converted captive people into cargo within a tightly organized commercial system. Traders packed ships to maximize returns, restricted movement, and used violence to suppress resistance. Disease, dehydration, abuse, and inadequate food caused high mortality. Captives resisted through revolt, refusal to eat, sabotage, communication, and efforts to preserve human relationships despite deliberate separation.

## Plantation Expansion and Atlantic Connections

Sugar cultivation in Brazil and the Caribbean generated especially intense demand for enslaved labor before the mainland English colonies became major destinations. Colonial North America received a smaller share of the total transatlantic trade, but its plantation economy depended on the wider system. By the eighteenth century, merchants, shipbuilders, insurers, and consumers in northern colonies were also connected to commodities and profits produced by slavery.

The trade changed Africa as well as the Americas. It intensified conflict in some regions, shifted political power, removed millions of people, and altered population patterns. Its effects differed across a large and diverse continent, so a single narrative of uniform devastation is inadequate. In the Americas, survivors combined knowledge from many African societies to build new languages, religions, foods, music, and family practices under coercive conditions.

The United States prohibited legal participation in the transatlantic trade beginning in 1808, but illegal importations continued and the domestic slave trade expanded dramatically. Forced movement from the Upper South to the [[Cotton Kingdom]] reproduced the separation and sale of families within national borders. The Atlantic Slave Trade is therefore a foundation of [[Chattel Slavery]] and Atlantic commerce, while remaining distinct from the later internal trade that sustained U.S. slavery.
