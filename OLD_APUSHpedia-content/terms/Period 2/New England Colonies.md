> The **New England colonies**—principally Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and New Hampshire—developed around family migration, town settlement, mixed farming, maritime commerce, and strong Puritan influence. A cooler climate and less fertile soil limited plantation staples, encouraging diversified economies that included fishing, shipbuilding, trade, and small farms. Congregations and town meetings organized much local life, yet participation remained restricted and religious dissent could bring punishment. For APUSH, New England shows how environment and migration shaped a regional society while exposing the tension between communal self-government and exclusion of dissenters, Native peoples, women, and the unfree.

## APUSH Significance

The [[Puritan Great Migration]] brought families to [[Massachusetts Bay Colony]], producing more balanced sex ratios and longer life expectancy than in the [[Chesapeake Colonies]]. Puritans sought to build a disciplined Christian community rather than broad religious freedom. Towns supported churches and schools, while covenanted male church members held particular authority. Dissenters including [[Roger Williams]] and [[Anne Hutchinson]] challenged aspects of the system; Rhode Island developed greater religious toleration, demonstrating that New England was not uniform.

## Economy and Conflict

New England merchants participated in the [[Atlantic Economy]], including commerce connected to slavery, even though plantation agriculture was less dominant locally. Colonial settlement pressed onto Native land and contributed to the [[Pequot War]] and [[King Philip's War]], conflicts that greatly expanded English control. Over time, commercial growth and religious diversification weakened the founding generation's institutional unity, while the [[First Great Awakening]] later challenged established ministers. The region's importance lies not in a myth of isolated, democratic villages but in the interaction of religious ideals, local institutions, Atlantic trade, and violent territorial expansion.

## Towns, Families, and Covenants

New England's settlement pattern reflected both religious aims and demography. Puritan migrants often arrived in family groups, creating a more balanced sex ratio and faster natural population growth than in the Chesapeake. Towns distributed land, supervised common resources, and gathered residents around meetinghouses. Congregations formed covenants and selected ministers, although colonial governments supported churches and did not embrace modern religious liberty.

The family was an economic and religious institution. Households farmed, produced goods, trained children, and enforced patriarchal authority. Women's labor was essential, but married women lacked independent political standing and faced legal limits under coverture. High literacy grew partly from the Puritan expectation that believers should read scripture, contributing to schools, printing, and Harvard College.

Religious unity was never complete. [[Roger Williams]] challenged the union of church and government and the legitimacy of taking Native land without fair agreement. [[Anne Hutchinson]] criticized ministers and held meetings that alarmed male authorities. Their banishment demonstrated the limits of dissent, while Rhode Island developed broader toleration outside Massachusetts.

## Expansion, Commerce, and Conflict

Rocky soil and a shorter growing season favored mixed farming rather than plantation staples, but New England was not economically isolated. Fishing, timber, shipbuilding, coastal commerce, and Atlantic trade supported port towns. Merchants participated in trade networks connected to Caribbean plantations and slavery, even though enslaved labor was less central to local agriculture than in the South.

Population growth increased pressure on Native land. The [[Pequot War]] and [[King Philip's War]] arose from struggles over territory, trade, sovereignty, and colonial expansion. English victory brought killing, captivity, enslavement, and land loss, while Native resistance nearly destroyed several settlements during King Philip's War. These conflicts reveal the coercion beneath the image of compact religious communities.

By the late seventeenth century, the [[Half-Way Covenant]], commercial growth, and imperial reorganization reflected change within Puritan society. The Crown's [[Dominion of New England]] challenged local institutions, and colonists overthrew it after the Glorious Revolution. New England's APUSH importance lies in the interaction of covenanted community, representative government, family settlement, commerce, dissent, and territorial expansion—not in a claim that the colonies were uniformly democratic or tolerant.
