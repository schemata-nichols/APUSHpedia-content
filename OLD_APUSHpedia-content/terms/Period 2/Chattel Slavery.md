> **Chattel Slavery** was a labor and property system in which enslaved people were legally owned, their bondage generally lasted for life, and status passed hereditarily to their children. British North American colonies constructed this system during the seventeenth century by connecting African ancestry to permanent enslavement through statute and custom. Chattel slavery supplied labor for tobacco, rice, indigo, and later cotton while giving owners power over movement, family, work, and bodily autonomy. Enslaved people resisted and sustained communities despite that control. For APUSH, racial slavery was not an ancient condition simply imported intact; colonial law built it over time, and its economic value made it central to American expansion and political conflict.

## APUSH Significance

Chattel slavery explains the relationship among labor, race, law, and wealth:

1. **Bondage became hereditary.** Colonial statutes made children's status follow that of enslaved mothers.
2. **Race justified unequal status.** Law increasingly associated African ancestry with enslavement and European ancestry with freedom.
3. **Property rights extended over people.** Enslaved workers could be sold, mortgaged, inherited, and separated from family.
4. **The system supported Atlantic production.** Plantation commodities connected owners to merchants, creditors, and consumers across the [[Atlantic Economy]].
5. **Resistance was continuous.** Enslaved people preserved family and culture, negotiated, escaped, slowed work, and sometimes revolted.

Chattel slavery differed from [[Indentured Servitude]], which was based on a time-limited contract and normally ended with legal freedom. Both were coercive, but only one became a hereditary racial property system.

## From Fluid Status to Racial Law

Africans arrived in Virginia by 1619, but early labor status was not yet governed by one complete slave code. Colonies used Native slavery, African slavery, indenture, and other dependencies. Some Africans obtained freedom and property during the early seventeenth century.

Over time, lawmakers restricted African mobility, testimony, marriage, weapons, and access to freedom. Virginia made a child's condition follow the mother, breaking with English paternal inheritance and allowing owners to claim children born to enslaved women. Conversion to Christianity did not guarantee emancipation.

These laws served planters seeking controllable labor and helped unite Europeans across class through legal whiteness. [[Bacon's Rebellion]] did not single-handedly create slavery, but elite fear of unrest encouraged greater reliance on enslaved labor and racial division.

## Plantation Economies

Tobacco shaped slavery in the Chesapeake. Workforces were often dispersed among plantations, and the domestic slave trade later moved people south and west. In South Carolina and Georgia, rice and indigo supported larger enslaved populations and close Caribbean connections.

Africans brought expertise essential to production, especially knowledge of rice cultivation. Owners profited from that skill while denying workers control of its rewards. Northern farms, households, docks, and workshops also used enslaved labor, and northern merchants participated in plantation commerce.

Slavery was therefore regional in concentration but national and Atlantic in economic reach. The lower number of enslaved people in northern colonies did not make them disconnected from the system.

## Control, Family, and Culture

Slave codes authorized violence and restricted assembly, movement, literacy, and legal standing. Sale made family separation a constant danger, and sexual violence reflected owners' legal and physical power.

Enslaved people nevertheless formed marriages, kin networks, religious practices, music, stories, and communities. African traditions combined with new conditions and interactions among peoples from different regions. Culture was not evidence that slavery was tolerable; it was a means of survival and self-definition within coercion.

Work conditions varied by place, skill, crop, age, and gender. Some artisans or urban workers gained limited mobility, but such flexibility remained subject to owners and law.

## Resistance and Rebellion

Everyday resistance included working slowly, breaking tools, feigning illness, preserving forbidden practices, and escaping. Runaways used Native communities, cities, maritime work, and informal networks to seek autonomy.

Organized revolts were less common because surveillance and punishment were severe. The [[Stono Rebellion]] of 1739 became the largest uprising in Britain's mainland colonies and led South Carolina to tighten restrictions. Fear of revolt shaped white politics even when planned uprisings failed.

## Growth and Legacy

The end of legal British participation in the Atlantic trade did not end slavery. Natural increase and a domestic trade supported expansion into the [[Cotton Kingdom]]. Enslaved people became a major form of wealth and collateral, linking slavery to [[Capitalism]] and territorial growth.

The [[Civil War]] and Thirteenth Amendment ultimately ended legal chattel slavery, but emancipation did not erase racial hierarchy or coerced labor. The amendment's punishment clause, [[Black Codes]], convict leasing, and sharecropping created later conflicts over the meaning of freedom.

## Property, Race, and Human Resistance

Chattel slavery was both an economic system and a political order. It required governments to define property, patrol movement, punish resistance, and protect owners' claims. Racist ideas reinforced those institutions while also developing from them.

Centering enslaved people as actors does not minimize coercion. Their labor, knowledge, family building, and resistance shaped colonial America and the long struggle that eventually destroyed slavery.
