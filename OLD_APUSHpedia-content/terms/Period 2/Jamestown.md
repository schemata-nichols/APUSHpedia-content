> **Jamestown**, founded in Virginia in 1607 by the [[Virginia Company]], was the first permanent English settlement in what became the United States. Its settlers struggled with disease, hunger, poor planning, internal conflict, and a tense relationship with the Powhatan chiefdom. Tobacco eventually made the colony profitable, encouraging land expansion and new labor systems. The [[House of Burgesses]] introduced representative government, while the arrival of Africans in 1619 preceded the gradual construction of racial slavery. For APUSH, Jamestown connects fragile early settlement to the enduring Chesapeake patterns of cash-crop agriculture, colonial self-rule, Native dispossession, and bound labor.

## APUSH Significance

Investors expected quick returns, but many early colonists lacked agricultural preparation and settled in an unhealthy location. Trade with Powhatan communities initially supplied food, while alternating diplomacy and warfare reflected English dependence and expansion. The winter of 1609–1610, known as the Starving Time, nearly destroyed the settlement. John Rolfe's cultivation of marketable tobacco then linked Virginia to the [[Atlantic Economy]], and the [[Headright System]] encouraged migration and the acquisition of Native land.

## Labor and Government

Tobacco required substantial labor, first supplied mainly through [[Indentured Servitude]] and increasingly through [[Chattel Slavery]] later in the seventeenth century. In 1619, the House of Burgesses met as an elected assembly, giving property-holding colonists a role in local taxation and law while remaining under company and then royal authority. Jamestown was not a model of immediate success; its analytical importance lies in how commercial survival produced institutions and inequalities characteristic of the [[Chesapeake Colonies]]. It became a precedent for representative government and for expansion built on coerced labor and Native land loss.

## Survival and the Tobacco Economy

Jamestown's location offered defense and access to deep water but also exposed settlers to brackish water, disease, and poor agricultural conditions. Many early colonists expected quick wealth and lacked the supplies, skills, or willingness needed for stable food production. The Virginia Company's demands and recurring conflict compounded these problems. During the “Starving Time” of 1609–1610, hunger and disease nearly destroyed the settlement.

Relations with the Powhatan chiefdom shifted among trade, diplomacy, coercion, and warfare. English colonists initially depended on Native food and knowledge, while Powhatan leaders tried to manage the newcomers within regional politics. English territorial expansion and demands for supplies made coexistence increasingly difficult. Pocahontas's life became part of diplomacy and English promotion, but later romantic stories obscure the coercive colonial setting.

John Rolfe's successful cultivation of marketable tobacco gave Virginia an export foundation. Tobacco demanded land and intensive labor, pushing settlement outward onto Native territory and creating chronic need for workers. The crop made survival economically viable while tying Virginia to the [[Atlantic Economy]] and producing decades of conflict over land.

## Government, Labor, and Transition

The House of Burgesses first met in 1619, giving property-holding colonists limited representation within company rule. That same year, recorded Africans arrived in Virginia, though colonial law had not yet fully defined the hereditary racial slavery that developed later. English indentured servants remained the main labor source for much of the seventeenth century.

High mortality and unequal access to land produced a volatile society. After the Crown revoked the Virginia Company's charter in 1624, Virginia became a royal colony, but representative government survived. [[Bacon's Rebellion]] later exposed conflict between frontier settlers and the colonial elite over land, Native policy, and political access. In its aftermath, planters increasingly relied on enslaved African labor and hardened racial laws.

Jamestown matters not because it was the first European settlement in North America—it was not—but because it became the first enduring English settlement that generated a lasting colonial polity. Its history connects a fragile corporate outpost to tobacco, representative institutions, Native dispossession, and the transition from mixed labor systems toward [[Chattel Slavery]].
