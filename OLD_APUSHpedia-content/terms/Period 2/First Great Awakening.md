> The **First Great Awakening** was a wave of Protestant revivalism that spread through the British mainland colonies mainly during the 1730s and 1740s. Preachers such as [[Jonathan Edwards]] and [[George Whitefield]] emphasized personal conversion, emotional religious experience, and the spiritual importance of individual choice. Revivals crossed colonial boundaries and attracted women, young people, and some Black and Native listeners, while dividing congregations between supporters and critics. For APUSH, the Awakening challenged established religious authority and created shared experiences across regions, but its relationship to the [[American Revolution]] was indirect rather than a simple cause-and-effect path.

## APUSH Significance

Itinerant ministers and inexpensive print allowed revival messages to circulate through the [[Atlantic Economy]]. “New Light” supporters welcomed emotional preaching and questioned ministers who lacked conversion experiences, while “Old Lights” defended established worship and clerical education. The resulting disputes split churches and encouraged new denominations and colleges. Revivals offered some people new religious roles, but they did not erase racial, gender, or denominational hierarchy, and participation varied greatly by region.

## Authority and Comparison

By inviting ordinary believers to judge spiritual authority, the Awakening contributed to a broader colonial culture willing to question inherited institutions. Its intercolonial networks also created limited common ground among otherwise distinct colonies. Historians nevertheless avoid claiming that revivalism directly produced revolution decades later. The First Great Awakening should also be distinguished from the [[Second Great Awakening]], which unfolded in the early republic, expanded mass evangelical denominations, and became closely connected to [[Antebellum Reform]]. Both emphasized conversion, but they operated in different political and social settings.

## Revival and Communication

The Awakening grew from earlier local revivals and broader Atlantic Protestant currents. Jonathan Edwards described intense conversions in Massachusetts, emphasizing human dependence on divine grace. George Whitefield's travels drew enormous crowds through emotional preaching and skillful use of print publicity. Newspapers, pamphlets, letters, and itinerant routes allowed people in distant colonies to recognize their experiences as part of a shared movement.

Revivalists urged listeners to examine their own conversion rather than depend entirely on education, office, or inherited church standing. This message could empower laypeople and new preachers, although most evangelists did not advocate political or social equality. Women participated actively in religious gatherings, and some enslaved and free Black people joined evangelical communities, yet church authority generally remained male and racial hierarchy endured.

## Division and Institutional Change

The movement divided congregations between “New Lights,” who welcomed revival, and “Old Lights,” who criticized emotional excess and itinerant challenges to settled ministers. These disputes created new congregations and denominations. Colleges including Princeton, Brown, Rutgers, and Dartmouth were connected to the need to educate ministers within a more competitive religious environment.

Evangelical growth could cross colonial borders and create common experiences, but the Awakening did not unite all colonists or directly cause the [[American Revolution]]. It weakened automatic deference to some established authorities and expanded networks through which arguments could circulate. Those habits later existed alongside political protest, making the relationship indirect rather than mechanically causal.

## Comparison with the Second Awakening

The [[Second Great Awakening]] began in the early republic and placed greater emphasis in many regions on free will, mass voluntary organization, and the possibility of improving society. It fed temperance, abolition, missions, and other forms of [[Antebellum Reform]] on a scale distinct from the earlier movement. The First Great Awakening occurred within British colonial society and focused more directly on conversion and church authority.

Both awakenings used itinerant preaching, emotion, print, and voluntary choice to challenge established religious patterns. The strongest comparison identifies their different historical settings and institutional consequences instead of treating all American revivals as one continuous event.
