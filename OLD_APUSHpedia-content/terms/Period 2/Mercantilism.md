> **Mercantilism** was an economic framework in which European states sought national power by regulating trade, accumulating wealth, and using colonies to supply raw materials and purchase manufactured goods. Under British mercantilism, Parliament restricted colonial commerce through the [[Navigation Acts]], while imperial protection and markets also benefited many colonial merchants and producers. The system was never enforced perfectly, and smuggling or legal loopholes allowed colonists room to maneuver. For APUSH, mercantilism explains both the integration of British North America into the [[Atlantic Economy]] and the later colonial argument that tighter imperial regulation threatened established economic autonomy.

## APUSH Significance

Mercantilist policymakers treated trade as a source of state revenue and strategic strength rather than as a purely private activity. Enumerated colonial products were directed through English channels, and shipping rules favored English or colonial vessels. These policies supported shipbuilding, commerce, and access to imperial markets in some colonies while limiting whom colonists could trade with and how goods moved. The system also connected colonial prosperity to plantation commodities and the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]].

## Regulation and Resistance

For much of the early eighteenth century, uneven enforcement contributed to [[Salutary Neglect]], allowing colonial assemblies and merchants to develop habits of autonomy within the empire. After the [[French and Indian War]], Britain sought more revenue and stricter control, turning older trade regulation into a constitutional conflict over representation and Parliament's authority. Mercantilism should not be reduced to the idea that colonies only lost: many colonists profited within imperial networks. The political crisis developed when the costs, enforcement, and legitimacy of the system changed and colonists interpreted those changes as threats to their rights.

## The Imperial Logic

Mercantilist policy assumed that government should organize commerce to increase national power. Colonies supplied raw materials, consumed metropolitan manufactures, and supported shipping. Navigation rules reserved parts of trade for English or colonial vessels, and enumerated commodities such as tobacco were directed through the imperial system. Customs duties and regulations sought revenue, but the larger goal was to keep strategic trade and maritime employment within the empire.

The system created benefits as well as restrictions for colonists. British naval power protected shipping, colonial vessels gained access to imperial markets, and bounties encouraged selected products. Merchants could profit through both legal routes and smuggling. Because enforcement varied, colonists learned to negotiate regulations rather than treating every rule as intolerable from the beginning.

## Enforcement and Colonial Development

The [[Navigation Acts]] supplied the major statutory framework, while customs officers, vice-admiralty courts, governors, and the Board of Trade attempted enforcement. Distance, limited personnel, local juries, corruption, and war made consistent supervision difficult. This uneven practice contributed to [[Salutary Neglect]], under which colonial assemblies and merchants acquired substantial room to manage local affairs.

Colonial trade extended far beyond a simple Britain-to-colony route. Mainland merchants exchanged food, timber, fish, and livestock with the Caribbean; plantation products moved to Europe; manufactured goods and enslaved Africans crossed the Atlantic. Britain tried to channel this network for imperial advantage, but local economies depended on commerce that officials could not fully control.

After the [[French and Indian War]], Parliament sought tighter enforcement and new revenue from the colonies. Many colonists distinguished between regulation of imperial commerce, which had precedent, and taxes they believed designed primarily to raise revenue without consent. Their protests transformed an economic dispute into a constitutional argument about representation and sovereignty.

Mercantilism should not be confused with the [[Atlantic Economy]] as a whole. The Atlantic economy describes the actual networks of goods, labor, capital, and migration; mercantilism describes government attempts to direct those networks. The gap between policy and practice explains both colonial prosperity within the empire and the later conflict over imperial control.
