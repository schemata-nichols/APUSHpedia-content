> **Puritanism** was a movement within English Protestantism that sought to “purify” the Church of England of practices its members considered insufficiently reformed. Puritans emphasized scripture, disciplined community, covenant theology, and the sovereignty of God. Some migrated to [[New England Colonies|New England]], especially [[Massachusetts Bay Colony]], intending to build a model Christian society rather than a modern system of universal religious liberty. For APUSH, Puritanism explains important New England institutions and cultural values while revealing how a community seeking freedom to practice its beliefs could restrict dissent and define political participation narrowly.

## APUSH Significance

The [[Puritan Great Migration]] of the 1630s brought families, ministers, and resources that supported stable towns and congregations. Local government and church membership were closely connected, though the church and colonial state were not identical institutions. Literacy was valued so believers could read scripture, contributing to schools and Harvard College. The ideal of a covenanted community encouraged collective responsibility, but authorities punished challenges from figures such as [[Roger Williams]] and [[Anne Hutchinson]].

## Change and Decline

Later generations faced tensions between inherited membership and the conversion experience required for full church participation, prompting the [[Half-Way Covenant]]. Commercial growth, territorial expansion, religious diversity, and crown intervention weakened the early Puritan order. The [[Salem Witch Trials]] exposed fear and division rather than representing ordinary Puritan life, and the [[First Great Awakening]] later challenged established religious authority. Puritan political control declined, but its influence survived in New England education, moral reform, communal ideals, and narratives of national mission. Puritanism should be distinguished from the Pilgrims' Separatism, though both belonged to the broader English Reformed tradition.

## Covenant and Community

Puritans understood society through covenants: binding agreements among believers and between a community and God. The gathered congregation selected a minister and disciplined members, while civil officials upheld a moral order that Puritans believed essential to collective success. Church membership and political participation were related but not identical, and rules varied among colonies and over time. The system was participatory for approved male members without recognizing equal freedom of conscience.

John Winthrop's image of a community watched by the world expressed both religious mission and social discipline. Puritans valued literacy so people could read scripture, leading Massachusetts to require schooling and communities to support education. Their commitment to ordered families reinforced male authority, although women's spiritual membership and labor were indispensable to congregations and households.

## Dissent and Conflict

Challenges from [[Roger Williams]] and [[Anne Hutchinson]] exposed the boundaries of acceptable belief. Williams argued for a clearer separation of civil power from religion and criticized colonial land claims. Hutchinson's meetings and theological arguments appeared to threaten clerical and gender authority. Banishment preserved Massachusetts orthodoxy while helping create alternative settlements.

Puritan expansion also brought conflict with Native peoples. Colonists interpreted military victories and disasters through providence, while their settlement and livestock transformed Native land use. The [[Pequot War]] and [[King Philip's War]] cannot be reduced to religious difference, but Puritan claims to a godly mission helped justify warfare and territorial growth.

## Adaptation and Legacy

Later generations worried that commercial success and inherited membership were weakening the founding mission. The [[Half-Way Covenant]] allowed the baptized children of church members a partial relationship to the church even without a conversion experience, seeking to preserve community attachment. The [[Salem Witch Trials]] reflected local fear, factional conflict, and legal breakdown rather than a routine feature of Puritan life.

Congregational traditions, town institutions, literacy, and moral reform outlasted the first generation even as New England became more diverse and commercially integrated. Puritanism should not be equated with all colonial religion or modern prudishness. It was a specific reform tradition whose attempt to create a covenanted society produced education and local participation alongside coercion, exclusion, and conflict.
