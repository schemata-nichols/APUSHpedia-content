> **British Colonization** was the establishment and expansion of English, and later British, settlements along the Atlantic coast of North America during the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. Colonists pursued profit, land, religious communities, and strategic advantage through charter companies, proprietorships, and royal colonies. Their settlements displaced Native peoples, imported European family and property systems, and developed labor forces that shifted from indentured servitude toward racial [[Chattel Slavery]]. Considerable local self-government emerged within the empire, especially under [[Salutary Neglect]]. For APUSH, British colonization created neither one uniform society nor an independent nation in waiting; regional environments, migration, labor, and Native relations produced distinct colonies linked by empire and Atlantic trade.

## APUSH Significance

British colonization established institutions and conflicts that shaped the later United States:

1. **Colonies had different origins.** Companies, proprietors, religious communities, and the crown pursued overlapping but distinct goals.
2. **Land required Native dispossession.** Settlement expanded through trade, treaties, warfare, disease, and occupation of Indigenous territory.
3. **Labor systems hardened racially.** Indentured servitude remained important, but plantation regions increasingly adopted hereditary African slavery.
4. **Local government became customary.** Assemblies and town institutions gave property-holding colonists experience defending local authority.
5. **Atlantic integration encouraged regional specialization.** Tobacco, rice, shipping, grain, and commerce connected the colonies to imperial markets.

British colonization differs from [[Spanish Colonization]] in settlement patterns and imperial administration, but both pursued power and resources through unequal relationships with Native peoples and coerced workers.

## Chesapeake Beginnings

The [[Virginia Company]] founded [[Jamestown]] in 1607 as a commercial venture. Early settlers suffered from disease, hunger, weak planning, and conflict with the Powhatan Confederacy. Tobacco cultivation eventually made the colony profitable and increased demand for land and labor.

The headright system awarded land for financing migration, helping wealthy planters accumulate property. [[Indentured Servitude]] supplied much early labor as migrants exchanged years of work for passage. High mortality and unequal access to land produced instability.

The [[House of Burgesses]] established representative government for property-holding colonists. [[Bacon's Rebellion]] later exposed tensions among frontier settlers, planters, colonial officials, and Native peoples. Afterward, elites increasingly relied on enslaved African labor and racial law, reducing dependence on potentially rebellious European servants.

## New England Communities

Pilgrims established Plymouth in 1620 and adopted the [[Mayflower Compact]] as an agreement for civil government. Puritans founded Massachusetts Bay in 1630, seeking a disciplined religious commonwealth rather than broad religious liberty.

New England's climate discouraged plantation staples, while family migration and longer life expectancy supported towns and population growth. Mixed farming, fishing, shipbuilding, and Atlantic commerce developed alongside congregational communities.

Religious uniformity produced dissent. Authorities expelled [[Roger Williams]] and [[Anne Hutchinson]], and Rhode Island became known for greater toleration. Puritan relationships with Native peoples shifted from exchange and alliance to expanding conflict, including the [[Pequot War]] and [[King Philip's War]].

## Middle and Southern Colonies

The [[Middle Colonies]] combined grain agriculture, ports, commerce, and greater ethnic and religious diversity. After England seized [[New Netherland]], New York retained a mixed population. [[William Penn]]'s Pennsylvania attracted [[Quakers]] and other migrants through land and religious toleration, though settlement still displaced Native communities.

The [[Southern Colonies]] developed plantation economies. Maryland resembled Virginia's tobacco society, while Carolina and Georgia increasingly relied on rice, indigo, and enslaved African knowledge and labor. The Lower South developed larger enslaved populations and stronger ties to Caribbean plantation systems.

These regions were not isolated. Coastal trade, migration, credit, and imperial regulation connected their economies and populations.

## Native Relations and Territorial Expansion

British settlements entered lands already governed by Native nations. Early survival often depended on Native food, trade, diplomacy, and knowledge. As settler populations grew, demand for permanent farms intensified conflict because English property practices sought exclusive control of land.

Native nations pursued their own strategies, including alliance, trade, migration, and war. They used rivalry among European powers to preserve autonomy where possible. Disease weakened many communities, but colonial victory was never simply automatic.

Unlike some Spanish borderlands, British colonies generally developed fewer institutions for incorporating Native peoples into colonial society. Settlers more often sought land and separation, though practices varied by region.

## Empire and Self-Government

The crown regulated trade through [[Mercantilism]] and the [[Navigation Acts]], intending colonial commerce to strengthen Britain. Enforcement remained uneven, and smuggling was common. During salutary neglect, assemblies gained influence over taxation, spending, and local administration.

Colonists did not view this autonomy as independence. Most remained loyal British subjects and benefited from imperial military and commercial protection. However, customary self-government later shaped resistance when Britain attempted tighter administration after the [[French and Indian War]].

## Colonial Diversity and Imperial Control

British colonization produced a set of [[Regional Differences in the British Colonies|regional societies]], not a single colonial model. Environment mattered, but migration patterns, labor choices, imperial policy, and relationships with Native peoples turned geography into institutions.

The colonies also combined political participation with exclusion. Assemblies represented some colonists while women, unpropertied men, enslaved people, and Native peoples lacked equal authority. That contradiction became a lasting feature of American development.
