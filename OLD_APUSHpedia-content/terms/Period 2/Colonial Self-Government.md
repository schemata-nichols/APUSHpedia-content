> **Colonial self-government** refers to the representative assemblies and local institutions through which British American colonists managed taxation, spending, property, and community affairs while remaining within the British Empire. Institutions such as the [[House of Burgesses]], New England town meetings, and elected colonial legislatures gave portions of the population political experience, especially during [[Salutary Neglect]]. Participation was limited by property, race, sex, religion, and legal status, and royal officials retained important authority. For APUSH, colonial self-government created expectations of consent and local control that later collided with Parliament's claims of sovereignty after the [[French and Indian War]].

## APUSH Significance

Different colonies had royal, proprietary, or corporate structures, but most developed assemblies that claimed the power to levy taxes and control public funds. Assemblies often used governors' salaries to bargain with imperial appointees, while county courts, churches, and town meetings administered local affairs. These practices encouraged political participation among eligible white men and trained colonial elites in legislation, but they did not constitute modern democracy. Enslaved people, women, servants, Native peoples, and many poor men lacked formal power.

## Rights Within Empire

Most colonists initially understood self-government as compatible with loyalty to the crown. The constitutional crisis emerged when post-1763 taxes and enforcement raised the question of whether Parliament could impose internal taxes without colonial representation. Colonial assemblies defended inherited English rights and their own established jurisdiction, eventually coordinating through the [[Continental Congress]]. Self-government therefore mattered not because the colonies had already become independent, but because practical autonomy created institutions and expectations from which resistance could grow. Its history also exposes the contradiction between demands for political consent and colonial systems of dispossession and unfree labor.

## Assemblies and Local Institutions

Representative bodies emerged under different colonial charters. Virginia's [[House of Burgesses]] began under company rule, while New England towns and colonial legislatures developed from covenanted settlement and local administration. Proprietary and royal colonies also possessed assemblies. Their structures varied, but elected representatives commonly claimed authority over taxation and local expenditure.

Control of money gave assemblies leverage over governors. Representatives could condition salaries or appropriations on acceptance of colonial priorities, while governors used appointments, vetoes, and instructions from the Crown. Politics therefore involved recurring negotiation rather than either complete popular rule or direct royal command. During [[Salutary Neglect]], assemblies converted practical autonomy into habits they later defended as constitutional rights.

Local government mattered too. Town meetings in parts of New England allowed eligible men to decide community questions, while county courts and parish institutions were more central in dispersed southern settlements. Geography, settlement pattern, religion, and labor systems shaped how colonists encountered public authority.

## Participation and Exclusion

Colonial electorates were often broad by contemporary European standards because land was more widely available to white men, but suffrage was never universal. Property, taxpaying, religion, race, sex, age, and residency restricted participation. Enslaved people, women, most Native people, servants, and many poor men could not exercise equal political power. Even eligible voters operated within societies dominated by wealthy families and patronage.

Assemblies also governed other people without consent. They created slave codes, authorized militia campaigns, regulated Native trade, punished dissent, and supported established churches. Colonial self-government thus expanded local representation while entrenching racial, gender, and class hierarchy. This contradiction anticipates later conflicts within American [[Democracy]].

After 1763, parliamentary taxation and tighter imperial administration threatened not only abstract rights but institutions that colonists had used for generations. Protesters argued that only their elected assemblies could tax them internally. The First and later [[Continental Congress]] then coordinated separate colonies without immediately replacing local governments. Self-government supplied institutional experience for revolution, but the new nation still had to confront the exclusions embedded in colonial practice.
