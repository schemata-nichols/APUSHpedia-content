> **Native American–European conflict** describes the wars, raids, territorial struggles, and diplomatic crises produced by European colonization of Native homelands. Conflict grew from competing claims to land, trade, political authority, and resources, but it was never a single two-sided war. Native nations pursued distinct interests, formed alliances with Europeans and one another, and changed strategies as imperial power shifted. Europeans also fought each other with Native allies. For APUSH, these conflicts show that colonization required force and negotiation and that Indigenous peoples remained central actors in the contest for North America.

## APUSH Significance

Early conflicts such as the [[Pequot War]] and [[King Philip's War]] enabled New England colonists to seize territory and weaken Native resistance, though they also imposed heavy costs on colonists. In Virginia, warfare with the Powhatan chiefdom followed cycles of trade, settlement, and expansion. Spanish mission systems provoked resistance including the [[Pueblo Revolt]]. Disease and population loss altered power, but epidemics did not eliminate Native military or diplomatic influence.

## Empire and Native Strategy

By the eighteenth century, Britain, France, and Spain competed through alliances with Native nations that sought weapons, trade, security, or leverage against rivals. The [[Iroquois Confederacy]] used diplomacy to protect its position, while other nations resisted both Haudenosaunee and European expansion. The [[French and Indian War]] became a decisive imperial struggle, but Native goals did not simply match those of European allies. Conflict should therefore be distinguished from unavoidable “cultural misunderstanding.” Its repeated cause was settler colonialism: expanding European populations claimed land permanently, while Native nations defended sovereignty through warfare, accommodation, migration, and diplomacy.

## Sources of Conflict

Land use created recurring tension. English farmers and livestock required exclusive fields and expanding boundaries, while many Native communities organized access through seasonal movement, kinship, hunting territories, and collective authority. Europeans often interpreted cultivation and fences as the only valid signs of ownership. Native peoples understood treaties as political agreements but did not necessarily accept English claims that a sale permanently ended all use or sovereignty.

Trade added both cooperation and danger. European metal tools, cloth, weapons, and other goods could strengthen particular Native groups, while colonists depended on furs, food, geographic knowledge, and military allies. Competition over trade routes and firearms altered rivalries. Debt, fraud, and efforts to control commerce could turn mutual dependence into warfare.

Epidemic disease caused enormous population loss and political disruption, sometimes before sustained settlement reached a community. Disease did not make conquest inevitable or erase Native power. Survivors regrouped, formed new settlements and confederacies, and pursued alliances suited to changed circumstances. Colonists nevertheless used depopulation to claim that land was vacant or providentially available.

## Wars and Shifting Coalitions

In New England, the [[Pequot War]] ended with killing, captivity, and enslavement after English colonists and Native allies defeated the Pequots. [[King Philip's War]] later brought a broad but not universal Native coalition against colonial expansion. Native and colonial communities fought on both sides, demonstrating that neither category was politically unified.

In the Chesapeake, Powhatan leaders initially used trade and diplomacy to manage Jamestown, but tobacco expansion intensified conflict. In the Southwest, Pueblo peoples coordinated the [[Pueblo Revolt]] against Spanish labor demands and religious repression. These wars grew from different local systems and should not be treated as repetitions of one frontier pattern.

European imperial competition created strategic choices. Native nations allied with France, Britain, Spain, or colonists to counter nearby rivals and defend territory. The [[Iroquois Confederacy]] used diplomacy to preserve leverage among empires. Alliances could provide weapons and bargaining power but also draw communities into destructive wars.

By 1754, Native nations still controlled large territories and shaped imperial outcomes. The central change was not their disappearance but a growing imbalance as settler populations, military resources, and land demands increased. Centering Native strategy reveals conflict as a contest among sovereign peoples rather than a natural stage of European expansion.
