> **New Immigration** describes the large migration from southern and eastern Europe to the United States from roughly the 1880s through the early 1920s. Italians, Jews, Poles, Greeks, Slavs, and others joined continuing migration from elsewhere, drawn by industrial work and family networks and pushed by poverty, land pressure, persecution, and political instability. Newcomers built ethnic neighborhoods, religious institutions, businesses, unions, and political organizations while facing dangerous labor and [[Nativism]]. For APUSH, “new” reflected native-born comparison with earlier northern and western European migrants, not a common immigrant identity. Their incorporation reshaped cities and culture while racial theories and wartime nationalism produced restrictive quotas.

## APUSH Significance

New immigration connects industrial growth to urban politics and restriction:

1. **Industry demanded labor.** Factories, mines, construction, and services relied on immigrant workers.
2. **Chain migration sustained communities.** Family and village networks shaped destinations and employment.
3. **Ethnic institutions aided adaptation.** Churches, newspapers, mutual-aid societies, and businesses offered support.
4. **Nativists racialized difference.** Critics treated religion, language, and nationality as evidence of permanent inferiority.
5. **Restriction became national policy.** Literacy tests and quotas sharply reduced entry by the 1920s.

New immigration differs from the post-1965 [[Immigration]] stream, which drew more heavily from Latin America, Asia, the Caribbean, and Africa under a different legal system.

## Causes and Patterns

[[Industrialization]] created demand for low-wage labor, while steamship travel reduced cost and time. Letters, remittances, and labor recruiters connected particular European villages to American workplaces.

Migrants left for diverse reasons. Jews fled antisemitic violence in the Russian Empire; Italians faced rural poverty; political and religious minorities sought security. Some intended permanent settlement, while others were “birds of passage” who expected to return with savings.

Entry through [[Ellis Island]] became a powerful symbol, though immigrants also arrived through other ports and land borders.

## Work and Urban Communities

Immigrants concentrated in industrial cities but also entered mines, farms, and construction. Employers used ethnic labor networks and sometimes recruited one group against striking workers from another.

[[Tenements]] offered proximity to work and community but often lacked space, sanitation, and safety. Family economies combined men's, women's, and children's wages. Women worked in garments, domestic service, factories, and home production.

Ethnic neighborhoods were not simply barriers to assimilation. They provided credit, worship, language, employment contacts, political organization, and mutual aid while immigrants learned to navigate American institutions.

## Politics, Labor, and Reform

Urban [[Political Machines]] exchanged jobs, food, legal assistance, and services for votes. This relationship could help immigrants gain influence while sustaining patronage and corruption.

Immigrant workers participated in unions and strikes. The garment industry's organizing and the Triangle Shirtwaist fire connected immigrant labor, women activists, and [[Progressivism|Progressive]] workplace reform.

Settlement houses such as [[Hull House]] provided education and services. Reformers sometimes respected immigrant cultures, but [[Americanization]] efforts could also impose middle-class Protestant standards.

## Nativism and Restriction

Nativists blamed immigrants for low wages, radicalism, urban disorder, Catholic or Jewish difference, and political machines. Scientific racism classified Europeans into supposedly unequal races and claimed newer arrivals could not assimilate.

[[World War I]] intensified pressure to abandon foreign languages and loyalties. The Red Scare associated immigrants with anarchism and communism. Congress imposed a literacy test and then adopted emergency quotas.

The [[Immigration Act of 1924]] established national-origins quotas favoring northern and western Europeans and excluding Asian immigration. Mexican migration remained less restricted by quotas because southwestern employers demanded labor, though coercive removal followed during the Depression.

Law treated migrants through racial and gendered categories rather than through one consistent standard. Asian exclusion had developed before European quotas, and courts decided who could naturalize under laws reserving citizenship for “white persons” and people of African descent. Contract-labor restrictions, public-charge rules, and medical inspections gave officials additional discretion. Women could face exclusion based on assumptions about dependency or morality, while married women's citizenship was at times tied to their husbands. Immigration policy thus helped government define the boundaries of membership.

## Adaptation, Belonging, and Restriction

Earlier Irish and German immigrants had also faced nativism before later being treated as established Americans. The category “new immigrant” therefore reveals changing boundaries of whiteness and national belonging.

Assimilation was neither immediate nor one-directional. Immigrants learned English, naturalized, and entered institutions while changing American food, religion, labor, politics, music, and urban life.

For comparison, the decisive change of the 1920s was not the appearance of nativism but the creation of a durable numerical system for selecting nationalities. The quota regime translated cultural prejudice and racial theory into federal administration, ending the comparatively open European migration system that had supplied much of the industrial workforce.
