> **Populism** was the late nineteenth-century agrarian movement that challenged railroad, banking, and corporate power through cooperatives, political education, and the [[People's Party]]. Farmers faced debt, declining commodity prices, high transportation costs, and a deflationary currency while participating in national markets. The Populists demanded government ownership or regulation of key infrastructure, currency expansion through free silver, a graduated income tax, and democratic reforms. They built interracial cooperation in some places but also confronted racism and white supremacy. For APUSH, Populism was more than rural resentment: it offered a structural critique of [[Industrial Capitalism]] and influenced reforms later adopted during the [[Progressivism|Progressive Era]].

## APUSH Significance

Populism connected farmers' economic problems to democratic reform:

1. **Market integration produced insecurity.** Farmers depended on railroads, banks, machinery, and global prices.
2. **Organization preceded the party.** Granges and [[Farmers' Alliances]] built cooperatives and political networks.
3. **The Omaha Platform proposed systemic change.** Populists sought public control over money, transportation, and political institutions.
4. **Race shaped coalition limits.** Interracial organizing threatened southern elites but rarely overcame disfranchisement and racism.
5. **Defeat did not erase influence.** Income taxation, direct election of senators, regulation, and currency reform outlived the party.

Populism differs from later uses of “populist” for any anti-elite rhetoric. The historical movement had specific organizations and an agrarian reform program.

## Agricultural Crisis

Farmers expanded production through mechanization and western settlement, but increased output contributed to lower prices. Many borrowed for land and equipment, so deflation made debts harder to repay.

Railroads charged rates that farmers considered discriminatory, while grain-elevator and merchant fees reduced income. Because households bought manufactured goods in protected markets but sold crops at world prices, many believed national policy favored industry and finance.

Drought and environmental limits intensified hardship in parts of the West and South. Individual thrift could not solve problems embedded in credit, transportation, and currency.

## From Grange to Alliance

The Grange promoted education, cooperation, and state railroad regulation. The Supreme Court initially upheld some Granger laws before later decisions encouraged federal regulation through the [[Interstate Commerce Act]].

Southern and National Farmers' Alliances organized cooperatives and proposed a subtreasury system in which the federal government would store crops and provide low-interest loans. Lecturers connected local grievances to concentrated economic power.

The Colored Farmers' Alliance organized Black farmers facing both economic exploitation and racial violence. Cooperation across race occurred, but white southern Democrats used intimidation, election fraud, and white supremacy to preserve control.

## People's Party

The People's Party formed nationally in the early 1890s. Its Omaha Platform demanded free silver, a graduated income tax, government ownership of railroads and communications, direct election of senators, and political reforms.

Populists also supported labor demands such as the eight-hour day and condemned the use of government force against workers. This attempted farmer-labor coalition faced regional and occupational divisions.

The party won western states and elected members of Congress but struggled in the industrial Northeast and the Democratic South.

## [[Election of 1896]]

The Panic of 1893 intensified debate over currency. Free silver advocates argued that expanding money would raise prices and ease debt; defenders of gold emphasized stability and creditor confidence.

Democrats nominated [[William Jennings Bryan]] after his “Cross of Gold” speech. Populists endorsed Bryan to avoid splitting the silver vote, sacrificing some independent identity. Republican William McKinley won on a coalition supporting gold, tariffs, and business recovery.

Economic improvement, Republican organization, and employer influence weakened the Populist Party after defeat.

## Race, Gender, and Legacy

Populist women such as Mary Elizabeth Lease became prominent speakers, and alliance activity expanded women's public political roles even before national suffrage.

The movement's racial record was contradictory. Some organizers pursued shared economic interests, while others accepted segregation or racist appeals. Southern disfranchisement destroyed possibilities for durable biracial democracy.

Progressives later adopted railroad regulation, direct election of senators, initiative and referendum, and income taxation. The Federal Reserve addressed currency through a different mechanism than free silver.

The comparison has limits: many Progressives were urban middle-class reformers who preferred expert administration, whereas Populists emphasized producer cooperation and mass democracy. Still, both responded to the concentration of power created by a national corporate economy, making Populism an important bridge between agrarian protest and twentieth-century federal reform.

## Commercial Farming and Democratic Power

Populism arose from commercial agriculture, not resistance to markets in general. Farmers wanted fairer access to credit, transportation, and political power within a national economy.

Its decline as a party should not be confused with failure of its analysis. Populists placed economic concentration and democratic accountability at the center of national debate, leaving a vocabulary later movements repeatedly revived.
