> **Political Polarization** is the widening ideological and social distance between the major parties and their supporters, especially evident from the late twentieth century into the twenty-first. Conservative southern realignment, civil-rights and cultural conflict, economic change, partisan media, geographic sorting, institutional incentives, and increasingly nationalized elections made Republicans and Democrats more internally cohesive and mutually hostile. Polarization can clarify voter choices, but it also encourages gridlock, distrust, and treatment of opponents as illegitimate. For APUSH, the development is best understood as a historical realignment within the enduring two-party system, not as proof that earlier American politics lacked intense conflict.

## APUSH Significance

Modern polarization reshaped democratic government:

1. **Party coalitions sorted ideologically.** Conservative Democrats and liberal Republicans became less common.
2. **Race and region drove realignment.** Civil-rights conflict helped move the South toward Republicans and Black voters toward Democrats.
3. **Economic and cultural issues reinforced one another.** Taxes, regulation, religion, gender, immigration, and national identity aligned with party loyalty.
4. **Institutions magnified division.** Primaries, district boundaries, the Senate, courts, and separated powers rewarded confrontation or enabled obstruction.
5. **Media and national campaigns changed political identity.** Partisans increasingly consumed different information and viewed politics as a struggle over ways of life.

Polarization differs from ordinary partisanship. It describes parties moving farther apart and political identity shaping attitudes across many otherwise separate issues.

## Historical Realignment

The [[New Deal Coalition]] once included northern workers, immigrants, Black voters, white southerners, and liberals despite major ideological differences. The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] made that coalition increasingly unstable.

National Democrats embraced civil-rights legislation, while the Republican [[Southern Strategy]] appealed to white resistance through themes of states' rights, law and order, and local control. This shift unfolded over decades and also reflected suburbanization, evangelical politics, and economic development.

The [[Reagan Revolution]] consolidated a conservative coalition favoring tax cuts, anticommunism, deregulation, traditional social values, and reduced welfare. Democrats became more consistently liberal as conservative southerners left the party and northern moderates declined.

## Economic and Cultural Conflict

[[Deindustrialization]], inequality, globalization, and regional growth altered class politics. Republicans increasingly attracted business interests, many white working-class voters, and rural communities, while Democrats gained strength among professionals, urban residents, racial minorities, and organized labor.

Court decisions and activism around abortion, school prayer, gun rights, feminism, and gay rights mobilized religious and secular constituencies. The [[New Right]] linked these cultural questions to economic conservatism.

[[Post-1965 Immigration]] and demographic change intensified disputes over borders, citizenship, language, and national identity. Economic anxiety did not operate separately from race and culture; political leaders connected them in competing narratives.

## Institutions and Political Strategy

Primary elections can reward candidates appealing to active partisan voters. Gerrymandering may reduce competitive districts, though geographic concentration also creates safe seats. Equal state representation in the Senate and the [[Electoral College]] can produce power without a national popular majority, raising questions of legitimacy.

Congressional leaders increasingly used disciplined voting, agenda control, filibusters, and procedural conflict. Divided government produced bargaining at times but also shutdowns, delayed appointments, and reliance on executive orders and courts.

The [[Contract with America]] and fights over [[Bill Clinton]]'s presidency illustrated aggressive congressional partisanship in the 1990s. The disputed [[Election of 2000]], wars after September 11, and battles over health care deepened mutual distrust.

## Media, Technology, and Identity

The end of the broadcast-era media environment created ideologically targeted cable, talk radio, websites, and later [[Social Media]]. These systems widened access and mobilization but rewarded outrage, rapid claims, and selective exposure.

Media did not create party differences by itself. It amplified divisions rooted in institutions, elite strategy, race, religion, geography, and economics. Political identity increasingly influenced social relationships and perceptions of factual information.

Nationalized campaigns also weakened local variation. Voters came to evaluate congressional and state candidates through attitudes toward national party leaders.

## Twenty-First-Century Consequences

The [[Great Recession]], Barack Obama's presidency, the Tea Party, Donald Trump's rise, and movements over policing and inequality intensified conflict. Disputes increasingly concerned not just policy but who represented the legitimate nation.

Polarization can increase participation and make party programs more coherent. Yet negative partisanship—voting mainly against the opposing party—can excuse norm-breaking by one's own side and make compromise appear like betrayal.

APUSH comparison places the present alongside the 1790s, 1850s, and [[Reconstruction]], all eras of severe division. Modern polarization is distinctive in its stable ideological sorting, national media system, and interaction with contemporary institutions, but conflict itself has always been part of American [[Democracy]].
