> **Post-1965 Immigration** describes migration to the United States after the [[Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965]] abolished national-origins quotas and prioritized family reunification and occupational skills. Migrants increasingly arrived from Latin America, Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, and the Middle East, while refugee admissions and unauthorized migration followed distinct legal and economic pressures. Newcomers reshaped cities, suburbs, workplaces, religion, culture, and electoral politics. For APUSH, this migration reversed earlier exclusionary priorities and accelerated demographic change, yet it did not create an unrestricted system: numerical limits, visa categories, enforcement, race, class, and citizenship continued to determine who could enter and belong.

## APUSH Significance

Post-1965 immigration transformed modern American society:

1. **National-origins selection ended.** Family and employment categories replaced a quota system favoring northern and western Europe.
2. **Sources of immigration shifted.** Asian and Latin American migration grew substantially and diversified national communities.
3. **[[Migration]] followed global and domestic forces.** Wars, U.S. foreign policy, labor demand, family networks, and inequality shaped movement.
4. **Legal status produced unequal experiences.** Citizens, permanent residents, refugees, temporary workers, and unauthorized migrants possessed different rights.
5. **Immigration became politically polarizing.** Debates joined economics and border enforcement to race, culture, federalism, and national identity.

The term differs from [[New Immigration]], which usually refers to southern and eastern European migration from the 1880s through the early twentieth century.

## The 1965 Law

The [[Immigration Act of 1924]] had used national-origins quotas and excluded Asian immigrants from citizenship-based entry. Civil-rights-era criticism made explicit national discrimination increasingly difficult to defend.

The 1965 act eliminated national-origins quotas and created preferences for close family members and workers with specified skills. Immediate relatives of U.S. citizens were exempt from some numerical limits. Policymakers did not fully anticipate how family sponsorship would sustain chain migration from newly admitted populations.

The law also imposed numerical limits on Western Hemisphere immigration for the first time. Reform expanded opportunity for some migrants while narrowing legal channels used by others.

## Migration Patterns

Asian migration grew from countries including China, India, Korea, the Philippines, and Vietnam. Family networks, education, professional employment, and refugee policy produced internally diverse communities rather than one uniform Asian American experience.

Migration from Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean reflected geographic proximity, labor markets, political instability, family ties, and prior U.S. involvement. Puerto Ricans were U.S. citizens and moved under a different legal framework.

Refugees from Cuba, Southeast Asia, the Soviet bloc, and later conflicts entered through policies shaped partly by the [[Cold War]]. The 1980 Refugee Act created a more general system, though political priorities continued to affect admissions.

## Work, Community, and Geography

Immigrants entered agriculture, construction, domestic and service work, manufacturing, health care, technology, and professional fields. Their labor supported economic growth but could be made vulnerable by legal status, employer power, and limited bargaining rights.

Settlement expanded beyond traditional gateway cities into suburbs, the South, Midwest, and smaller communities. Churches, businesses, newspapers, cultural organizations, and transnational networks helped newcomers adapt while changing local institutions.

Immigration contributed to [[Globalization]] through remittances, trade, entrepreneurship, language, food, media, and family connections. Assimilation remained multidirectional: migrants entered American institutions while reshaping what counted as American culture.

## Law, Enforcement, and Unauthorized Migration

The [[Immigration Reform and Control Act]] of 1986 legalized millions of qualifying residents, penalized employers for knowingly hiring unauthorized workers, and increased enforcement. Employer sanctions proved difficult to administer and encouraged document fraud and workplace discrimination.

Legal avenues did not match labor demand and family connections. Unauthorized migration grew, while repeated enforcement measures made crossing more dangerous without eliminating underlying incentives.

The boundary between federal authority and state or local enforcement became contested. Policies affecting schools, policing, public benefits, detention, asylum, and childhood arrivals turned immigration into a recurring constitutional and humanitarian debate.

## Politics and Interpretation

Supporters emphasize economic contribution, family unity, refuge, and the nation's immigrant traditions. Critics emphasize wage competition, public costs, security, cultural change, and governmental control of borders. Outcomes vary by industry, region, time, and workers' bargaining power.

Immigrants and their children became increasingly important voters and organizers, while restrictionist movements used immigration to mobilize fears about demographic change. The issue contributed to modern [[Political Polarization]].

Post-1965 immigration represents both change and continuity. The origins and legal categories changed dramatically, but labor demand, family networks, nativism, and unequal access to citizenship remained features of American [[Immigration]] history.
