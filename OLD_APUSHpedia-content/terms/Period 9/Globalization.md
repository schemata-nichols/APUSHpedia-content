> **Globalization** is the accelerating integration of economies, production, finance, communication, culture, and migration across national borders, especially visible in the United States after the 1970s and the [[End of the Cold War]]. Trade agreements, container shipping, multinational corporations, digital technology, deregulated finance, and global supply chains lowered barriers to exchange. American consumers gained inexpensive goods and firms reached new markets, while manufacturing communities faced plant closures, weakened unions, and wage pressure. For APUSH, globalization is neither entirely new nor purely economic: it intensified older international connections and reshaped immigration, regional inequality, political conflict, culture, and the scope of national policy.

## APUSH Significance

Globalization reorganized the postindustrial United States:

1. **Production crossed borders.** Firms separated design, manufacturing, assembly, and sales across global supply chains.
2. **Trade and finance expanded.** Agreements and institutions reduced barriers and increased capital mobility.
3. **Domestic regions experienced unequal effects.** Technology and services grew while many industrial communities faced [[Deindustrialization]].
4. **[[Migration]] and culture became more transnational.** People, media, remittances, and ideas connected American communities to the world.
5. **Political backlash intensified.** Debates over jobs, sovereignty, immigration, security, and inequality contributed to [[Political Polarization]].

Globalization differs from simple international trade, which long predated it. The modern development involved the scale, speed, and organizational integration of cross-border activity.

## Historical Foundations

The United States had participated in an [[Atlantic Economy]], overseas trade, immigration, and imperial expansion for centuries. After [[World War II]], American leaders built institutions that encouraged monetary stability, reconstruction, and trade among noncommunist countries.

The postwar United States possessed exceptional industrial and financial power. As European and Asian economies recovered, competition increased. Containerization, jet travel, telecommunications, and computers dramatically lowered the cost of coordinating activity across distance.

The end of communist regimes opened additional markets and strengthened confidence in market capitalism. Policy choices—not technology alone—shaped globalization through tariff reductions, investment rules, financial deregulation, and intellectual-property protections.

## Trade, Corporations, and Labor

The [[North American Free Trade Agreement]] reduced trade barriers among the United States, Mexico, and Canada. The [[World Trade Organization]] established broader rules and dispute procedures. Supporters argued that trade increased efficiency, exports, and consumer choice; critics emphasized job displacement, labor standards, and corporate influence.

Multinational firms moved some production to lower-cost regions while retaining management, finance, research, and marketing functions. Automation also eliminated industrial jobs, making it misleading to attribute every factory loss to trade alone.

The [[Rust Belt]] experienced population loss, reduced tax bases, and weakened unions, while technology, finance, logistics, health care, and other parts of the [[Service Economy]] expanded. New employment did not always match the wages, security, or locations of lost manufacturing work.

## Digital and Financial Integration

The [[Digital Revolution]] enabled rapid communication, data processing, online commerce, and coordination of global supply chains. American technology firms gained enormous economic and cultural influence.

Capital moved more freely across borders, allowing investment and growth but also transmitting financial shocks. The [[Great Recession]] demonstrated how mortgage markets, banks, securities, and international finance could spread instability.

Digital networks widened access to information and organizing while concentrating data and platform power. Globalization therefore created new forms of dependence as well as openness.

## Migration and Cultural Exchange

[[Post-1965 Immigration]] changed American demographics through migration from Latin America, Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, and elsewhere. Immigrants maintained transnational family, business, political, and cultural connections aided by travel and communication.

Food, music, film, sports, fashion, and news circulated globally. American culture influenced other societies, while American identity became more visibly multicultural. These exchanges provoked both cosmopolitan celebration and nativist reaction.

Global labor demand coexisted with restrictive borders and unequal legal status. This tension connected immigration policy to agriculture, service work, technology, family reunification, and national security.

## Politics, Inequality, and Interpretation

Globalization produced gains that were widely distributed through lower consumer prices but losses that were often concentrated in particular workers and communities. Education, capital ownership, geography, race, and bargaining power shaped who could adapt.

Presidents of both parties generally supported expanded trade from the 1980s through the early twenty-first century. Opposition later grew across ideological lines, contributing to protectionism and distrust of institutions and elites.

The strongest APUSH interpretation treats globalization as both continuity and acceleration. Americans had always been connected to world systems, but modern policy and technology made borders less restrictive for goods, money, and information than for many people. Its central question is how a national democracy can govern economic forces organized across jurisdictions.
