> The **War on Terror** was the open-ended American military, intelligence, law-enforcement, and diplomatic campaign launched after the [[September 11 Attacks]] of 2001. The United States invaded Afghanistan to destroy al-Qaeda and remove the Taliban, later invaded Iraq on claims involving weapons and security, expanded surveillance and executive authority, and pursued terrorist networks across multiple countries. Unlike a conventional war against one state, it defined the enemy through tactics and organizations, making its geographic and temporal boundaries uncertain. For APUSH, the campaign transformed foreign policy and civil liberties while raising enduring questions about presidential power, security, discrimination, and the limits of military force.

## APUSH Significance

The War on Terror redirected American power after the [[Cold War]]:

1. **Nonstate terrorism became the central security framework.** Policymakers reorganized institutions around preventing another mass attack.
2. **Presidential war powers expanded.** Broad congressional authorization supported operations far beyond Afghanistan.
3. **Domestic surveillance and policing changed.** The [[USA PATRIOT Act]] increased investigative powers and information sharing.
4. **Long wars exposed limits of military transformation.** Regime removal proved easier than building stable political orders.
5. **Security policy affected identity and rights.** Muslim, Arab, Sikh, and South Asian communities faced suspicion, profiling, and hate violence.

The War on Terror was not one war with a clear surrender. It was a policy framework encompassing distinct conflicts, covert actions, detention, policing, and diplomacy.

## September 11 and the Initial Response

Al-Qaeda operatives hijacked four airplanes on September 11, 2001, striking the World Trade Center and Pentagon while passengers resisted aboard a fourth plane. Nearly three thousand people were killed.

Congress enacted an Authorization for Use of Military Force against those responsible and their associated supporters. [[NATO]] invoked collective defense, and a broad international coalition initially supported action against al-Qaeda.

The George W. Bush administration framed the attacks as war rather than solely crime. The [[Bush Doctrine]] emphasized preventive action against emerging threats, opposition to states supporting terrorism, and willingness to act unilaterally.

## Afghanistan

The [[War in Afghanistan]] began in October 2001 after the Taliban refused American demands concerning al-Qaeda. U.S.-supported forces quickly removed the Taliban government, but al-Qaeda leaders escaped and the conflict evolved into counterinsurgency and state building.

The Taliban regrouped, aided by sanctuary, weak governance, corruption, civilian casualties, and local grievances. American objectives expanded from counterterrorism to constructing security forces and political institutions.

Successive administrations increased or reduced troops without producing a durable settlement. U.S. forces withdrew in 2021, and the Taliban rapidly returned to power. The outcome demonstrated the difficulty of translating military superiority into legitimate institutions.

## Iraq and Preventive War

The United States invaded Iraq in 2003, arguing that Saddam Hussein's government possessed weapons of mass destruction, violated international obligations, and might support terrorism. No active stockpiles of the claimed kind were found.

American forces removed Hussein, but occupation decisions and institutional collapse contributed to insurgency and sectarian war. Abuses at Abu Ghraib damaged American credibility. A later troop “surge” and changes in Iraqi politics reduced violence without resolving underlying divisions.

The [[Iraq War]] showed how 9/11 fears enabled a conflict not directly caused by the attacks. It weakened public trust and shaped later skepticism toward intelligence and intervention.

## Security State and Civil Liberties

The [[Department of Homeland Security]] combined agencies responsible for borders, transportation, emergencies, and domestic protection. Airport security, watchlists, financial monitoring, and intelligence sharing expanded.

The PATRIOT Act broadened surveillance and investigative authority. The government detained prisoners at Guantánamo Bay, used military commissions, and employed interrogation practices widely condemned as torture. Courts placed some limits on indefinite detention and executive claims.

These policies revived recurring wartime tensions between security and rights. Because enforcement often focused on Muslim and immigrant communities, debates also involved religion, race, and [[Citizenship]].

## Consequences and Interpretation

Drone strikes and special operations extended counterterrorism beyond declared battlefields. The killing of Osama bin Laden in 2011 was an operational success, but terrorist organizations and ideologies persisted in changing forms.

The wars cost lives, displaced civilians, injured veterans, and required vast public spending. They also strained alliances and affected American standing abroad. Domestically, support after September 11 gave way to division over Iraq, surveillance, refugees, and Islamophobia.

The War on Terror demonstrates both the reach and limits of post-Cold War American power. Its open-ended authorization and dispersed enemies made accountability difficult, leaving unresolved how a constitutional democracy should define victory and constrain emergency authority.
