> The **Second Great Awakening** was a wave of Protestant revivalism that spread from the late eighteenth century through the antebellum era. Camp meetings, itinerant preachers, and organized revival techniques emphasized emotional conversion, individual moral choice, and the possibility that people could help perfect themselves and society. The movement expanded evangelical denominations, encouraged independent Black religious institutions, and supplied people, language, and organizational methods to [[Antebellum Reform]]. For APUSH, it is both a response to the disruption of [[Westward Expansion]] and the [[Market Revolution]] and a major cause of reform—though revivalism did not produce one unified political program.

## APUSH Significance

The Second Great Awakening matters because it connects religion to broader changes in early nineteenth-century society:

1. **Religious participation became more accessible.** Emotional preaching and personal conversion challenged older traditions that emphasized formal education, established churches, or predestination.
2. **Revivalism followed population movement.** Camp meetings and circuit riders reached communities beyond established eastern churches, especially in the South and West.
3. **Voluntary associations multiplied.** Converts formed missionary, Bible, tract, temperance, and reform societies that operated outside government.
4. **Religion encouraged social reform.** If individuals could choose salvation and overcome sin, many Protestants reasoned that society could also overcome [[Slavery|slavery]], alcohol abuse, inadequate education, and other moral problems.
5. **Religious growth remained divided by race, region, and doctrine.** Black churches created spaces for autonomy and resistance, while some white churches and ministers defended slavery.

In APUSH causation, the Awakening is best treated as an **organizational and moral engine** of reform, not as a claim that every reformer shared the same theology or goals.

## Revival and Religious Choice

The movement began amid the social instability of the early republic. Population growth, [[Migration|migration]], weak institutions on the frontier, and declining attachment to some established churches created opportunities for revivalists. Large outdoor camp meetings brought preaching, prayer, and conversion to dispersed communities. Methodists and Baptists expanded through itinerant ministers and relatively decentralized organizations.

Revival messages generally stressed that ordinary people could respond to grace and change their lives. This differed from stricter Calvinist teachings that emphasized predestination. The religious shift paralleled the more participatory culture associated with [[Jacksonian Democracy]]: both appealed to individual choice and challenged inherited authority, although neither erased hierarchy or exclusion.

The Awakening did not have a single leader or exact starting point. Early revivals were especially influential in Kentucky and Tennessee, while later revivals spread through towns connected by the [[Transportation Revolution]]. Charles Grandison Finney became known for carefully organized meetings and direct appeals for immediate conversion. Western New York, repeatedly reached by revival preachers, became known as the [[Burned-over District]].

## Networks of Print and Association

Revivalism became durable because it created institutions. Churches trained ministers, missionary societies sent preachers, and organizations distributed Bibles and religious tracts. Improvements in printing and transportation allowed ideas, speakers, and petitions to circulate across communities.

These voluntary associations were important in a nation with limited administrative government. Americans could coordinate national campaigns without waiting for federal action. The same methods—local chapters, traveling lecturers, newspapers, fundraising, petitions, and moral pledges—later supported [[Temperance Movement|temperance]], [[Abolitionism]], public education, prison and asylum reform, and women's rights.

This connection also ties religious change to the Market Revolution. Commercial networks helped revival and reform organizations expand, while the new middle class often embraced values of discipline, self-control, and improvement. Revivalism could criticize the disorder and inequality associated with economic change, yet its emphasis on individual effort could also resemble the era's celebration of self-making.

## From Conversion to Reform

Some revivalists embraced **perfectionism**, the belief that people and communities could move toward freedom from sin. Reformers applied that confidence to social institutions. Temperance advocates asked individuals to renounce alcohol and sought laws limiting its sale. Education reformers such as [[Horace Mann]] promoted public schools as a way to create disciplined republican citizens. [[Dorothea Dix]] appealed to legislatures to improve the treatment of people confined in prisons and asylums.

Abolitionists drew especially powerful arguments from evangelical ideas about sin and moral responsibility. [[William Lloyd Garrison]], Theodore Dwight Weld, the [[Grimké Sisters]], and many Black abolitionists demanded action against slavery, though abolitionists disagreed over [[Political Parties|political parties]], gradualism, immediate emancipation, and the Constitution.

Women were central to church and reform associations as fundraisers, petitioners, writers, and organizers. Their activism expanded women's public roles without automatically rejecting the [[Cult of Domesticity]]. Many justified public action as an extension of women's supposed moral responsibility. Conflict over women's right to speak and lead within abolitionism helped some activists move toward an independent [[Women's Rights|women's-rights movement]] and the [[Seneca Falls Convention]].

## Black Religious Life and the Problem of Slavery

Christianity could support opposing claims about slavery. White southern ministers often taught obedience and defended the institution, while enslaved and free Black Americans developed religious communities that emphasized deliverance, dignity, and collective survival. Independent Black churches such as the African Methodist Episcopal Church created leadership and organizational space outside white control.

The Awakening therefore did not automatically produce abolitionism. It supplied moral language that abolitionists used, but denominations divided over slavery, and religious authority could reinforce the existing social order. This is an important APUSH limit: a shared religious movement could deepen sectional disagreement rather than resolve it.

## Religious Diversity and New Movements

The era's religious experimentation extended beyond mainstream evangelical denominations. [[Mormonism]] emerged in western New York under Joseph Smith and built a new religious community, provoking hostility as its members migrated west. Other groups organized [[Utopian Communities]] that attempted to restructure property, family life, [[Gender Roles|gender roles]], or work.

## Religious Choice and Social Discipline

The Second Great Awakening should be distinguished from the [[First Great Awakening]]. The earlier movement occurred in the British colonies during the 1730s and 1740s and helped challenge established religious authority before the [[American Revolution]]. The second unfolded in an independent, expanding republic and became closely connected to mass voluntary reform.

Its long-term importance lies less in any single revival meeting than in the link it created between personal conversion and collective action. It democratized parts of Protestant practice, built national networks, widened some opportunities for women and Black religious leadership, and encouraged reform. Yet its effects remained shaped by race, region, denomination, and competing interpretations of moral responsibility.
