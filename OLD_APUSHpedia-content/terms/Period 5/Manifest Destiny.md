> **Manifest Destiny** was the 1840s belief that the United States possessed a providential mission and historical right to expand across North America. Journalist [[John O'Sullivan]] popularized the phrase, but its ideas drew on older settler expansion, republican mission, land hunger, and racial hierarchy. Supporters connected western settlement to liberty and economic opportunity; critics saw conquest, slavery's expansion, and unconstitutional war. The ideology helped justify Texas annexation, claims to Oregon, and the [[Mexican–American War]]. For APUSH, Manifest Destiny was not migration itself or a policy with one author. It was a flexible justification that made territorial growth appear inevitable while obscuring federal power, Native dispossession, and Mexican sovereignty.

## APUSH Significance

Manifest Destiny connected national identity to territorial conflict:

1. **Expansion appeared providential.** Supporters described continental growth as natural and divinely favored.
2. **Land promised white opportunity.** Farming, commerce, and political independence supported popular appeal.
3. **Race structured the ideology.** Claims of Anglo-American superiority justified rule over Native and Mexican peoples.
4. **Expansion reopened slavery.** Every acquisition raised whether enslaved labor would enter western territory.
5. **Opposition exposed limits.** Whigs, abolitionists, and others challenged war, executive power, and conquest.

Manifest Destiny differs from [[Westward Expansion]], the longer process of territorial acquisition and settlement. The phrase names one influential justification during a particular era.

## Earlier Foundations

The United States had expanded long before the phrase appeared. The [[Louisiana Purchase]], Native land cessions, [[Indian Removal]], and settlement beyond the Appalachians established a pattern of government-supported growth.

Republican ideology associated landownership with independent citizenship. Population growth, the [[Market Revolution]], and transportation increased demand for western farms, trade routes, ports, and resources.

These material interests interacted with belief. Expansionists presented American institutions and Protestant culture as superior, turning political choices into a story of inevitable progress.

## Texas and Oregon

American settlers entered Mexican Texas, where conflict developed over immigration, government, and slavery. After the Texas Revolution, the Republic of Texas sought annexation. Antislavery northerners feared that annexation would expand slaveholding power and provoke Mexico.

The 1844 election elevated expansion. James K. Polk supported [[Texas Annexation]] and American claims to the [[Oregon Territory]]. Congress annexed Texas in 1845, while the United States and Britain compromised on Oregon's boundary at the forty-ninth parallel.

The different outcomes show that Manifest Destiny did not require war in every case. Diplomacy settled Oregon, while disputed Texas borders contributed to war with Mexico.

## War and the Mexican Cession

Polk sent troops into territory between the Nueces River and Rio Grande claimed by both countries. After a clash, he declared that Mexico had shed American blood on American soil. Congress authorized war amid dissent.

United States victory produced the [[Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo]] and [[Mexican Cession]], including California and New Mexico. The acquisition fulfilled continental ambitions but immediately intensified the [[Sectional Crisis]].

The [[Wilmot Proviso]] proposed barring slavery from acquired territory. It failed, yet sectional voting demonstrated that expansion threatened party unity and the balance between free and slave states.

## Native Peoples and Mexican Residents

Expansion crossed inhabited land. Native nations faced treaties, warfare, reservations, declining access to resources, and settler violence. Communities negotiated and resisted, but population and military power increasingly favored the United States.

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo promised protections to Mexican residents, yet taxes, violence, legal expenses, and unfamiliar property systems contributed to land loss. Racial hierarchy limited the equal citizenship that formal annexation appeared to offer.

## Opposition and Interpretation

Many Democrats supported expansion, while Whigs often criticized Polk's war. Abraham Lincoln's Spot Resolutions questioned where fighting began. [[Henry David Thoreau]] connected resistance to the war and slavery in “Civil Disobedience.”

Manifest Destiny combined opportunity with coercion. Settlers gained land and the nation reached the Pacific, but those outcomes depended on conquest and unequal recognition of prior claims. Its analytical value lies in explaining how ideology converted interests and power into a claim of moral inevitability.
