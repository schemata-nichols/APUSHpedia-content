> **Reconstruction** was the struggle from 1865 to 1877 to restore former Confederate states, define freedom after slavery, and establish the political status of formerly enslaved people. Presidential and congressional plans competed over readmission and federal protection. The [[Reconstruction Amendments]] abolished slavery, established birthright citizenship and equal protection, and prohibited racial discrimination in men's voting. Black communities built schools, churches, families, and political institutions, while white-supremacist violence and economic dependence constrained freedom. For APUSH, Reconstruction was a major democratic transformation whose constitutional achievements endured, but whose retreat allowed the [[Jim Crow System]] and disfranchisement to develop.

## APUSH Significance

Reconstruction redefined the Union created by [[Civil War]] victory:

1. **Freedom became a national constitutional issue.** The federal government acquired new authority over citizenship and rights.
2. **Black political participation expanded dramatically.** Freedmen voted, held office, and shaped state constitutions.
3. **Land and labor remained unresolved.** Emancipation did not provide most freedpeople with economic independence.
4. **White resistance was organized and violent.** Terror sought to destroy biracial government and restore racial control.
5. **Federal commitment weakened.** Political compromise, court decisions, racism, and economic crisis narrowed enforcement.

Reconstruction was not simply rebuilding physical destruction. It was a conflict over the legal, political, economic, and social meaning of emancipation.

## Presidential Reconstruction

Lincoln's [[Ten Percent Plan]] offered lenient readmission while requiring acceptance of emancipation. Congressional Republicans proposed the stricter [[Wade–Davis Bill]], which Lincoln did not sign.

After Lincoln's assassination, Andrew Johnson rapidly restored southern governments and issued pardons. These governments enacted [[Black Codes]] restricting movement, labor, and civil rights. Former Confederate leaders returned to influence, convincing many Republicans that presidential policy had failed.

The [[Freedmen's Bureau]] supplied education, labor supervision, legal assistance, and relief with limited resources. Freedpeople sought family reunification, churches, schools, land, and control over their labor.

## Congressional Reconstruction

Congress passed the [[Civil Rights Act of 1866]] over Johnson's veto and proposed the Fourteenth Amendment. The amendment established national citizenship, due process, and equal protection while penalizing states that denied male voting.

The [[Reconstruction Acts]] divided the South into military districts and required new constitutions and ratification of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Fifteenth Amendment prohibited voting discrimination based on race, color, or previous servitude.

Johnson's obstruction led to his impeachment after conflict over removal of Secretary of War Edwin Stanton. The Senate fell one vote short of conviction, preserving the separation between impeachment and ordinary policy disagreement.

## Biracial Government

Black men voted in large numbers and served in local, state, and federal office. [[Hiram Revels]] and Blanche K. Bruce represented Mississippi in the Senate. Republican coalitions included freedpeople, white southern Republicans called [[Scalawags]], and northern migrants labeled [[Carpetbaggers]].

Reconstruction governments created public-school systems, rebuilt infrastructure, and expanded social services. Charges of corruption contained some truth but were applied selectively; corruption was widespread nationally, and the era's governments also enacted important democratic reforms.

Women remained excluded from voting, and debate over the Fifteenth Amendment divided former abolitionist allies in the women's-rights movement.

## Labor and Land

Freedpeople wanted land and autonomy, but broad redistribution did not occur. Most confiscated land returned to former owners. Without capital or property, many families entered [[Sharecropping]] or tenant arrangements.

Sharecropping allowed family labor outside direct gang supervision but tied tenants to landowners and merchants through credit and the crop-lien system. Debt and violence restricted mobility. Southern agriculture remained dependent on cotton, slowing diversification.

Freedom nevertheless changed daily life: people legalized marriages, searched for relatives, rejected corporal punishment, negotiated contracts, and built independent institutions.

## Violence and Retreat

The [[Ku Klux Klan]] and similar organizations used murder, assault, and intimidation against Black voters and white Republicans. Congress responded with the [[Enforcement Acts]], temporarily suppressing some terror through federal prosecution.

Supreme Court decisions narrowed federal protection, while northern commitment declined amid racism, fatigue, and the Panic of 1873. Southern [[Redeemers]] regained governments through elections, fraud, and violence.

The [[Compromise of 1877]] accompanied the withdrawal of remaining federal troops from southern statehouses. Reconstruction governments ended, although constitutional amendments remained.

The end date therefore marks a change in enforcement, not the immediate disappearance of Black voting or officeholding everywhere. Redeemer governments gradually constructed poll taxes, literacy tests, segregation statutes, and other restrictions, with the most systematic disfranchisement developing in the following decades. That chronology helps distinguish Reconstruction's retreat from the later consolidation of Jim Crow.

## Achievements, Retreat, and Legacy

Reconstruction did not fail because Black citizenship was inherently unworkable. It was overthrown through organized violence and abandonment of enforcement. Its achievements—public institutions, political participation, and constitutional rights—were substantial.

Its retreat enabled segregation and disfranchisement, but the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments later provided foundations for the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]]. Reconstruction was therefore both an unfinished revolution and a source of enduring constitutional change.
