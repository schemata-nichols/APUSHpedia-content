> The **Mexican–American War** was fought from 1846 to 1848 after the United States annexed Texas and disputed Mexico's northern boundary. President James K. Polk pursued California and New Mexico as part of [[Manifest Destiny]], sent troops into contested territory, and obtained a declaration of war after a clash. American forces invaded northern Mexico, California, and Mexico City. The [[Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo]] transferred an enormous [[Mexican Cession]] to the United States. For APUSH, victory completed continental expansion but destabilized national politics: the question of slavery in the new territories intensified the [[Sectional Crisis]] and helped break the party system.

## APUSH Significance

The war connected foreign policy to domestic crisis:

1. **Texas annexation created an immediate dispute.** Mexico rejected both annexation and the American claim to the Rio Grande boundary.
2. **Polk pursued expansion deliberately.** California, New Mexico, and Pacific access formed part of his program.
3. **American military advantages produced victory.** Resources, naval power, and invasions overcame determined Mexican resistance.
4. **The cession transformed continental borders.** The United States acquired present-day California and much of the Southwest.
5. **[[Slavery]] dominated the consequences.** The [[Wilmot Proviso]] made the territories a sectional issue before the war ended.

The war should be distinguished from the earlier Texas Revolution. Texas fought for independence from Mexico in 1835–1836; the United States and Mexico fought a decade later.

## Causes

Mexico had never accepted the independence or annexation of Texas. Texas claimed the Rio Grande as its boundary, while Mexico treated the Nueces River farther north as the border. The United States inherited this dispute through [[Texas Annexation]] in 1845.

Polk also sought California and New Mexico and attempted to purchase them. Mexico rejected the mission. Polk then ordered Zachary Taylor's forces into the disputed zone between the rivers.

Mexican troops attacked an American patrol in April 1846. Polk told Congress that Mexico had shed American blood on American soil. Congress approved war, although critics argued that the administration had provoked the clash.

## Course of the War

Taylor won victories in northern Mexico, while Stephen Kearny occupied New Mexico and moved toward California. American settlers and naval forces helped seize California during the Bear Flag Revolt.

General Winfield Scott conducted an amphibious landing at Veracruz and marched inland. His army captured Mexico City in September 1847. Mexico faced political instability and limited resources but resisted invasion rather than simply surrendering territory.

The war brought significant casualties from disease as well as combat. Irish immigrant deserters in the Saint Patrick's Battalion fought for Mexico, reflecting religious prejudice and harsh treatment within the American army.

## Opposition at Home

Democratic expansionists generally supported the war. Many Whigs questioned Polk's account and feared executive manipulation. Abraham Lincoln introduced the Spot Resolutions demanding identification of the exact location where blood had been shed.

[[Abolitionism|Abolitionists]] argued that slaveholders sought new territory. [[Henry David Thoreau]]'s refusal to pay a tax became associated with opposition to both war and slavery. Opposition was substantial but did not prevent military funding.

## Treaty and Territorial Change

The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ended the war in 1848. Mexico recognized the Rio Grande border and ceded California and New Mexico; the United States paid $15 million and assumed certain claims.

The treaty promised citizenship and property protections to Mexican residents. In practice, legal procedures, taxes, violence, and racial discrimination contributed to extensive land loss and unequal incorporation.

The later [[Gadsden Purchase]] adjusted the southern boundary and secured land useful for a railroad route, but the 1848 cession supplied the core southwestern acquisition.

## Sectional Consequences

The Wilmot Proviso proposed prohibiting slavery in territory gained from Mexico. It repeatedly passed the House but failed in the Senate. Its sectional voting weakened national party unity.

Debate produced the [[Free Soil Party]], the [[Compromise of 1850]], and continuing conflict over popular sovereignty. Expansion meant to strengthen the nation instead exposed incompatible views of slavery, free labor, and federal authority.

## Expansion, Conquest, and Sectional Crisis

The Mexican–American War was both a military victory and a war of territorial conquest. Supporters saw national growth and Pacific opportunity; critics identified aggression and expansion of slaveholding power.

Its greatest APUSH significance lies in unintended domestic consequences. Territorial success reopened a constitutional and political dispute that compromise could postpone but not resolve, moving the country closer to the [[Civil War]].
