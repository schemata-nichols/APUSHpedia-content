> The **African American Civil Rights Movement** was the broad struggle, especially visible from the 1940s through the 1960s, to dismantle the [[Jim Crow System]], secure voting and equal protection, and challenge racial inequality across the United States. Black communities combined litigation, boycotts, direct action, voter registration, institution building, armed self-defense, and federal lobbying. National organizations and charismatic leaders mattered, but local organizers and ordinary participants sustained the movement. For APUSH, the movement transformed law and democratic participation while revealing that ending formal segregation did not automatically eliminate economic inequality, residential discrimination, police violence, or unequal political power.

## APUSH Significance

The movement completed major unfinished work of [[Reconstruction]]:

1. **Constitutional rights gained federal enforcement.** Courts, Congress, and presidents acted against state segregation and disfranchisement.
2. **Mass direct action changed national politics.** Boycotts, sit-ins, marches, and campaigns exposed repression and created pressure for legislation.
3. **Black organizing drove change.** Federal action generally followed sustained local activism rather than initiating it.
4. **Strategy and goals were diverse.** Integration, nonviolence, self-defense, economic justice, and [[Black Power Movement|Black Power]] coexisted and sometimes conflicted.
5. **The movement reshaped other activism.** Its tactics and rights language influenced women's, Chicano, Native, disability, and gay liberation movements.

The movement was not confined to the South or to 1954–1968. That period produced landmark victories within a much longer Black freedom struggle.

## Wartime Foundations and Legal Challenge

The [[Great Migration]] created Black voting blocs and institutions in northern and western cities. During [[World War II]], the [[Double V Campaign]] linked victory over fascism abroad to victory over racism at home. A threatened march pushed Franklin Roosevelt to prohibit discrimination in defense employment.

Black veterans returned determined to claim rights they had defended overseas. The [[Cold War]] made segregation an international embarrassment, although anticommunism also targeted activists.

The NAACP pursued a long legal campaign against unequal education. In [[Brown v. Board of Education]], the Supreme Court ruled segregated public schools inherently unequal. The decision overturned the constitutional foundation of school segregation but depended on local action and federal enforcement.

## Mass Protest and Nonviolent Direct Action

The [[Montgomery Bus Boycott]] began after Rosa Parks's arrest and relied on a year of community organization. It elevated [[Martin Luther King Jr.]] and helped inspire the [[Southern Christian Leadership Conference]], which coordinated church-based campaigns.

Students launched the [[Sit-In Movement]] in 1960 and formed the [[Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee]]. The [[Freedom Rides]] tested federal desegregation orders on interstate travel, facing mob violence that forced federal intervention.

Nonviolent direct action exposed the violence required to maintain segregation. In the [[Birmingham Campaign]], police attacks on demonstrators produced national outrage. The [[March on Washington]] joined civil rights to jobs and economic justice, while the [[Selma Campaign]] dramatized the denial of Black voting rights.

## Federal Legislation and Enforcement

The [[Civil Rights Act of 1964]] prohibited segregation in public accommodations and discrimination in employment while strengthening federal enforcement. The [[Voting Rights Act of 1965]] suspended discriminatory tests and authorized federal oversight in jurisdictions with histories of exclusion.

Lyndon Johnson's [[Great Society]] included antipoverty, education, and health programs that addressed some economic dimensions of inequality. Federal courts and agencies became central protectors of rights, altering the balance of [[Federalism]].

These laws produced dramatic gains in registration, officeholding, and access. Yet white resistance, unequal implementation, and later court decisions limited their reach.

## Black Power and Northern Inequality

Civil-rights problems were national. Residential segregation, redlining, school inequality, job discrimination, and police abuse shaped northern and western cities. Urban uprisings in the 1960s reflected anger over conditions that public-accommodations laws did not resolve.

[[Malcolm X]] criticized integrationist assumptions and emphasized Black dignity and self-determination. SNCC and the [[Black Panther Party]] increasingly used Black Power language, while the Panthers combined armed observation of police with community programs.

This shift was not simply a rejection of earlier activism. It reflected frustration with violence, slow economic change, and white control over movement priorities. Martin Luther King Jr. also increasingly addressed poverty, housing, labor, and the [[Vietnam War]].

## Limits and Legacy

The movement faced surveillance and disruption, including FBI campaigns against King and Black organizations. Activists were murdered, jailed, fired, and assaulted. Women performed essential organizing while often receiving less public recognition than male leaders.

Legal victories dismantled explicit Jim Crow and transformed democratic participation, but wealth gaps and spatial inequality persisted. Policies such as [[Affirmative Action]] attempted to address the effects of past exclusion and generated lasting political conflict.

The movement demonstrates that constitutional change requires organized citizens, institutions, and enforcement. Its broadest legacy is not only a set of laws but a transformed understanding that unequal outcomes rooted in public and private power are legitimate subjects of national democratic action.
