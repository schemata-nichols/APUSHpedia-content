> The **Vietnam War** was the conflict in which the United States supported South Vietnam against communist North Vietnam and the southern National Liberation Front, escalating from advisers and aid in the 1950s to large-scale American combat after 1965. Guided by [[Containment]] and the [[Domino Theory]], officials treated Vietnamese communism primarily as part of the [[Cold War]], while Vietnamese actors also fought over nationalism, reunification, and social revolution. The war produced enormous destruction, deepened domestic distrust, energized the [[Antiwar Movement]], and ended with communist victory in 1975. For APUSH, it reveals limits of presidential power, technological warfare, and American global influence.

## APUSH Significance

Vietnam reshaped foreign policy and domestic politics:

1. **Containment became a costly ground war.** Successive presidents expanded commitments rather than accept perceived strategic defeat.
2. **Presidents acted with broad discretion.** Congress ceded authority through the [[Gulf of Tonkin Resolution]] before later reasserting a role.
3. **Military superiority did not ensure political victory.** Firepower could not create a stable, legitimate South Vietnamese state.
4. **The war divided American society.** The draft, casualties, television coverage, and official credibility fueled protest.
5. **Defeat produced lasting caution.** The “Vietnam syndrome” shaped later debate over intervention and public support.

The war was both a Cold War conflict and a Vietnamese struggle shaped by colonialism. Ignoring either dimension produces an incomplete explanation.

## Origins and Escalation

Vietnamese nationalists under Ho Chi Minh fought French colonial rule after [[World War II]]. The United States financed much of France's war because officials feared communist expansion. After French defeat in 1954, agreements temporarily divided Vietnam pending elections that were never held.

The Eisenhower administration supported Ngo Dinh Diem's government in South Vietnam. Diem's repression and limited legitimacy strengthened opposition, and he was overthrown and killed in a 1963 coup with American knowledge.

After reported attacks in the Gulf of Tonkin, Congress gave Lyndon Johnson broad authority to use force. Johnson began sustained bombing and deployed combat troops in 1965, seeking to avoid both defeat and a wider war with China or the Soviet Union.

## Fighting the War

The United States used search-and-destroy missions, airpower, artillery, chemical defoliants, and a large logistical system. North Vietnam and the National Liberation Front relied on political organization, guerrilla tactics, conventional forces, and supply routes through Laos and Cambodia.

American commanders measured progress through enemy casualties, but body counts did not demonstrate control or legitimacy. Bombing and forced relocation displaced civilians, while atrocities such as My Lai exposed the moral costs of a war in which combatants and civilians were difficult to distinguish.

The draft distributed burdens unequally. College deferments and local boards favored many middle-class men, while working-class and minority servicemembers were disproportionately exposed during important phases.

## Tet and the Credibility Gap

The [[Tet Offensive]] of 1968 was a military setback for communist forces but a political turning point in the United States. Its scale contradicted optimistic official claims that victory was near.

Television brought combat and protest into American homes, though reporting did not simply turn the public against the war. Casualties, unclear goals, repeated escalation, and misleading statements created a broader “credibility gap.”

Johnson restricted bombing and declined to seek reelection. The war contributed to Democratic division, urban unrest, and the turbulent election of 1968.

## Protest, Repression, and Polarization

Students, clergy, veterans, civil-rights activists, and others opposed the war for moral, strategic, or political reasons. [[Martin Luther King Jr.]] connected militarism abroad to poverty and racism at home. Some activists resisted the draft; others demanded negotiation or immediate withdrawal.

Supporters of the war and [[Richard Nixon]]'s [[Silent Majority]] criticized protesters as unpatriotic. The [[Kent State Shootings]], in which National Guard troops killed four students during protests against the Cambodia expansion, intensified division.

The leaked [[Pentagon Papers]] showed that administrations had misled the public about policy and prospects. Their publication reinforced distrust that later deepened during the [[Watergate Scandal]].

## Vietnamization and Defeat

Nixon pursued [[Vietnamization]], withdrawing American ground troops while expanding and equipping South Vietnamese forces. He also widened bombing into Cambodia and Laos while negotiating with North Vietnam.

The 1973 Paris agreement ended direct American combat and returned prisoners, but fighting continued. Congress passed the [[War Powers Resolution]] in an effort to limit unilateral presidential deployments. North Vietnamese forces captured Saigon in 1975.

The war killed millions of Vietnamese people and more than 58,000 Americans, devastated landscapes, and displaced refugees. Veterans faced physical injury, trauma, and an often-conflicted public reception.

Vietnam did not end American intervention, but it made credibility, objectives, exit strategy, and congressional authorization central questions. It also demonstrated how viewing local conflicts only through superpower rivalry could obscure nationalism and weaken policy.
