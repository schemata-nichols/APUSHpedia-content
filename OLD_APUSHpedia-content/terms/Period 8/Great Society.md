> The **Great Society** was [[Lyndon B. Johnson]]'s domestic reform program, enacted mainly from 1964 through 1966, that used federal power to reduce poverty, expand health care and education, protect civil rights, improve cities and the environment, and support arts and public broadcasting. Built on [[New Deal]] liberalism and enabled by Democratic congressional majorities, it created Medicare, Medicaid, federal education aid, antipoverty programs, and landmark civil-rights legislation. For APUSH, the Great Society greatly expanded social citizenship and federal responsibility, yet poverty persisted, implementation was unequal, urban conflict grew, and the [[Vietnam War]] diverted money and political attention.

## APUSH Significance

The Great Society represented the high point of postwar liberal reform:

1. **Health care became a federal entitlement.** Medicare and Medicaid expanded access for older and low-income Americans.
2. **The federal government entered education more directly.** Aid targeted disadvantaged schools, college access, and early childhood.
3. **Civil rights gained national enforcement.** Landmark laws attacked segregation and disfranchisement.
4. **Antipoverty programs encouraged both services and participation.** Community action sometimes challenged local political control.
5. **The program provoked a conservative reaction.** Critics attacked cost, bureaucracy, federal authority, and perceived dependency.

The Great Society was broader than the “War on Poverty.” Poverty policy was one part of a larger effort to use abundance for public opportunity.

## Political Context

Johnson became president after John F. Kennedy's assassination and used his legislative skill to pass stalled proposals. His landslide victory in 1964 and large Democratic congressional majorities created an unusual opening for reform.

The [[Postwar Economic Boom]] encouraged confidence that federal resources could address persistent deprivation. Michael Harrington's writing and media coverage made poverty visible within an affluent society, while the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] forced racial inequality onto the national agenda.

Johnson presented reform as an expansion of opportunity rather than a rejection of capitalism. The goal was to provide education, health, legal rights, and community resources that would allow citizens to participate more fully.

## War on Poverty and Education

The Economic Opportunity Act created Job Corps, VISTA, Head Start, legal services, and Community Action Programs. “Maximum feasible participation” gave poor residents a voice in some local programs, provoking conflict with mayors and established organizations.

The Elementary and Secondary Education Act directed federal funds toward schools serving low-income children while navigating resistance to national control of curricula. Higher education legislation expanded grants, loans, and institutional support.

Programs reduced some hardship and created durable services, but they lacked resources sufficient to restructure labor markets, housing, and concentrated poverty. Policymakers sometimes treated poverty as a deficit in individuals rather than the product of unequal institutions.

## Health, Welfare, and Quality of Life

The 1965 Social Security amendments created Medicare for older Americans and Medicaid for eligible low-income people. These programs transformed access to health care and became among the Great Society's most durable achievements.

Food assistance expanded, and immigration reform through the [[Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965]] abolished the national-origins quota system. Environmental, consumer, highway-safety, arts, and public-broadcasting laws widened the meaning of federal social policy.

These initiatives built on New Deal institutions but extended government into new areas of daily life. They also deepened partnerships among Washington, states, localities, nonprofits, and private providers.

## [[Civil Rights]] and Federalism

The [[Civil Rights Act of 1964]] prohibited discrimination in public accommodations and employment. The [[Voting Rights Act of 1965]] authorized strong federal action against disfranchisement. Together they attacked the legal structure of Jim Crow more effectively than earlier civil-rights measures.

Federal grants and enforcement shifted power away from states that had protected segregation. This expansion of [[Federalism|federal authority]] fulfilled [[Reconstruction]]-era constitutional promises but fueled arguments about local control.

Johnson also issued affirmative-action requirements for federal contractors. Civil-rights victories expanded Black political participation, yet northern housing segregation, unemployment, and police conflict remained.

## Limits, Vietnam, and Backlash

Urban uprisings exposed conditions untouched by formal desegregation. White resistance to housing integration, school changes, taxes, and welfare contributed to the [[Southern Strategy]] and a broader conservative movement.

Escalation in Vietnam consumed budgetary resources and divided liberal supporters. Inflationary pressure and Johnson's political decline narrowed possibilities for further domestic reform.

Critics on the right portrayed programs as wasteful and dependency-producing; critics on the left argued that they managed inequality without redistributing power. Some initiatives were reduced or reorganized, but Medicare, Medicaid, education aid, civil-rights enforcement, and environmental regulation endured.

The Great Society demonstrates both the capacity and limits of federal reform. It substantially widened access and rights, but national programs operated within labor, housing, and racial systems they could not quickly transform.
