> The **Postwar Economic Boom** was the broad expansion of American production, income, consumption, and middle-class employment from the end of [[World War II]] through the 1960s. Wartime industrial capacity, pent-up demand, federal spending, union wages, the [[GI Bill]], housing construction, population growth, and U.S. dominance in world markets supported prosperity. Automobiles, suburbs, appliances, and television became symbols of abundance, but access was unequal: discrimination excluded many Black families from housing wealth, women faced restricted employment, and poverty persisted in cities and rural areas. For APUSH, the boom joined private growth to public policy while reshaping geography, family life, and politics.

## APUSH Significance

Postwar prosperity changed American society and expectations:

1. **Federal policy supported private consumption.** Veteran benefits, mortgages, highways, and defense spending stimulated markets.
2. **A mass middle class expanded.** Rising productivity and union contracts increased purchasing power for many wage earners.
3. **[[Suburbanization]] reorganized metropolitan life.** Housing and highways shifted population, taxes, and commerce outward.
4. **Growth was racially unequal.** Segregated lending and local discrimination restricted access to homes, schools, and accumulated wealth.
5. **Abundance supported liberal reform and conservative critique.** Prosperity financed the [[Great Society]] while suburban politics often resisted taxation and integration.

The boom was not simply a triumph of an unregulated market. Public spending and rules helped determine where investment flowed and who benefited.

## Foundations of Growth

World War II mobilization ended the [[Great Depression]], modernized factories, and trained millions of workers. After the war, households released savings and pent-up demand for homes, cars, and appliances. American industries faced limited foreign competition because European and Asian economies had suffered wartime destruction.

The GI Bill funded veterans' education, unemployment support, and loan guarantees. It helped millions gain credentials and homes, although discriminatory administration and segregated institutions limited Black veterans' access.

The federal government sustained demand through defense spending, research, Social Security, and infrastructure. The [[Cold War]] directed military contracts toward aerospace, electronics, and growing southern and western regions.

## Work, Wages, and [[Consumer Culture]]

Large industrial unions negotiated wages, pensions, and health benefits, sharing some productivity gains with workers. Stable corporate employment and progressive taxation supported broad income growth, although agricultural, domestic, and nonunion workers received fewer protections.

Credit, advertising, and expanding retail markets promoted homes, automobiles, televisions, and appliances. [[Television Culture]] created national entertainment and a powerful advertising medium. The [[Baby Boom]] increased demand for houses, schools, consumer goods, and family services.

The idealized middle-class household assumed a male breadwinner and female homemaker. In practice, many women worked for wages, especially women of color, while occupational discrimination and domestic expectations constrained choices.

## Suburbs, Highways, and Regional Change

Mass-produced developments such as [[Levittown]] offered affordable homes to many white families. Federal mortgage guarantees favored new suburban construction and often reinforced racially restrictive lending. Homeownership became a major source of family wealth distributed unequally by race.

The [[Interstate Highway System]] facilitated commuting, trucking, tourism, and suburban commerce. Highway construction also displaced urban neighborhoods, often Black or Latino, and weakened some city tax bases.

Defense investment, air conditioning, oil, and migration supported the [[Sun Belt]]. Population and political influence shifted toward the South and West, contributing to later conservative electoral strength.

## Poverty and Racial Inequality

Not all Americans shared prosperity. Mechanization displaced farmworkers; central cities lost jobs and investment; Appalachia and other rural regions experienced persistent poverty. Native reservations and migrant-labor communities received limited public resources.

Redlining, restrictive practices, violence, and exclusionary zoning confined many Black families to neighborhoods with lower property investment and overcrowded schools. Urban segregation was produced through public and private policy, not merely personal preference.

The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] challenged employment, housing, education, and public discrimination. Mexican American, Native American, and Puerto Rican activists similarly exposed exclusions hidden by national narratives of abundance.

## Slowing Growth and Legacy

By the 1970s, international competition, oil shocks, productivity slowdown, and [[Stagflation]] weakened the postwar pattern. [[Deindustrialization]] harmed communities built around unionized manufacturing, while growth shifted toward services, technology, and finance.

The boom created enduring infrastructure, educational access, consumer expectations, and household wealth. It also locked in metropolitan sprawl and racial wealth disparities.

For APUSH, postwar prosperity illustrates how economic growth can expand opportunity while reproducing inequality. The crucial analytical question is not simply whether living standards rose, but how policy distributed the security, property, and mobility that growth made possible.
