> **Democracy** is government in which political authority ultimately rests with the people, exercised in the United States mainly through elections, representation, parties, law, and organized participation. American democracy expanded from a republic with restricted suffrage into a mass political system with nearly universal adult [[Citizenship|citizenship]], but that development was neither steady nor complete. Property, race, sex, Native status, [[Immigration|immigration]], age, violence, and election administration have determined who could participate effectively. Institutions designed to restrain majority power have also shaped outcomes. For APUSH, democracy is best studied as both an ideal and a contested system: participation widened through struggle while political equality repeatedly coexisted with [[Slavery|slavery]], dispossession, [[Segregation|segregation]], and unequal influence.

## APUSH Significance

Democracy connects changes in voting to broader questions of institutions and power:

1. **The founding created representative government, not universal rule.** Constitutions rested on [[Popular Sovereignty|popular sovereignty]] while limiting direct participation and leaving suffrage mainly to states.
2. **Mass politics expanded unevenly.** Property restrictions fell for white men as racial restrictions, party organization, and majority rule became more powerful.
3. **Constitutional change widened formal participation.** Reconstruction, women's suffrage, direct election of senators, and the voting-rights era altered who could vote and how officials were selected.
4. **Participation extends beyond elections.** Petitions, conventions, unions, [[Social Movements|social movements]], protest, journalism, and courts allow people to influence government.
5. **Political equality does not eliminate unequal power.** Wealth, violence, districting, administration, and access to information shape whose preferences become policy.

The key distinction is between **democracy as majority participation** and **constitutional democracy**, which also protects rights and limits what majorities and officials may do.

## Republican Foundations

Colonial assemblies and local governments gave some settlers experience with self-rule, but participation varied by colony and was usually limited by property, sex, race, religion, and status. The [[American Revolution]] attacked government without representation and grounded legitimate authority in consent. State constitutions and the federal Constitution then created elected institutions within a [[Republicanism|republic]].

The framers distrusted both concentrated power and unchecked popular majorities. Separation of powers, [[Federalism|federalism]], an indirectly elected Senate, the [[Electoral College]], and an appointed judiciary filtered public choice. The Constitution offered no national right to vote and protected compromises with slavery. Democratic principles therefore existed inside institutions that limited participation.

The first party conflict made opposition politics legitimate despite founders' suspicion of factions. Competitive elections, partisan newspapers, and peaceful transfer of power after the [[Election of 1800]] helped establish that organized disagreement could occur within the constitutional system.

## Mass Politics and Exclusion

During the early nineteenth century, most states removed property requirements for white men. [[Jacksonian Democracy]] built mass parties, nominating conventions, rallies, patronage, and partisan newspapers. Voter participation increased, and politicians presented themselves as representatives of ordinary citizens rather than a governing elite.

This democratization had firm boundaries. Women remained excluded, some states withdrew [[Voting Rights|voting rights]] from free Black men, and Native nations faced removal as white electorates demanded land. The celebration of majority rule could strengthen a racialized political community rather than produce universal equality.

Reformers used democratic methods outside elections. Abolitionists flooded Congress with petitions; women organized conventions; workers formed parties and unions; and voluntary associations mobilized public opinion. Such activity widened public life even when participants could not vote.

## [[Civil War]], Reconstruction, and Disfranchisement

The destruction of slavery forced a reconsideration of democracy and citizenship. During [[Reconstruction]], the [[Reconstruction Amendments|Fifteenth Amendment]] prohibited racial discrimination in men's voting, and Black voters helped create biracial state governments across the South. Those governments expanded public education and civil institutions while operating amid intense political conflict.

White-supremacist violence and the retreat of federal enforcement destroyed much of this democracy. Southern states used poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, white primaries, and intimidation to disfranchise Black citizens and many poor white citizens. The Supreme Court often permitted restrictions presented as formally race-neutral.

This reversal shows why constitutional language alone cannot sustain democratic participation. Ballots require registration access, physical safety, enforceable law, and institutions willing to protect political opposition.

## Progressive and [[New Deal]] Democracy

Progressive reformers argued that industrial corporations, party machines, and corruption had weakened popular government. Initiatives, referenda, recalls, direct primaries, and the [[Seventeenth Amendment]] sought more direct control. These mechanisms could broaden participation, but Progressive democracy coexisted with segregation, immigration restriction, and coercive assimilation.

The [[Women's Suffrage Movement|Nineteenth Amendment]] prohibited voting discrimination based on sex after decades of organization by women, though southern disfranchisement and barriers to Native and Asian citizenship limited its reach. The New Deal later expanded expectations that democratic government should provide economic security and regulate private power. It also built a durable electoral coalition without dismantling Jim Crow.

## Voting Rights and Modern Participation

The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] placed political participation at the center of freedom. The Twenty-Fourth Amendment prohibited poll taxes in federal elections, and the [[Voting Rights Act of 1965]] authorized strong federal action against discriminatory voting rules. Registration and Black officeholding rose significantly where exclusion had been most systematic.

The Twenty-Sixth Amendment lowered the voting age to eighteen during the Vietnam era. Other movements expanded participation through community organizing, litigation, protest, and campaigns for representation. Television and later digital media changed how citizens received information and how candidates mobilized supporters.

Modern disputes over campaign finance, district boundaries, voter identification, registration, primary rules, and the Electoral College continue older questions about political equality. Expanding the electorate does not settle how votes are translated into power.

## Participation and Exclusion

American democracy has changed through pressure from people whom existing institutions excluded or underrepresented. Its history includes genuine expansion: far more adults possess formal political rights than at the founding. It also includes contraction, especially when violence or law removed rights previously exercised.

Democracy should therefore be measured through more than election frequency. The strongest analysis asks who may participate, whether choices are meaningful, how institutions aggregate those choices, what rights protect minorities, and whether unequal social and economic power prevents formal equality from becoming effective self-government.
