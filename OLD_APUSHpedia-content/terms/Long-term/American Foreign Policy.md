> **American Foreign Policy** consists of the strategies through which the United States has pursued security, trade, territory, influence, and ideological goals in its relations with other nations. It developed from the early republic's effort to preserve independence, through continental expansion and overseas empire, to participation in world wars and leadership of a global Cold War alliance system. Policy has never followed a simple path from isolation to [[Internationalism|internationalism]]: even when avoiding European military commitments, Americans sought commerce, territory, and regional power. For APUSH, foreign policy is best understood through changing capabilities and recurring tensions between ideals and interests, unilateral action and cooperation, and public reluctance and sustained international involvement.

## APUSH Significance

Foreign policy links domestic development to the nation's changing position in the world:

1. **Security shaped early policy.** A weak republic tried to avoid dependence on stronger European powers while protecting trade and sovereignty.
2. **Expansion joined diplomacy to conquest.** Treaties, purchases, warfare, and Native dispossession extended United States control across North America.
3. **Economic interests encouraged overseas influence.** Access to markets, investment, resources, and sea routes became important motives for imperial and diplomatic action.
4. **World wars changed national power.** Industrial capacity and military mobilization helped turn the United States into a leading global power.
5. **Ideology and strategy interacted.** Claims about liberty, [[Democracy|democracy]], anticommunism, and human rights could inspire policy, justify strategic interests, or expose contradictions at home.

The useful distinction is that avoiding permanent alliances was never the same as withdrawing from the world. Trade, expansion, and intervention remained central even during eras commonly labeled [[Isolationism|isolationist]].

## Independence and Neutrality

The Revolution depended on a [[French Alliance|French alliance]], but the new republic lacked the military and financial strength to compete freely with European empires. President Washington's neutrality policy during war between Britain and revolutionary France sought time for the United States to consolidate. [[Washington's Farewell Address]] warned especially against permanent political attachments, not against diplomacy or commerce.

The early republic defended neutral shipping while trading broadly. British impressment and restrictions on American commerce contributed to the [[War of 1812]]. The war produced no decisive territorial settlement, but survival strengthened nationalism and reduced dependence on Europe. The [[Monroe Doctrine]] later warned European powers against new colonization in the Western Hemisphere, even though British naval strength initially made that claim more credible than American force did.

These policies combined principle with practical weakness. Neutrality protected national independence, while commerce connected the United States to Atlantic conflict.

## Continental Expansion

Foreign policy became an instrument of territorial growth. The [[Louisiana Purchase]] doubled the country's claimed territory through diplomacy, while the [[Adams–Onís Treaty]] secured Florida and defined a western boundary. In the 1840s, [[Manifest Destiny]] joined expansion to claims about providence, racial superiority, and democratic development.

The annexation of Texas and the [[Mexican–American War]] produced the [[Mexican Cession]], extending the United States to the Pacific. Expansion increased agricultural and commercial opportunity for settlers, but it also displaced Native and Mexican communities and reopened the dispute over [[Slavery|slavery]]'s expansion. Foreign policy therefore fed directly into the [[Sectional Crisis]].

After the [[Civil War]], policymakers acquired Alaska, pursued Pacific trade, and increasingly connected commercial growth to naval power. Continental expansion did not end foreign ambition; it supplied resources and confidence for a larger international role.

## Empire and Progressive-Era Power

The [[Spanish–American War]] marked a major assertion of overseas power. The United States annexed Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines, established influence over Cuba, and fought Filipino independence forces. Supporters described expansion as strategic, commercial, and civilizing. Anti-imperialists argued that ruling people without their consent violated republican principles.

Presidents then used different methods to advance American influence. [[Theodore Roosevelt]] emphasized military strength and claimed a policing role in the Caribbean through the [[Roosevelt Corollary]]. [[William Howard Taft]] promoted investment through [[Dollar Diplomacy]], while [[Woodrow Wilson]] framed intervention in moral and democratic language. The methods differed, but all expanded United States involvement in Latin America and the Pacific.

This era demonstrates that [[American Imperialism]] was both a foreign and domestic question: debates over empire concerned race, constitutional rights, [[Executive Power|executive power]], and the meaning of consent.

## World Wars and Internationalism

The United States initially remained neutral in [[World War I]], then entered in 1917 because of [[Unrestricted Submarine Warfare|unrestricted submarine warfare]], economic and strategic ties, and conflict with Germany. Wilson promoted a [[League of Nations]], but the Senate rejected membership. The interwar country nevertheless used loans, treaties, tariffs, and diplomacy abroad; it was selectively engaged rather than completely isolated.

The attack on [[Pearl Harbor]] brought the United States into [[World War II]]. Mobilization helped defeat the Axis and left the country with unmatched industrial strength, nuclear weapons, and a permanent global military role. Unlike after World War I, the United States joined the [[United Nations]] and helped construct institutions such as the International Monetary Fund and World Bank.

## Cold War and After

During the [[Cold War]], containment of Soviet influence organized foreign policy. The [[Truman Doctrine]], [[Marshall Plan]], [[NATO]], [[Korean War]], nuclear deterrence, covert action, and [[Vietnam War]] used different means toward that goal. Anticommunism often supported alliances with authoritarian governments, exposing tension between democratic rhetoric and strategic priorities.

The Vietnam conflict weakened trust in government and encouraged Congress to challenge presidential war-making. [[Détente]] later reduced some tensions without ending the rivalry. The Soviet Union's collapse left the United States with unusual global reach, but interventions, terrorism, economic [[Globalization|globalization]], and rising powers produced new debates over costs and limits.

## Power, Ideals, and National Interest

American foreign policy shows more continuity than a simple isolation-to-internationalism story suggests. Leaders consistently pursued security, trade, and influence, but the geographic reach and institutional permanence of those policies expanded. Domestic actors—Congress, presidents, businesses, [[Social Movements|social movements]], voters, and military institutions—also competed over priorities.

The central APUSH question is how national ideals relate to power. Policies may have defended independence or democratic allies while also enabling conquest, racial hierarchy, covert intervention, and unequal influence. Both purposes and consequences are necessary to explain the United States' changing role in the world.
