> **Republicanism** is the political tradition that locates legitimate government in the people and emphasizes representative institutions, civic participation, public virtue, and resistance to arbitrary or corrupt power. It shaped colonial opposition to Britain, the creation of state and federal constitutions, and later arguments about [[Citizenship|citizenship]], economic independence, reform, and empire. Republicanism does not mean rule by the modern [[Republican Party]], nor is it identical to democracy. Americans often defended republican government while limiting participation by property, race, sex, or status. For APUSH, republicanism matters because rival groups repeatedly claimed to protect the republic while disagreeing over which concentrations of power—monarchy, mobs, banks, [[Slavery|slavery]], corporations, or government—posed the greatest danger.

## APUSH Significance

Republicanism provides a language connecting political ideals across periods:

1. **Consent replaced inherited rule.** Legitimate authority came from the people rather than monarchy or hereditary privilege.
2. **Liberty required institutions and character.** Constitutions limited power, while civic virtue asked citizens to place public good above corruption and dependency.
3. **Economic independence had political meaning.** Landownership and control of one's labor were often treated as foundations of independent judgment.
4. **Competing groups defined corruption differently.** [[Federalists]] feared disorder; Jeffersonians feared centralized finance; Jacksonians attacked monopoly; labor and [[Reform Movements|reform movements]] challenged concentrated wealth.
5. **The tradition contained exclusions.** A government could call itself republican while denying equal membership to women, enslaved people, Native peoples, and others.

Republicanism differs from direct [[Democracy]]. It permits representative government and constitutional limits on majorities; democracy emphasizes broader popular participation and political equality.

## Colonial Opposition and Revolution

British colonists drew on English traditions that warned of conspiracies against liberty and praised balanced government. After the [[French and Indian War]], new taxes, standing armies, and imperial administration appeared to many colonists as evidence that ministers intended to reduce them to dependence.

Patriot writers argued that [[Taxation Without Representation|taxation without representation]] violated consent. Boycotts and committees asked citizens to sacrifice private convenience for public liberty. The [[American Revolution]] thus depended on both rights and civic virtue: a free people had to resist corruption and govern themselves responsibly.

The [[Declaration of Independence]] grounded government in [[Natural Rights|natural rights]] and popular consent. Its language was broader than actual political membership, creating a contradiction later reformers could exploit.

## Building a Republic

Independence raised the problem of constructing authority without recreating monarchy. State constitutions established elected legislatures, and the [[Articles of Confederation]] preserved extensive state sovereignty. Economic instability and weak national capacity later convinced many leaders that liberty also required effective government.

The [[United States Constitution]] created a stronger federal republic with separated powers, checks and balances, and representation. Federalists argued that a large republic could control factions and protect stability. [[Anti-Federalists]] feared distant authority and demanded a [[Bill of Rights]].

Both sides spoke in republican terms. Their disagreement concerned which institutional design best protected self-government. The result balanced popular authority with indirect elections, an appointed judiciary, [[Federalism|federalism]], and other restraints on immediate majorities.

## Competing Economic Visions

[[Alexander Hamilton]] believed public credit, manufacturing, and a national bank would make the republic secure and prosperous. [[Thomas Jefferson]] feared that financiers, cities, and centralized authority could create dependence. He idealized independent farmers as citizens capable of political judgment.

These arguments persisted as the [[Market Revolution]] expanded banks, [[Wage Labor|wage labor]], corporations, and commerce. [[Jacksonian Democracy]] attacked the Second Bank as a privileged monopoly while building a powerful presidency and party organization. Whigs answered that economic development and moral reform strengthened republican society.

The ideal of free labor later distinguished northern republicanism from slavery. Republicans argued that western land should allow independent farmers and workers to rise through their own labor. This vision opposed slavery's expansion but did not necessarily include racial equality.

## Slavery, Civil War, and Citizenship

Slavery created the deepest contradiction in American republicanism. Enslavers claimed property rights and warned against federal tyranny, while enslaved and free Black activists identified bondage itself as arbitrary power. Abolitionists argued that a republic could not remain legitimate while holding people as property.

Secessionists claimed to defend self-government, but the Confederacy protected slavery and denied consent to the enslaved. Unionists increasingly linked preservation of republican government to emancipation. The [[Gettysburg Address]] described the [[Civil War]] as a test of whether government “of the people” could endure.

The [[Reconstruction Amendments]] abolished slavery and established national citizenship and voting protections. They moved republicanism toward a more inclusive constitutional meaning, though violence and disfranchisement prevented equal participation.

## Industrial Power and Reform

[[Industrial Capitalism]] raised new fears of dependency and corruption. Workers argued that wage earners required organization and political power to remain free citizens. Farmers attacked railroad and financial concentration, while Progressives sought to curb [[Political Machines|political machines]], trusts, and legislative corruption.

Reforms such as direct primaries, initiatives, referenda, and direct election of senators aimed to make government more responsive. Yet Progressives sometimes combined democratic reform with [[Segregation|segregation]] and coercive expertise, showing that opposition to corruption did not guarantee equal citizenship.

The [[New Deal]] redefined economic security as a support for freedom, while critics warned that federal administration threatened local self-rule. Similar conflicts continued over the welfare state, [[Civil Rights|civil rights]], corporate power, and executive authority.

## Liberty, Virtue, and Exclusion

Republicanism is a flexible tradition, not one party platform. Political actors across the ideological spectrum have claimed to defend liberty against corruption and dependence. Analysis requires identifying whose power they feared and whose independence they recognized.

The broad change in American history is an expansion of formal membership in the republic. The continuity is conflict over whether freedom depends mainly on limited government, effective public power, private property, economic security, or equal participation. Those rival answers explain republicanism's durability.
