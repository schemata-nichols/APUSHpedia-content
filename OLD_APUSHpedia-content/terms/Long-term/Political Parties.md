> **Political Parties** are durable organizations that nominate candidates, mobilize voters, coordinate officeholders, and offer competing programs for government. Although the Constitution did not create parties and many founders distrusted factions, disagreements over federal power, economic policy, foreign affairs, [[Slavery|slavery]], reform, and the scope of government repeatedly produced party systems. Parties expanded participation and made peaceful competition possible, but they also used patronage, racial appeals, restrictive rules, and institutional advantages to define whose interests counted. For APUSH, party history is not a list of changing labels: realignments reveal when existing coalitions could no longer contain major conflicts and when new issues reorganized American politics.

## APUSH Significance

Political parties connect popular participation to governing institutions:

1. **Parties organize conflict.** They combine regional, economic, racial, religious, and ideological groups into coalitions capable of winning elections.
2. **Party systems change when issues change.** Slavery, [[Industrialization|industrialization]], depression, [[Civil Rights|civil rights]], and cultural conflict have broken old alignments and created new ones.
3. **Parties broaden and manage participation.** Campaigns, conventions, newspapers, patronage, primaries, and digital media mobilize voters while giving leaders control over nominations.
4. **Institutional rules encourage two large parties.** Winner-take-all elections and the presidency make it difficult for smaller parties to persist nationally.
5. **Third parties still matter.** They introduce demands that major parties may absorb even when their candidates do not win.

A political party differs from an interest group or social movement. Parties seek control of government through elections; other organizations may pressure officials without nominating a broad slate of candidates.

## The [[First Party System]]

The first parties formed from conflict inside [[George Washington]]'s administration. [[Alexander Hamilton]] supported a national bank, commercial development, broad [[Constitutional Interpretation|constitutional interpretation]], and closer relations with Britain. [[Thomas Jefferson]] and James Madison feared concentrated federal power and organized opposition around agrarianism, strict construction, and sympathy for France.

[[Federalists]] and Democratic-Republicans created newspapers, congressional coordination, and local networks despite claiming to oppose faction. The [[Election of 1800]] produced a peaceful transfer between rival parties, helping establish opposition as legitimate. Federalists later declined because of regional concentration, internal division, and opposition to the [[War of 1812]].

The resulting [[Era of Good Feelings]] did not eliminate conflict. Without two competitive national parties, sectional and personal factions competed inside the Democratic-Republican coalition, culminating in the disputed election of 1824.

## Mass Parties and the Second Party System

[[Jacksonian Democracy]] created the Democratic Party around Andrew Jackson's opposition to concentrated privilege and his claim to represent the white male majority. Democrats generally resisted a national bank and some federal economic programs. The [[Whig Party]] united critics of Jackson's [[Executive Power|executive power]] and supporters of congressional government, internal improvements, and economic development.

Both parties built national coalitions spanning regions and used conventions, rallies, partisan newspapers, slogans, and patronage. This [[Second Party System]] increased white male turnout and made politics a regular part of [[Popular Culture|popular culture]]. Its cross-sectional structure temporarily contained conflict over slavery.

Western expansion broke that balance. The [[Kansas–Nebraska Act]] reopened the territories to possible slavery, destroying the Whigs and dividing northern Democrats. The [[Republican Party]] combined former Whigs, Free Soilers, and other antislavery voters around opposition to slavery's expansion.

## Civil War and Republican Ascendancy

Abraham Lincoln's election without southern electoral support triggered [[Secession|secession]]. During the [[Civil War]], Republicans used federal power to preserve the Union, emancipate enslaved people, support railroads and homesteads, and establish national banking. Democrats divided between supporters of the war and critics known as [[Copperheads]].

Afterward, Republicans associated themselves with Union victory and [[Reconstruction]], while Democrats rebuilt strength through the white South and northern urban organizations. The end of Reconstruction helped create a sectional pattern: a largely Democratic “Solid South” opposed a Republican coalition strong in the North.

Late nineteenth-century parties achieved high turnout but often avoided fundamental economic reform. Farmers and workers turned to third parties, especially the [[Populism|Populists]], whose demands for railroad regulation, currency expansion, and direct [[Democracy|democracy]] influenced later reforms.

## [[Progressivism]], Depression, and the [[New Deal Coalition]]

Progressives worked through both major parties and independent campaigns. Primary elections and civil-service reform weakened some party organizations, while women's suffrage enlarged the electorate. Republican divisions helped [[Woodrow Wilson]] win in 1912.

The [[Great Depression]] produced a deeper realignment. Franklin Roosevelt's [[New Deal]] coalition joined urban workers, immigrants, white southerners, farmers, liberals, and increasingly Black voters. Democrats became associated with federal economic intervention and social welfare; Republicans criticized expansion of national power while accepting parts of the new framework.

This coalition contained a major contradiction. Southern segregationists and northern civil-rights supporters belonged to the same party, limiting federal action on racial equality until movement pressure and political change broke the arrangement.

## Civil Rights and Modern Realignment

The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] reshaped party loyalties. National Democratic support for civil-rights legislation accelerated Black voters' movement toward the party. Conservative white southerners gradually shifted toward Republicans, influenced by race, religion, taxation, anticommunism, and opposition to federal intervention.

The conservative movement culminated in the [[Reagan Revolution]], while Democrats retained stronger support among many racial minorities, union households, and social liberals. Geographic sorting increased: metropolitan areas leaned Democratic, while rural areas and much of the South became more Republican.

Parties also became more ideologically distinct as conservative Democrats and liberal Republicans declined. Primaries empowered voters and activists over party leaders, while campaign finance, television, and digital media changed organization. Polarization made competition clearer but compromise more difficult.

## Coalition Building and Political Change

Party names alone do not identify stable beliefs across time. The Democratic and Republican coalitions of the twenty-first century differ greatly from those of Reconstruction or the [[Gilded Age]]. Analysis must identify the voters, regions, issues, and institutions behind each label.

Parties have made democracy workable by simplifying choices and coordinating government, but they can also narrow debate and reinforce exclusion. Realignment occurs when new conflicts reorganize coalitions—not merely whenever one election changes control.
