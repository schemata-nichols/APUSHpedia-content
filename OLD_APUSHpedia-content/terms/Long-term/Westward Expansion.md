> **Westward Expansion** was the long process through which the United States acquired, governed, settled, and economically integrated territory across North America. It involved purchases, treaties, war, federal land policy, transportation, [[Migration|migration]], resource extraction, and the displacement of Native peoples and other existing communities. Expansion offered land and mobility to many settlers and linked farms, mines, ranches, and towns to national markets. It also extended [[Slavery|slavery]], intensified sectional conflict, transformed environments, and imposed federal authority over Native nations and Mexican residents. For APUSH, westward expansion is not settlement of empty land or a single mid-nineteenth-century event; it is a state-supported contest over sovereignty, labor, race, and resources.

## APUSH Significance

Westward expansion connects territorial growth to national development and conflict:

1. **Government made settlement possible.** Diplomacy, warfare, surveys, land sales, military protection, and infrastructure transferred and organized territory.
2. **Native dispossession was central.** Expansion crossed homelands governed and used by diverse Native nations, which negotiated, adapted, and resisted.
3. **Land policy distributed opportunity unequally.** Settlers and speculators gained property while enslaved people, Native communities, and many Mexican landholders lost control of land and labor.
4. **Expansion drove [[Sectional Crisis|sectional crisis]].** New territory repeatedly reopened whether slavery would expand and which section would control Congress.
5. **Markets transformed the West.** Railroads, mining, ranching, commercial farming, and federal policy integrated western resources into national and global [[Capitalism|capitalism]].

Expansion differs from migration alone. Individuals moved west, but federal and state power determined borders, title, security, infrastructure, and whose prior claims would be recognized.

## Early Republic and Native Land

After independence, the United States claimed land west of the Appalachians that Native nations still governed and occupied. The Northwest Ordinance created a process for territories to become states and prohibited slavery north of the Ohio River, establishing a model for continental incorporation.

Settlers crossed treaty boundaries, while federal officials used negotiations, annuities, military campaigns, and land cessions to reduce Native control. Native confederacies resisted, and outcomes depended on diplomacy and war rather than an inevitable flow of population.

The [[Louisiana Purchase]] doubled the nation's claimed territory in 1803. The [[Lewis and Clark Expedition|Lewis and Clark expedition]] gathered geographic and diplomatic information, while traders and migrants expanded American presence. The purchase raised constitutional questions and placed many Native nations within borders asserted by the United States without their consent.

## Market Growth and Removal

The [[Market Revolution]] increased the value of western land. Roads, canals, steamboats, and later railroads connected farmers to distant buyers. Federal land sales and credit encouraged both family settlement and speculation, while commercial agriculture transformed forests, waterways, and grasslands.

In the Southeast, cotton profits intensified white demand for Native homelands. The [[Indian Removal]] Act and coercive treaties displaced Cherokee, Muscogee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole people toward Indian Territory. Removal joined racial ideology to the expansion of the [[Cotton Kingdom]] and slavery.

Expansion also moved enslaved people through the domestic slave trade. Western and southwestern growth therefore spread forced labor as well as independent farming.

## Manifest Destiny and War with Mexico

During the 1840s, [[Manifest Destiny]] described continental growth as providential and democratic. The phrase gave ideological unity to older ambitions but also relied on beliefs in white and Anglo-American superiority.

The annexation of Texas, division of Oregon with Britain, and [[Mexican–American War]] rapidly extended national borders. The [[Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo]] transferred the [[Mexican Cession]] to the United States. Mexican residents were promised rights, but legal costs, violence, taxes, and unfamiliar property procedures contributed to land loss.

The acquisition reopened the slavery question. The [[Wilmot Proviso]] failed to ban slavery in the new territory, but it revealed sectional voting. The [[Compromise of 1850]] and [[Kansas–Nebraska Act]] could not settle whether western development would be organized around free or enslaved labor.

## Civil War–Era Development

With southern representatives absent during the [[Civil War]], Republicans enacted a development program. The [[Homestead Act]] offered land to settlers who met residence and improvement requirements. The [[Pacific Railway Act]] subsidized a [[Transcontinental Railroad|transcontinental railroad]], and the Morrill Act supported land-grant colleges.

These laws encouraged farming, migration, education, and national markets, but the land was not empty. Railroad construction, military forts, and settler expansion intensified warfare and reduced Native access to hunting grounds. Federal policy increasingly confined Native peoples to reservations.

Chinese immigrants performed essential railroad labor while facing discrimination and later exclusion. Black migrants, including Exodusters and Buffalo Soldiers, pursued opportunity in the West within persistent racial boundaries.

## Conquest, Allotment, and Commercial Integration

After 1865, the army fought Native nations across the Plains and Southwest as migrants, miners, ranchers, and railroads entered their lands. Native peoples used diplomacy, adaptation, flight, and armed resistance. The destruction of bison herds undermined Plains economies and strengthened federal pressure.

Reservation policy restricted movement and made communities dependent on frequently inadequate federal supplies. The [[Dawes Act]] later divided collectively held tribal land into individual allotments, presenting private property and farming as assimilation. “Surplus” land passed to non-Native ownership, causing enormous Native land loss.

Mining booms, cattle drives, commercial farms, and railroads tied the region to corporate finance and distant markets. Aridity made irrigation and federal water policy especially important. Western development was therefore shaped by large institutions as well as individual settlers.

## Expansion, State Power, and Dispossession

The frontier did not move across vacant space, and its supposed closure did not end western history. Expansion involved overlapping sovereignties and deliberate government choices about land, labor, transportation, and [[Citizenship|citizenship]].

Its legacy combines national growth with dispossession. Landownership and markets created opportunity for many migrants, while conquest and racial policy determined who could claim that opportunity. This contradiction makes expansion central to understanding [[Democracy|democracy]], [[Sectionalism|sectionalism]], [[Environmental Change|environmental change]], and federal power.
