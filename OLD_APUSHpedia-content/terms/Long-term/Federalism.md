> **Federalism** is a constitutional system in which authority is divided between a national government and state governments, each acting directly on the people. The United States adopted federalism when the Constitution replaced the state-dominated [[Articles of Confederation]] with a stronger but limited national government. The location of power has never remained fixed. Courts, elections, wars, economic crises, [[Social Movements|social movements]], and legislation have repeatedly altered the balance. For APUSH, federalism is not simply a list of delegated and reserved powers; it is the institutional setting for conflicts over economic development, [[Slavery|slavery]], Native sovereignty, [[Civil Rights|civil rights]], social welfare, and whether national standards or local control better protect liberty.

## APUSH Significance

Federalism connects constitutional structure to recurring political conflict:

1. **The Constitution divided sovereignty.** National and state governments received authority rather than one level merely administering the other.
2. **Constitutional language requires interpretation.** Enumerated powers, the Necessary and Proper Clause, the Supremacy Clause, and the Tenth Amendment do not settle every boundary.
3. **Policy interests shape constitutional arguments.** Political groups have supported state or national power differently depending on the issue.
4. **Crises often expand federal capacity.** War, [[Industrialization|industrialization]], depression, and rights movements have encouraged national taxation, regulation, administration, and enforcement.
5. **Decentralization has mixed consequences.** States can adapt and experiment, but variation can also preserve unequal services, political rights, and legal protections.

Federalism differs from separation of powers. Federalism divides authority geographically between levels of government; separation of powers divides functions among legislative, executive, and judicial branches.

## From Confederation to Constitution

Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress depended heavily on states for revenue and enforcement. It could not tax individuals directly or regulate interstate commerce. Financial instability, interstate disputes, and [[Shays' Rebellion]] persuaded many leaders that national authority was inadequate.

The [[United States Constitution]] granted Congress powers over taxation, commerce, currency, defense, and other national concerns while preserving state governments. The Supremacy Clause made constitutional federal law superior to conflicting state law. The Tenth Amendment later confirmed that powers neither delegated to the United States nor prohibited to the states remained with the states or people.

These provisions created a framework rather than a precise boundary. Some powers are enumerated, some are implied as means of carrying them out, and some are exercised concurrently. Political conflict and judicial review determine how those categories apply.

## Early Constitutional Conflict

[[Alexander Hamilton]]'s financial program produced an early argument over implied power. Hamilton claimed that Congress could create the [[First Bank of the United States]] as a useful means of exercising fiscal powers. [[Thomas Jefferson]] argued for a narrower reading because the Constitution did not expressly authorize a bank.

In [[McCulloch v. Maryland]], the [[Marshall Court]] upheld the Second Bank and ruled that Maryland could not tax it. Chief Justice John Marshall interpreted the Necessary and Proper Clause broadly and treated state interference with a valid federal institution as inconsistent with national supremacy. [[Gibbons v. Ogden]] similarly strengthened federal authority over interstate commerce.

The decisions did not eliminate state power. States remained central to property, family law, elections, education, policing, and economic development. The recurring question was which subjects had become sufficiently national to justify federal action.

## Nullification, Slavery, and Civil War

During the [[Nullification Crisis]], South Carolina asserted that a state could invalidate a federal tariff. President Andrew Jackson rejected that claim and supported coercive authority while accepting a negotiated tariff reduction. The episode showed that defenders of limited government could still use federal power to preserve the Union.

Slavery made federalism more explosive. Slave states demanded federal protection for slave property, including enforcement of fugitive-slave laws, while also invoking state sovereignty against restrictions. Some northern states used personal-liberty laws to resist federal enforcement. After Abraham Lincoln's election, secessionists claimed the authority to leave the Union.

Union victory in the [[Civil War]] rejected unilateral [[Secession|secession]]. The [[Reconstruction Amendments]] then altered federalism by abolishing slavery, defining national [[Citizenship]], and authorizing federal protection of civil and [[Voting Rights|voting rights]]. Yet declining enforcement allowed southern states to construct the [[Jim Crow System]].

## Industrialization and the New Deal

National markets created pressure for federal railroad, corporate, labor, and consumer regulation. Courts initially drew limits around the commerce power and sometimes protected contracts against state regulation. Progressive legislation expanded national oversight without establishing comprehensive federal economic management.

The [[Great Depression]] transformed this balance. State and local governments lacked resources to address mass unemployment and banking collapse. The [[New Deal]] created national relief programs, financial regulation, labor protections, agricultural policy, and Social Security. After early resistance, the Supreme Court accepted broader federal commerce and spending powers.

Federal grants also changed the relationship between governments. National funding allowed states to administer shared programs while meeting federal conditions, creating cooperation as well as conflict over control.

## Civil Rights and Modern Federalism

Federalism became central to the struggle against [[Segregation|segregation]]. Southern officials defended local control, while civil-rights activists demanded federal protection when states denied constitutional rights. _[[Brown v. Board of Education]]_, federal enforcement of desegregation, the [[Civil Rights Act of 1964]], and the [[Voting Rights Act of 1965]] used national power against state-supported discrimination.

Later movements for devolution sought to return authority over welfare, education, regulation, and criminal justice to states. Modern conflicts over healthcare, [[Immigration|immigration]] enforcement, environmental rules, reproductive rights, voting, and firearms continue to move between courts, Congress, federal agencies, and state governments.

## Divided Authority and National Power

Neither state nor national authority is inherently more democratic or protective of liberty. States have pioneered reform and resisted national injustice; they have also maintained slavery, segregation, and disfranchisement. Federal institutions have enforced rights and provided national solutions while also carrying out removal, exclusion, surveillance, and coercion.

Federalism endures because the Constitution creates overlapping authority rather than a final allocation for every issue. Its history is therefore a record of how Americans redefine the scale of public problems and decide which government has the legitimacy and capacity to address them.
