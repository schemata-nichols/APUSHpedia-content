> **Urbanization** is the growth of cities and the increasing concentration of population, work, institutions, and infrastructure in metropolitan areas. American cities expanded through commerce, [[Industrialization]], [[Immigration]], internal [[Migration]], and transportation networks, especially during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Urban growth created jobs, cultural exchange, political organization, and innovation while also concentrating poverty, pollution, disease, and unequal access to housing and services. For APUSH, urbanization is not merely population increase: it is a reorganization of social and political life whose benefits and burdens were divided by class, race, ethnicity, and neighborhood.

## APUSH Significance

Colonial ports connected North America to the [[Atlantic Economy]], but industrial cities grew dramatically after the Civil War as factories attracted immigrants and rural migrants. Dense neighborhoods supported ethnic institutions, labor organizing, mass entertainment, and political machines that traded services for votes. Overcrowding and poor sanitation helped inspire [[Progressivism]], including housing codes, public-health measures, and municipal reform. Reformers could improve conditions while also imposing middle-class judgments on immigrant and working-class communities.

## Metropolitan Change

The [[Great Migration]] reshaped northern and western cities, but restrictive covenants, redlining, violence, and segregation confined many Black residents to neighborhoods denied equal investment. After [[World War II]], highways, suburbanization, and federal mortgage policy moved many jobs and white families outward while urban renewal displaced established communities. Later reinvestment revived some city centers but often raised housing costs and displaced poorer residents. Urbanization therefore connects economic change to local government, environmental infrastructure, racial inequality, and political power, and it must be distinguished from suburbanization even though both belong to the development of metropolitan regions.

## Industry, Infrastructure, and Governance

Industrial employment pulled people toward cities, but urban growth also required systems for land, transportation, water, food, waste, and public safety. Streetcars enlarged the area from which workers could commute and encouraged neighborhoods divided by income. Skyscrapers concentrated offices on expensive central land, while rail terminals and ports connected urban factories and consumers to national and international markets. These technologies made density productive while creating new dependence on coordinated infrastructure.

Local governments struggled to serve rapidly growing populations. Political machines exchanged jobs, aid, and access for votes, providing real assistance while encouraging patronage and corruption. Reformers promoted professional administration, building codes, parks, sanitation, and public health. Their changes reduced some hazards, but “reform” could also weaken immigrant political influence or displace working-class neighborhoods. Urban governance was a contest over whose needs counted and who would pay for common services.

## Segregation and Metropolitan Inequality

Cities created proximity without equal access. Immigrants often formed ethnic neighborhoods around family networks, churches, businesses, and mutual-aid societies. These communities eased adjustment and preserved culture, even as overcrowding and discrimination constrained housing choices. Black urban communities built businesses and institutions during the [[Great Migration]], while racial violence and housing restrictions enforced segregation outside the South.

The federal government helped create the later metropolis. Mortgage guarantees, highway construction, and tax policy subsidized suburban homeownership and automobile commuting. Redlining and discriminatory real-estate practices excluded many Black families from appreciating suburban property, while highways and urban renewal cut through established neighborhoods. Jobs and tax resources moved outward, leaving many central cities with concentrated poverty and reduced revenue.

Late twentieth-century deindustrialization eliminated many stable urban jobs, but service, finance, technology, and cultural industries revived selected areas. Gentrification brought investment and rising property values while threatening displacement. Urbanization should thus be analyzed at the metropolitan scale: city growth and suburban growth are interconnected outcomes of work, transportation, public policy, and unequal control of land.
