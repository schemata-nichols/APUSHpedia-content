> **Environmental change** in APUSH is the transformation of land, water, ecosystems, and resource use through human activity, together with the ways geography shapes human choices. Native agriculture and controlled burning altered environments before European arrival; the [[Columbian Exchange]], settler expansion, industrialization, and urban growth then accelerated ecological change. Environmental history is not separate from political or economic history: control of land and resources structured migration, Native dispossession, labor systems, and federal policy. The central analytical question is who gained from environmental transformation, who bore its costs, and how those consequences prompted conservation or regulation.

## APUSH Significance

European livestock, plants, diseases, and commercial extraction reshaped colonial environments, while plantation agriculture depleted soils and encouraged expansion onto new land. During the nineteenth century, canals, railroads, mining, commercial farming, and the destruction of bison supported [[Westward Expansion]] and the [[Market Revolution]] while undermining Native economies and sovereignty. Federal land policy treated much western land as a resource for settlement and business, even though aridity and existing Native claims complicated the image of an empty frontier.

## Development and Response

Industrial cities concentrated smoke, waste, and unsafe water, and large corporations extracted timber, minerals, and oil on an unprecedented scale. Progressive conservation produced national forests, parks, and regulatory agencies, but preservation sometimes removed Native communities or restricted their traditional land use. Later disasters and activism contributed to modern environmental regulation, including federal clean-air and clean-water policies. Environmental change therefore combines continuity—persistent resource extraction and unequal exposure—with change in scale, technology, scientific knowledge, and the federal government's willingness to regulate environmental harm.

## Land, Labor, and Expansion

Environmental transformation often depended on political authority and coerced labor. Colonial governments granted land and regulated access to forests and waterways, while enslaved workers converted wetlands and forests into rice, tobacco, sugar, and cotton landscapes. As soils were exhausted, plantation expansion placed new pressure on Native land. The [[Cotton Kingdom]] therefore joined ecological change to [[Slavery]], commodity markets, and forced migration.

In the nineteenth-century West, federal surveys, land grants, military campaigns, and railroad subsidies converted Indigenous homelands into farms, ranches, mines, and transportation corridors. Settlers introduced fences and private property boundaries into ecosystems organized around water, grazing, and seasonal movement. Irrigation allowed intensive agriculture and urban growth in arid regions, but dams and diversions shifted costs downstream. Geography constrained settlement, yet technology and state policy redistributed rather than removed those constraints.

## Conservation, Justice, and Federal Regulation

The late nineteenth-century conservation movement contained different goals. Preservationists sought to protect certain landscapes from development, while conservationists emphasized scientific management and sustained resource use. National parks and forests restricted private exploitation, but officials sometimes treated landscapes as wilderness only after removing or limiting Native residents. Conservation could thus restrain corporations while extending federal control over Indigenous land.

Twentieth-century environmental politics grew from visible connections among pollution, public health, and industrial risk. Urban sanitation systems reduced disease; later activism addressed pesticides, smog, contaminated water, oil spills, and endangered species. Laws and agencies expanded federal regulation because pollution crossed property and state boundaries. Businesses and workers sometimes resisted rules they believed threatened jobs or local control, making environmental protection a debate over costs as well as scientific evidence.

Environmental burdens have also been unequal. Highways, waste sites, industrial pollution, and hazardous work have disproportionately affected low-income communities and communities of color, encouraging environmental-justice organizing. Climate change extends the same analytical problem across generations and national borders. Throughout APUSH, environmental change is best explained as an interaction among ecosystems, technology, markets, government, and power—not as a backdrop that humans simply occupy.
