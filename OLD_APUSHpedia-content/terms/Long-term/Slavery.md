> **Slavery** was a system of forced labor and legal domination that treated human beings as property and made enslaved status hereditary through racialized law. In British North America and the United States, slavery developed from Atlantic colonization, expanded with plantation agriculture, and became central to wealth, political power, territorial growth, and social hierarchy. Enslaved people endured sale, violence, family separation, and denial of legal autonomy while building communities and resisting bondage. Slavery ended through enslaved people's actions, abolitionism, civil war, federal policy, and constitutional amendment—not through gradual economic decline. For APUSH, slavery is a national institution whose growth and destruction shaped [[Capitalism|capitalism]], race, [[Democracy|democracy]], [[Federalism|federalism]], and the Constitution.

## APUSH Significance

Slavery connects labor and race to the country's major political transformations:

1. **Law constructed hereditary racial bondage.** Colonial statutes made African ancestry and maternal status central to permanent enslavement.
2. **Slavery generated wealth beyond plantations.** Merchants, shippers, insurers, banks, manufacturers, and governments benefited from slave-produced commodities.
3. **Enslaved people resisted and created community.** Family, religion, culture, escape, work resistance, and revolt limited enslavers' control.
4. **Expansion made slavery a national political crisis.** Each new territory raised questions about representation, property, and the future balance of power.
5. **Emancipation changed the Constitution but not every hierarchy.** Abolition ended legal ownership of people, while struggles over labor, [[Citizenship|citizenship]], land, and racial violence continued.

Slavery should be distinguished from other coerced labor systems. [[Indentured Servitude]] was contractually limited; racial [[Chattel Slavery]] generally lasted for life, passed to children, and denied personhood through property law.

## Colonial Development

European colonization created intense demand for labor in plantation regions. Native enslavement and European indentured labor existed alongside the forced [[Migration|migration]] of Africans. Over the seventeenth century, colonies increasingly codified racial chattel slavery, tying bondage to African descent and making children's status follow their mothers.

This transition served economic and political purposes. Permanent hereditary labor benefited landowners, while legal privileges encouraged Europeans of different classes to identify as white. Religion did not automatically free an enslaved person, and restrictions on movement, assembly, literacy, weapons, and interracial relations reinforced control.

Slavery varied by region. Tobacco plantations shaped the Chesapeake; rice and indigo plantations in the Lower South often used large enslaved workforces. Northern colonies held fewer enslaved people but participated through farms, households, ports, shipping, and Atlantic commerce.

## Revolution and the Early Republic

The [[American Revolution]] created both challenges and protections for slavery. Natural-rights language encouraged petitions, lawsuits, and gradual emancipation in northern states. Thousands of enslaved people pursued freedom by joining or fleeing to British and American forces.

Yet the Constitution protected slaveholding through the [[Three-Fifths Compromise]], fugitive-slave provision, and delayed prohibition of the international trade. Federal representation gave slave states added political influence without granting rights to enslaved people.

After Congress ended legal international importation in 1808, the domestic slave trade expanded. Families were separated as enslaved people were sold from the Upper South to growing plantation regions farther south and west.

## Cotton Expansion and Enslaved Life

The [[Cotton Gin|cotton gin]] and international textile demand made short-staple cotton highly profitable. The [[Cotton Kingdom]] spread across Native lands taken through treaties, warfare, and [[Indian Removal]]. By the Civil War, slave labor produced the country's leading export and connected southern plantations to northern and British industry.

Enslaved people's lives differed by crop, task, location, age, gender, skill, and proximity to owners. All lived under coercion and threat of sale. Families and religious communities created support and meaning but lacked secure legal protection. Sexual violence was a feature of owners' power, and enslaved women's labor included both production and reproduction under bondage.

Resistance ranged from slowing work and breaking tools to preserving culture, learning to read, escaping, and revolt. Rebellions by [[Denmark Vesey]]'s alleged conspiracy and [[Nat Turner's Rebellion]] intensified white fear and restrictions, but everyday resistance was more common.

## Abolition and [[Sectional Crisis]]

Black activists and white allies built [[Abolitionism]] around immediate emancipation, moral argument, testimony, political action, and assistance to freedom seekers. Most white northerners were not abolitionists, and many accepted racial inequality. Opposition to slavery's expansion was therefore broader than support for immediate abolition.

Western growth repeatedly reopened the issue. The [[Missouri Compromise]], [[Compromise of 1850]], stronger fugitive-slave enforcement, [[Kansas–Nebraska Act]], and [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] attempted or imposed different settlements. Each connected slavery to federal power and sectional representation.

The [[Republican Party]] opposed expansion, arguing that western territories should support free labor. Lincoln's election convinced secessionists that slavery faced long-term political containment. Confederate leaders explicitly protected slavery when leaving the Union.

## War and Emancipation

Enslaved people made the [[Civil War]] a struggle over slavery by fleeing to Union lines, withholding labor, providing intelligence, and serving the Union. Congress adopted confiscation measures, and the [[Emancipation Proclamation]] declared freedom in rebelling territory while authorizing Black military service.

The proclamation was a war measure with geographic limits. The [[Reconstruction Amendments|Thirteenth Amendment]] abolished slavery throughout the United States, except as punishment for crime. Emancipation destroyed owners' legal property claims but did not distribute land or guarantee economic independence.

During [[Reconstruction]], freedpeople pursued family reunification, education, churches, land, wages, and political participation. [[Sharecropping]], violence, [[Black Codes]], and weak federal enforcement constrained freedom. Slavery ended, but racialized labor exploitation and unequal citizenship persisted.

## Coercion, Resistance, and National Development

Slavery was neither a static southern custom nor an institution naturally approaching extinction. It expanded through markets, territorial acquisition, law, and violence. Its profitability and political power made compromise increasingly unstable.

Enslaved people were subjected to a system designed to control them, yet they remained historical actors. Their survival, labor, resistance, escape, military service, and demands for citizenship were essential to slavery's destruction and to the meaning of freedom afterward.
