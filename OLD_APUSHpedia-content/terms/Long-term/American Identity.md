> **American Identity** is the changing collection of ideas through which people have defined membership in the United States and the qualities believed to distinguish the nation. It has drawn on republican government, liberty, equality, religious and ethnic pluralism, territorial expansion, economic opportunity, and claims of national mission. Those ideals have never described every American's experience equally. Native dispossession, [[Slavery|slavery]], racial exclusion, unequal [[Citizenship|citizenship]], and conflicts over [[Immigration|immigration]] repeatedly exposed the distance between national principles and political practice. For APUSH, American identity is not one permanent character trait; it is an argument over who belongs, which history the nation remembers, and whether American ideals justify existing institutions or demand their reform.

## APUSH Significance

American identity connects political ideals to contests over power and belonging:

1. **Revolution created a national claim.** Independence encouraged colonists to imagine themselves as one people committed to republican government and [[Natural Rights|natural rights]].
2. **Citizenship remained bounded.** Law and custom often treated white men as full political members while excluding Native peoples, enslaved people, women, and many immigrants.
3. **Expansion shaped national purpose.** Continental growth and overseas power were celebrated as progress but depended on conquest, removal, war, and racial hierarchy.
4. **[[Migration]] repeatedly changed the nation.** Immigrants and internal migrants formed new communities while facing pressures to assimilate and episodes of [[Nativism]].
5. **Reformers used national ideals critically.** Abolitionists, suffragists, labor activists, and civil-rights leaders demanded that the United States fulfill promises it had applied selectively.

The important analytical distinction is between **civic ideals**, which define the nation through political principles, and ethnic, racial, or religious definitions that restrict membership. Both have operated throughout United States history.

## Revolution and the New Republic

Before independence, British North American colonists usually identified strongly with colony, region, religion, and the British Empire. Imperial conflict after the [[French and Indian War]] encouraged cooperation and a shared language of rights. The [[Declaration of Independence]] asserted that governments derived power from the consent of the governed, giving the new nation a universal-sounding explanation for its existence.

The Revolution did not make membership universal. Enslaved people remained in bondage, Native nations retained their own sovereignties, and women lacked equal political rights. The Constitution counted and governed populations differently while leaving voting qualifications largely to states. Even so, republican institutions and written declarations supplied standards later movements could turn against exclusion.

Early leaders also tried to build attachment to the Union through national symbols, public rituals, schools, newspapers, and histories. Political conflict showed that identity was contested from the beginning. [[Federalists]] emphasized order and national authority; Democratic-Republicans feared centralized power and celebrated an agrarian republic.

## Expansion, Region, and Race

The nineteenth-century nation was often imagined through westward movement. [[Manifest Destiny]] presented continental expansion as providential and democratic. Settlement created opportunities for many white families, but it also drove [[Indian Removal]], war with Mexico, and disputes over whether slavery would enter western territories.

Regional identities could reinforce or challenge national unity. Northeastern manufacturing, the southern slave economy, and [[Western Settlement|western settlement]] produced different interests and social orders. The [[Sectional Crisis]] revealed that Americans disagreed not only over policy but over the meaning of liberty, equality, property, and Union. The [[Civil War]] preserved the nation and emancipation destroyed slavery, while the [[Reconstruction Amendments]] redefined national citizenship. Their weak enforcement allowed racial exclusion to survive under new forms.

Ideas about race were central to these boundaries. Citizenship law initially favored free white immigrants; slavery and [[Segregation|segregation]] classified Black Americans as subordinate; federal policy treated Native peoples as distinct nations and later pressed them toward assimilation. National identity therefore grew through both inclusion and exclusion.

## Immigration and Cultural Pluralism

Successive migrations complicated claims that Americans shared one ancestry, religion, or culture. Irish and German immigration provoked anti-Catholic politics in the antebellum period. Southern and eastern European, Asian, Mexican, Caribbean, and later global migrations expanded the country's languages, faiths, foods, labor movements, and political coalitions.

Immigrants often embraced American citizenship while maintaining family, religious, and ethnic institutions. Governments alternated between incorporation and restriction through naturalization rules, Chinese exclusion, quota laws, deportation, and the more open framework created by the [[Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965]]. Debates over assimilation and pluralism ask whether unity requires cultural sameness or can rest on shared civic commitments.

Internal migrations mattered too. The [[Great Migration]] enabled millions of Black southerners to pursue industrial work and political opportunity outside the South. Their communities reshaped northern and western cities, national culture, and civil-rights politics while encountering housing discrimination and racial violence.

## War, Power, and National Mission

Foreign conflict often strengthened national solidarity while revealing disagreement about American purpose. Wars against Britain supported an independent identity; expansion into Mexico joined nationalism to territorial conquest. In the twentieth century, participation in two world wars encouraged claims that the United States defended [[Democracy|democracy]], even while segregation and unequal rights persisted at home.

The [[Cold War]] promoted an image of the nation as leader of the “free world.” Civil-rights activists used that claim to expose the contradiction of racial discrimination in a country competing for global influence. Opposition to the [[Vietnam War]] showed that dissent could be framed either as disloyalty or as a defense of American principles.

## Belonging and Contested Identity

American identity should not be reduced to patriotism or a fixed list of values. It changes when institutions, borders, populations, and power relationships change. The same ideas can support opposing arguments: liberty has defended property and challenged slavery; individualism has celebrated opportunity and obscured structural inequality; national mission has inspired democratic reform and justified expansion.

Its strongest APUSH use is to compare claims with access. Who could vote, own land, enter the country, become a citizen, or receive legal protection at a particular moment? That comparison shows both continuity in American ideals and change in the people able to claim them.
