> **Migration** is the movement of people within or across regions, whether voluntary, coerced, or driven by pressures that narrow meaningful choice. In United States history, Native displacement, settler movement, the forced relocation of enslaved people, rural-to-urban movement, and the Great Migration all redistributed population and power. Migrants carried labor, culture, religion, and political demands while confronting new environments and hierarchies. For APUSH, migration differs from [[Immigration]]: immigration crosses an international boundary, whereas migration also includes movement within lands claimed by the United States.

## APUSH Significance

Before European contact, Native peoples moved through trade, seasonal subsistence, diplomacy, and conflict. European colonization then caused catastrophic disruption and created forced movement through the [[Atlantic Slave Trade]]. In the nineteenth century, [[Westward Expansion]] drew white settlers toward land and markets while [[Indian Removal]] and the domestic slave trade compelled others to move. These streams were connected: federal policy and racial power made land available to some migrants by dispossessing or transporting others.

## Work, Community, and Power

Industrialization encouraged movement from farms to cities, and the [[Great Migration]] carried millions of African Americans from the South to northern and western cities in the twentieth century. They sought jobs and relief from the [[Jim Crow System]], reshaping music, neighborhoods, labor, and electoral politics while encountering housing discrimination and racial violence. Later migration toward the Sun Belt shifted population and political influence. Migration therefore produces both change and continuity: destinations offer new opportunities, but migrants often meet familiar inequalities. Its causes must be explained through both “push” pressures and “pull” attractions, without assuming that all movement was freely chosen.

## Push, Pull, and Networks

Migration rarely has one cause. Economic crisis, war, persecution, environmental change, and dispossession can push people from a place, while land, employment, security, or family connections pull them elsewhere. These forces operate through government policy and social networks. Earlier migrants send information and resources, employers recruit labor, transportation companies make movement possible, and law determines who may travel, settle, own property, or become a citizen.

These mechanisms explain why movement often follows established paths. The [[Great Migration]] drew Black southerners to cities where relatives, newspapers, and labor recruiters provided knowledge and assistance. Mexican migration expanded with labor demand and rail connections across a border created by the Mexican–American War. Federal recruitment brought workers through the Bracero Program, while deportation campaigns exposed how quickly welcomed labor could be reclassified as unwanted.

## Settlement, Community, and Conflict

Migration changes both origin and destination. Departing workers alter household economies and political communities; arrivals reshape neighborhoods, religious institutions, schools, labor markets, and elections. Migrants do not merely assimilate into a static society. They preserve and revise cultural practices while influencing the culture around them. Second generations may develop identities distinct from both their parents' communities and dominant expectations.

Internal movement has been as consequential as entry from abroad. White settlement across Native homelands, the forced migration of enslaved people into the Deep South, Dust Bowl movement to California, and postwar relocation to the Sun Belt reorganized political power and regional economies. Suburbanization redistributed tax bases and access to housing while federal policy and discrimination limited who could participate. Calling each movement “migration” should not erase the difference between voluntary relocation, constrained choice, and forced removal.

Migration often produces backlash when residents or political leaders treat newcomers as economic competitors, racial outsiders, or threats to social order. Yet the demand for migrant labor frequently coexists with exclusion from full membership. That contradiction connects migration to [[Citizenship]], [[Social Class]], race, and national identity across nearly every APUSH period.
