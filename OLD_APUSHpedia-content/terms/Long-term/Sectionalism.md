> **Sectionalism** is political loyalty organized around a region whose economic interests, labor systems, social institutions, or cultural identity appear to conflict with those of other regions or the nation. In United States history, sectionalism most often describes the antebellum struggle among North, South, and West, especially over [[Slavery|slavery]]'s expansion and federal power. Regional difference alone does not produce a [[Sectional Crisis|sectional crisis]]; it becomes sectionalism when institutions and voters interpret national issues through opposing regional interests. For APUSH, the term explains both conflict and coalition: regions developed distinct economies, yet parties and compromises sometimes bridged them until western expansion made the future of slavery increasingly impossible to contain.

## APUSH Significance

Sectionalism connects geography to political power:

1. **Regional economies shaped interests.** Manufacturing, commercial farming, plantation slavery, and [[Western Settlement|western settlement]] generated different policy demands.
2. **Representation made expansion politically important.** New states affected the balance of power in Congress, especially the Senate.
3. **National parties could suppress or intensify sectional conflict.** Cross-sectional coalitions delayed rupture; their collapse made regional voting more dangerous.
4. **Slavery became the central antebellum division.** Tariffs and internal improvements mattered, but slavery's expansion concerned labor, race, property, and political power simultaneously.
5. **Sectional identities survived the Civil War.** Reconstruction, [[Segregation|segregation]], economic development, and later party realignment retained strong regional patterns.

Sectionalism differs from ordinary regional diversity. A region becomes a political section when people organize around shared interests and portray another region's power as a threat.

## Early Regional Differences

Colonial geography and settlement produced distinct regional patterns. New England developed towns, mixed farming, shipping, and commerce; the Chesapeake and Lower South relied more heavily on plantation staples and enslaved labor; the [[Middle Colonies]] combined grain production, ports, and diversity.

Independence did not erase those differences. Debate over the Constitution revealed conflicts between large and small states, commercial and agricultural interests, and slaveholding and nonslaveholding regions. Compromises over representation and slavery enabled union without resolving its inequalities.

The [[First Party System|first party system]] crossed regional lines, but foreign policy and economic development often had sectional dimensions. New England Federalist opposition to the [[War of 1812]] culminated in the [[Hartford Convention]] and accusations of disloyalty.

## Economic Development and the West

The [[Market Revolution]] integrated regions while making their production more specialized. Northeastern manufacturing used western food and southern cotton. The Old Northwest developed commercial agriculture, while the Deep South expanded the [[Cotton Kingdom]] through enslaved labor.

Integration did not guarantee political agreement. Tariffs benefited some manufacturers but raised objections among southern exporters. The [[American System]] proposed a national bank, protective tariff, and internal improvements to bind regions together, but arguments over cost and constitutional authority limited the program.

The West was not a stable third section. Western representatives sometimes aligned with the North over free labor, with the South over cheap land and limited government, or with national development coalitions. As settlement advanced, the key question became whether new territory would strengthen free or slave states.

## Slavery and Political Balance

The [[Missouri Compromise]] admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state while drawing a line for slavery in the remaining [[Louisiana Purchase]] territory. It preserved Senate balance and revealed that expansion could turn slavery into a national constitutional crisis.

During the 1830s, abolitionist petitions, the gag rule, and resistance to abolition sharpened mistrust. Southern leaders defended slavery as essential to their economy and social order. Many northerners who rejected abolition nevertheless opposed what they called the Slave Power's influence over the federal government.

National Democrats and Whigs contained these tensions by competing in both free and slave states. Their coalitions encouraged leaders to avoid measures that would split voters sectionally.

## The Sectional Crisis

Territory acquired in the [[Mexican–American War]] reopened the question. The [[Wilmot Proviso]] proposed banning slavery in the [[Mexican Cession]]. Although it failed, voting on it revealed a growing North-South divide.

The [[Compromise of 1850]] admitted California as a free state and addressed other territorial issues, but its stronger Fugitive Slave Act brought federal enforcement of slavery into northern communities. The [[Kansas–Nebraska Act]] then repealed the Missouri line and used [[Popular Sovereignty|popular sovereignty]], producing [[Bleeding Kansas]] and destroying the [[Whig Party]].

The new [[Republican Party]] opposed slavery's western expansion and became dominant in much of the North. The Supreme Court's [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]] decision denied congressional power to prohibit slavery in the territories, persuading many Republicans that compromise favored slaveholders.

Abraham Lincoln's election in 1860 demonstrated that a candidate could win without southern electoral votes. [[Secession]] followed because slaveholding leaders treated permanent minority status as a threat to slavery. The [[Civil War]] was therefore the culmination of sectionalism organized around the future and security of slavery.

## After the Civil War

Union victory abolished slavery and rejected secession, but regional conflict continued. [[Reconstruction]] placed former Confederate states under new constitutional requirements and temporarily supported biracial politics. White southern resistance and northern retreat contributed to the restoration of conservative home rule and the [[Jim Crow System]].

Economic differences persisted between an industrializing North, an agricultural South, and a developing West, although railroads and national corporations tied them more closely together. The “Solid South” became a durable Democratic region, later shifting toward Republicans during twentieth-century realignment.

## Regional Difference and National Breakdown

Sectionalism did not make Civil War inevitable from the founding. Institutions, compromises, parties, and shared economic interests repeatedly managed regional conflict. Crisis emerged when territorial expansion intensified the slavery issue and national parties could no longer bridge it.

Economic difference alone is also insufficient. The central antebellum conflict involved the political and moral order built around slavery: who could own labor, where that system could expand, and whether the federal government would protect it. That combination turned regions into rival political sections.
