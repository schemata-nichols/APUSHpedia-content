> **Native American–United States relations** encompass diplomacy, warfare, treaties, trade, land seizure, removal, assimilation policy, tribal resistance, and struggles over sovereignty from the early republic to the present. Native nations were and remain political actors with distinct governments, not merely racial minorities within U.S. society. Federal policy shifted from treaty-making and removal to reservations, allotment, termination, and greater support for self-determination, yet pressure on Native land and authority persisted. For APUSH, the central issue is the conflict between Indigenous sovereignty and an expanding settler state.

## APUSH Significance

The United States inherited and expanded colonial struggles over territory. Treaties formally recognized Native nations as negotiating parties, but military pressure, settler intrusion, and unequal enforcement repeatedly transferred land. [[Indian Removal]] drove southeastern nations west in the 1830s, while later western conquest confined many Native peoples to reservations. The destruction of bison, attacks on Native communities, and suppression of resistance supported [[Western Settlement]]. These policies were not inevitable encounters between cultures; they were government-backed efforts to acquire territory and reorganize Native life.

## Sovereignty and Policy

The [[Dawes Act]] broke communal lands into individual allotments and opened “surplus” acreage to non-Native settlement, combining assimilation with further dispossession. Boarding schools attacked Native languages and family ties. The Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 reversed allotment in part, while postwar termination again sought to dissolve federal tribal relationships. Native activism later helped produce self-determination policies and renewed legal claims involving land, treaties, water, and jurisdiction. The history is therefore not a simple progression from defeat to inclusion: federal approaches changed, Native nations survived and adapted, and sovereignty remained contested within the constitutional system.

## Treaties, Sovereignty, and Expansion

The early United States dealt with Native nations through both diplomacy and warfare. Treaties recognized tribes as political communities capable of negotiating, but American officials often imposed unequal terms or failed to restrain settlers who crossed negotiated boundaries. Federal claims to land rested on doctrines that limited Indigenous title without extinguishing Native occupancy. As population and commodity production expanded, this legal structure helped convert Native homelands into property available to settlers.

The Constitution assigned important authority over Indian affairs to the federal government, yet states repeatedly sought control of Native land. The Cherokee cases and [[Indian Removal]] revealed the gap between legal recognition of tribal political status and enforcement. Removal did not end Native sovereignty; relocated nations rebuilt governments in Indian Territory while peoples who remained in the East continued to defend land and identity.

## Conquest, Assimilation, and Survival

After the Civil War, military campaigns and the destruction of food sources confined many western nations to reservations. Reservation boundaries and federal agents restricted movement and economic independence. The [[Dawes Act]] then divided communally held reservation land into individual allotments, presenting private property and farming as assimilation while transferring “surplus” land to non-Native owners. Boarding schools similarly attempted to suppress Indigenous languages, religions, and family ties.

Native resistance took many forms: armed defense, treaty negotiation, legal claims, religious renewal, cultural preservation, and adaptation to new economic circumstances. The [[Ghost Dance]] expressed hope for restoration under catastrophic pressure; the killing at Wounded Knee demonstrated the violence with which officials interpreted Indigenous movements. Survival should not be mistaken for consent to federal policy.

Twentieth-century policy shifted without following a straight line. The Indian Reorganization Act ended allotment and supported limited tribal self-government, while later termination policy tried to dissolve the federal relationship with selected tribes. Native activism helped produce a turn toward self-determination, stronger control of education and services, and renewed enforcement of treaty rights. Modern tribes remain governments, not racial interest groups, although jurisdiction is divided among tribal, federal, and state authorities. This history is therefore best understood as a continuing contest over land, sovereignty, and the meaning of treaty obligations.
