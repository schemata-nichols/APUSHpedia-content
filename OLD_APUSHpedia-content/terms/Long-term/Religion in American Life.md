> **Religion in American life** has shaped community, identity, reform, politics, and conflicts over authority from Indigenous traditions and colonial establishments to modern religious pluralism. The Constitution prohibited a national establishment and protected free exercise, but those principles did not remove religion from public life or end discrimination. Revivals expanded participation, congregations created institutions, and religious arguments supported both challenges to hierarchy and defenses of it. In APUSH, religion is best treated as a changing source of organization and meaning whose political effects depend on historical context rather than as an automatic force for either liberty or conservatism.

## APUSH Significance

Puritan New England linked church and civic order, while the [[First Great Awakening]] challenged established authority and created networks across colonies. After independence, disestablishment expanded denominational competition. The [[Second Great Awakening]] emphasized conversion and moral agency, helping energize [[Antebellum Reform]], abolition, temperance, and women's activism. At the same time, white Christians divided over slavery, and enslaved people developed religious practices that combined Christian teachings with communal survival and resistance.

## Pluralism and Politics

Immigration increased Catholic, Jewish, and later Muslim, Hindu, Buddhist, and other communities, often provoking [[Nativism]]. Black churches anchored organizing from [[Reconstruction]] through the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]]. In the late twentieth century, conservative evangelicals became an influential constituency in the [[Reagan Revolution]], especially on education, abortion, gender, and family policy. These developments show both continuity and change: religion repeatedly supplied voluntary organizations and moral language, but demographic diversity and constitutional disputes altered its public role. Religious freedom protected belief and practice unevenly, while debates over establishment asked when government accommodation becomes government endorsement.

## Revival, Voluntary Action, and Reform

The disestablishment of churches after independence did not remove religion from public life. It encouraged denominations to compete for adherents through preaching, publications, schools, missions, and voluntary societies. The [[Second Great Awakening]] flourished in this environment, teaching many Protestants that individuals could choose conversion and act to improve society. Its networks fed temperance, abolition, education, and other forms of [[Antebellum Reform]].

Religious conviction did not lead to one politics. Proslavery and antislavery Christians interpreted scripture differently, and disputes over slavery split major denominations along sectional lines. Black churches became autonomous institutions that sustained community, education, abolition, and later civil rights organizing. Women supplied much of the labor in religious associations while often being denied formal clerical authority. Religion could challenge hierarchy while reproducing it.

## Pluralism, Conflict, and Public Authority

Immigration increased religious diversity and provoked resistance. Nineteenth-century nativists portrayed Catholic loyalty as incompatible with republican government, and public schools frequently reflected Protestant practices. Jewish, Catholic, Orthodox Christian, Buddhist, Muslim, Hindu, and other communities created institutions that reshaped urban and national life. Legal toleration did not prevent social prejudice or demands that minorities conform to a Protestant cultural norm.

Constitutional conflicts usually concern government action, not a general exclusion of faith from politics. The First Amendment bars an establishment of religion and protects free exercise, creating tension when public funding, school practices, military service, or regulation affects religious belief. Court decisions gradually applied these protections against state governments through the Fourteenth Amendment. The resulting boundaries have changed with doctrine and circumstance.

In the twentieth century, religious activism supported both liberal and conservative causes. Black clergy and churches provided leadership and meeting spaces for the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]]. Later evangelical mobilization contributed to the rise of the Religious Right around education, abortion, gender, and family policy. Religion in American life is therefore neither steadily declining nor politically uniform. Its APUSH importance lies in how voluntary faith communities create identity and collective power while competing over whose moral claims should guide a religiously diverse republic.
