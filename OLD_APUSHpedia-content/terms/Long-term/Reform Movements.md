> **Reform Movements** are organized efforts to change institutions, laws, behavior, or distributions of power in response to perceived social problems. Across United States history, reformers have challenged [[Slavery|slavery]], alcohol consumption, gender inequality, industrial exploitation, political corruption, [[Segregation|segregation]], environmental damage, and other conditions. They have worked through churches, voluntary associations, journalism, [[Political Parties|political parties]], unions, litigation, direct action, and government administration. Reform is not automatically progressive or liberating: campaigns may expand rights, impose middle-class discipline, exclude marginalized groups, or strengthen state authority. For APUSH, reform movements reveal how economic transformation, religious belief, democratic participation, and crisis turn private grievances into collective political action.

## APUSH Significance

Reform movements connect social conditions to organized change:

1. **Movements require networks.** Churches, print, transportation, schools, unions, and community institutions allow ideas and resources to travel.
2. **Moral and material arguments interact.** Reformers may describe injustice as sin, violated rights, inefficient government, economic exploitation, or a threat to social order.
3. **Outsiders and officials influence one another.** Protest shifts public debate, while courts, parties, legislatures, and agencies determine institutional results.
4. **Reform creates opposition and compromise.** Beneficiaries of existing arrangements resist, and coalitions divide over goals, tactics, race, gender, and class.
5. **Achievement does not end conflict.** Laws can change formal rules without equal enforcement or removal of underlying power differences.

A reform movement differs from a spontaneous protest or isolated charity. It develops sustained organization and a theory—explicit or implicit—about what causes a problem and how collective action can address it.

## Religious and Revolutionary Foundations

The [[American Revolution]] itself drew on networks of committees, pamphlets, boycotts, and crowd action. Its language of [[Natural Rights|natural rights]] later gave reformers standards against which to judge slavery and inequality. At the same time, constitutional government directed many claims into petitions, elections, and legislation.

Religious communities supplied organizational experience and moral language. [[Quakers]] opposed slavery; evangelical revivals emphasized individual conversion and responsibility. Yet religious belief could also defend hierarchy. Reform movements must be studied through their specific coalitions rather than attributed to one common faith.

Early campaigns against slavery, alcohol, and unequal education established methods later movements repeated: local societies, national conventions, fundraising, lectures, newspapers, petitions, and personal testimony.

## Antebellum Reform

The [[Second Great Awakening]] and [[Market Revolution]] helped create the broadest early reform wave. Revivalism encouraged belief that individuals and society could overcome sin. Expanding print and transportation allowed voluntary associations to coordinate across communities, while [[Urbanization|urbanization]] and market disruption made poverty, disorder, and inequality more visible.

[[Antebellum Reform]] included temperance, common schools, prisons and asylums, [[Utopian Communities|utopian communities]], [[Abolitionism]], and [[Women's Rights|women's rights]]. These movements overlapped but did not form one program. Temperance could protect families from alcohol abuse while imposing Protestant middle-class standards. Schools widened access while promoting cultural conformity.

Abolition directly challenged property, racial power, and the Union. Women's antislavery work expanded public participation, while exclusion within reform organizations helped produce the [[Seneca Falls Convention]]. Reform therefore changed activists as well as institutions.

## Reconstruction and Industrial Reform

Emancipation created a federal reform project during [[Reconstruction]]. Constitutional amendments, the [[Freedmen's Bureau]], public schools, and biracial governments attempted to build freedom and [[Citizenship|citizenship]]. White-supremacist violence and retreat from enforcement sharply limited that transformation.

[[Industrialization]] then generated labor, agrarian, and urban movements. Workers formed unions for shorter hours, safer conditions, and bargaining power. [[Farmers' Alliances|Farmers' alliances]] and the [[Populism|Populist movement]] challenged railroad rates, debt, and financial policy. Settlement houses addressed urban conditions while often blending assistance with assimilation.

These movements disagreed over whether problems came from monopoly, class power, political corruption, personal behavior, or insufficient expertise. Their diagnoses shaped their proposed solutions.

## Progressivism and the [[New Deal]]

[[Progressivism]] brought journalists, social workers, labor activists, middle-class organizations, and politicians into campaigns for regulation and democratic reform. Achievements included food and drug standards, antitrust action, conservation, direct election of senators, and women's suffrage. Progressive governments also enforced segregation, eugenics, and cultural coercion, revealing that administrative reform could strengthen exclusion.

The [[Great Depression]] encouraged a larger federal response. New Deal programs regulated finance, protected collective bargaining, provided work relief, and established Social Security. Labor organizing helped make reform possible, while exclusions from key programs left many agricultural and domestic workers—disproportionately Black and Mexican American—without equal protection.

The New Deal illustrates the difference between a social movement and a governmental reform program. Movements created pressure and constituencies; officials translated some demands into policy while rejecting or narrowing others.

## Rights-Based and Modern Movements

The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] combined local organizing, litigation, boycotts, direct action, mass media, and federal pressure. Its victories against segregation and disfranchisement influenced women's, Chicano, Native, Asian American, LGBTQ, disability-rights, antiwar, and environmental movements.

Shared tactics did not erase distinct goals. Native activism often emphasized sovereignty and treaties; labor movements focused on workplace power; environmentalists sought regulation of public and private harm. Coalitions sometimes fractured over strategy, identity, and whether reform should seek inclusion within institutions or structural transformation.

Conservative activism also used movement organization. Anticommunist, religious, tax, gun-rights, and antiabortion groups built networks that reshaped the [[Republican Party]] and public policy. Reform describes efforts to change society, not one position on an ideological spectrum.

## Reform, Power, and Unintended Limits

Reform emerges when people define a condition as changeable, construct organizations, and identify targets with power to respond. Economic crises, wars, religious revivals, new media, and demographic change often create opportunities, but none produces reform automatically.

Success should be measured at several levels: altered debate, durable organizations, legal change, enforcement, and shifts in power. This approach captures both genuine achievements and the limits that remain after a movement wins formal recognition.
