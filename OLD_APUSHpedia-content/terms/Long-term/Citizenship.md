> **Citizenship** is formal membership in a political community, carrying legal rights, protections, and obligations. In United States history, its meaning developed through state practice, federal naturalization law, court decisions, constitutional amendments, conquest, [[Immigration|immigration]] policy, and [[Social Movements|social movements]]. Citizenship has never automatically guaranteed equal political or social power: people recognized as citizens have still faced disfranchisement, [[Segregation|segregation]], dispossession, or unequal treatment. For APUSH, citizenship is a struggle over both status and substance—who legally belongs to the nation, which government defines that belonging, and whether citizenship includes meaningful access to voting, [[Civil Rights|civil rights]], economic opportunity, and protection under law.

## APUSH Significance

Citizenship connects constitutional development to race, gender, [[Migration|migration]], and federal power:

1. **The founding left important questions unresolved.** The Constitution referred to citizens but did not provide one complete national definition of citizenship.
2. **Naturalization created racial boundaries.** The 1790 law limited naturalization to “free white persons,” linking legal membership to race.
3. **Civil War and Reconstruction transformed status.** Emancipation and the Fourteenth Amendment established birthright citizenship and national protection against state action.
4. **Formal status did not ensure equal rights.** Jim Crow, Native policy, Asian exclusion, and restrictions on women exposed gaps between citizenship and power.
5. **Movements expanded citizenship's substance.** Voting-rights, civil-rights, women's-rights, labor, and immigrant movements demanded that legal membership carry enforceable rights.

Citizenship should be distinguished from suffrage. A person may be a citizen without the right to vote, while the boundaries of voting have also depended on age, sex, race, residence, criminal law, and state administration.

## Membership in the Early Republic

During the colonial era, political membership depended on empire, colony, locality, property, religion, race, and legal status. Independence replaced British subjecthood with republican citizenship, but states continued to determine many civil and [[Voting Rights|voting rights]]. Most states confined regular political power to men, and racial restrictions grew even as property requirements for white men declined.

The Naturalization Act of 1790 created a federal route to citizenship for “free white persons” of good character. That rule encouraged European incorporation while excluding others from naturalization. Enslaved people were denied freedom and national membership, although free Black communities claimed rights through military service, taxation, petitioning, and participation in public life.

Native nations presented a different issue. They possessed political identities and treaty relationships that did not fit neatly into individual United States citizenship. Federal expansion increasingly constrained their sovereignty, and later policy often offered citizenship as part of coercive assimilation.

## [[Slavery]] and the Constitutional Crisis

The status of Black Americans became central to the sectional conflict. In [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], the Supreme Court declared that people of African descent could not be United States citizens under the Constitution and that Congress could not prohibit slavery in the territories. The ruling attempted to protect slaveholders' property claims while denying Black national membership.

The [[Civil War]] overturned that constitutional order. The [[Reconstruction Amendments|Thirteenth Amendment]] abolished slavery, but freedom alone did not define civil status. The [[Civil Rights Act of 1866]] declared people born in the United States citizens and protected specified civil rights. The Fourteenth Amendment then constitutionalized birthright citizenship and prohibited states from denying due process or equal protection.

This was a major shift in [[Federalism]]. National citizenship became a basis for federal action when states violated individual rights. The amendment did not immediately produce equality, but it supplied language later used against segregation and other discriminatory laws.

## Unequal Citizenship

During [[Reconstruction]], Black men voted, held office, and built institutions, but violence and political compromise weakened federal protection. Southern states imposed Jim Crow segregation and disfranchisement through poll taxes, literacy tests, grandfather clauses, and intimidation. Black Americans remained citizens while being denied much of citizenship's practical power.

Women were also citizens without national voting rights. The women's-rights movement sometimes invoked the Fourteenth Amendment, but the Supreme Court rejected the claim that citizenship itself guaranteed women's suffrage. The [[Women's Suffrage Movement|Nineteenth Amendment]] prohibited voting discrimination based on sex, though racial and administrative barriers continued to exclude many women.

Citizenship for Native Americans developed unevenly through treaties, statutes, allotment, and individual naturalization. The Indian Citizenship Act of 1924 extended United States citizenship broadly to Native people born in the country without eliminating tribal citizenship. Some states nevertheless continued to prevent Native voting.

Asian immigrants encountered explicit racial restrictions. Chinese exclusion limited entry and naturalization, while courts interpreted whiteness requirements to exclude other Asian applicants. Birthright citizenship, affirmed in _United States v. Wong Kim Ark_, protected U.S.-born children even when their parents could not naturalize.

## Civil Rights and Immigration

The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] made equal citizenship a national political demand. _[[Brown v. Board of Education]]_, the [[Civil Rights Act of 1964]], the [[Voting Rights Act of 1965]], and the Twenty-Fourth Amendment used federal power to attack segregation and disfranchisement. These victories expanded enforcement rather than creating citizenship for the first time.

Immigration law continued to determine who could enter and eventually naturalize. National-origin quotas favored northern and western Europeans until the [[Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965]] created a system emphasizing family reunification and employment categories. Modern debates over undocumented residents, refugees, dual nationality, and birthright citizenship continue to separate legal status from residence and community membership.

## Membership, Rights, and Enforcement

Citizenship has at least three related dimensions: legal nationality, civil rights, and political participation. Treating them as identical hides how a government can recognize membership while denying equal schools, ballots, property, movement, or protection.

The broad historical change has been toward a more national and inclusive legal definition. Yet implementation has depended on courts, Congress, presidents, states, and organized citizens. Citizenship is therefore both a constitutional status and an ongoing conflict over whether membership produces equal power in practice.
