> The **labor movement** is the long effort by workers to improve wages, hours, safety, dignity, and bargaining power through unions, strikes, political action, and legislation. It grew as the [[Market Revolution]] and [[Industrialization]] moved more workers into wage labor and concentrated authority in employers. Workers sometimes built broad alliances, but divisions of skill, race, gender, immigration status, industry, and ideology repeatedly limited solidarity. For APUSH, labor history reveals that industrial growth was negotiated through conflict rather than simply produced by invention and investment.

## APUSH Significance

Early craft unions and workingmen's parties challenged wage dependence, while national organizations grew after the Civil War. The [[Knights of Labor]] attempted a broad coalition, whereas the [[American Federation of Labor]] emphasized skilled workers and immediate workplace gains. Struggles at Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman showed the power of corporations and the frequent use of courts, police, troops, and injunctions against strikes. Employers also exploited racial and ethnic divisions, and many unions excluded Black workers, women, Asian immigrants, or unskilled laborers.

## Power, Law, and Change

The [[Progressivism|Progressive Era]] brought limited protective laws, but federal support changed most dramatically during the [[New Deal]]. The Wagner Act protected collective bargaining, and industrial unions organized mass-production workers through the Congress of Industrial Organizations. Union membership and contracts helped many workers enter the postwar middle class, though agricultural and domestic workers—disproportionately Black and Latino—were excluded from major protections. Since the late twentieth century, deindustrialization, globalization, employer resistance, and policy changes have weakened union density. The movement's history connects class conflict to state power and shows that gains for workers have always depended on who could organize and be recognized as part of “labor.”

## Organization and Employer Resistance

Workers have organized at the workplace, by craft, across an industry, and through broad political movements. Craft unions could defend scarce skills but often excluded the mass of industrial laborers. Industrial unions attempted to unite everyone in a workplace or industry, making them better suited to factories organized around semiskilled production. Employers countered with lockouts, private guards, replacement workers, blacklists, yellow-dog contracts, court injunctions, and associations that coordinated opposition to unions.

The state did not simply stand outside these conflicts. Officials deployed militia or federal troops during major nineteenth-century strikes, and courts treated some collective action as conspiracy or restraint of trade. During the [[Progressivism|Progressive Era]], states began regulating hours, compensation, and safety, although courts sometimes struck down these laws in the name of liberty of contract. The Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire demonstrated how organizing, journalism, and catastrophe could build support for regulation.

## From the New Deal to Decline

The [[Great Depression]] weakened workers' bargaining position but discredited older assumptions that labor conflict should be governed chiefly by employers and courts. The Wagner Act protected collective bargaining and created a federal enforcement framework. The Congress of Industrial Organizations then organized steel, automobile, rubber, and other mass-production workers, using tactics such as the sit-down strike. Union membership and wartime production helped many workers gain wages and benefits that supported the postwar middle class.

Those gains were incomplete. Agricultural and domestic workers—occupations containing many Black and Mexican American workers—were initially excluded from major New Deal labor protections. Racial discrimination persisted within unions, even as organizers such as A. Philip Randolph used labor power to press for fair employment. Women entered industrial work in large numbers during wars but were often pushed toward lower-paid jobs or out of plants afterward.

The Taft–Hartley Act restricted union practices, and from the late twentieth century deindustrialization, employer opposition, automation, globalization, and the growth of less-unionized service work weakened organized labor. Public-sector unions expanded even as private-sector membership fell. The movement's history therefore combines real improvements with unequal access and recurring vulnerability: legal rights help workers organize, but economic structure and political power determine how usable those rights are.
