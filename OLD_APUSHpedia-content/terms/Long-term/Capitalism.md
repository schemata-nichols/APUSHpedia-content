> **Capitalism** is an economic system in which private owners control much productive property, goods and labor are exchanged through markets, and investment is organized in pursuit of profit. In United States history, capitalism developed through Atlantic commerce, settler access to land, enslaved and [[Wage Labor|wage labor]], financial institutions, industrialization, corporations, and government policy. It generated extraordinary production, innovation, and consumer choice while distributing wealth and bargaining power unequally. For APUSH, capitalism should not be treated as government absence or as identical to industrialization. Its development depended on law, infrastructure, territorial acquisition, and public regulation, and it repeatedly produced conflicts over labor freedom, monopoly, race, social welfare, and the proper economic role of government.

## APUSH Significance

Capitalism provides a framework for connecting major economic and social changes:

1. **Markets expanded through policy and infrastructure.** Governments protected property, regulated money, transferred land, and financed or authorized transportation.
2. **Labor systems changed but remained unequal.** Enslaved labor, household production, independent farming, and wage labor coexisted and supported connected markets.
3. **Industrialization concentrated capital.** Factories and corporations increased production while creating new relationships between owners, managers, and workers.
4. **Economic growth generated political conflict.** Debates over banks, tariffs, monopoly, unions, taxation, and regulation shaped party systems and [[Reform Movements|reform movements]].
5. **Crises expanded government responsibility.** Financial panics and the Great Depression challenged the belief that markets could regulate themselves adequately.

The essential distinction is between capitalism and a completely unregulated economy. American capitalism has always operated through government-created rules, even when political leaders disagreed sharply over which interests those rules should protect.

## Commercial Foundations

Colonial economies participated in an Atlantic system of trade, credit, land speculation, and commodity production. Merchants pursued profit across imperial boundaries, while plantations produced tobacco, rice, indigo, and sugar-related goods through enslaved labor. [[Mercantilism]] attempted to direct that commerce toward imperial goals, but it did not eliminate private enterprise.

After independence, federal and state governments created conditions for a national market. The Constitution protected contracts, authorized taxation and commerce regulation, and prevented states from issuing their own currencies. [[Alexander Hamilton]]'s financial program funded the national debt and created the [[First Bank of the United States]], connecting public credit to private investment. Critics feared that financiers and speculators would gain power over farmers and local communities.

Territorial growth also mattered. Federal land policy transferred Native homelands into surveyed property available to settlers and speculators. Capitalist development therefore depended not only on voluntary exchange but also on state power and dispossession.

## The Market Revolution and [[Slavery]]

The [[Market Revolution]] connected farms and towns through canals, roads, steamboats, railroads, banks, and the [[Telegraph|telegraph]]. Commercial farmers produced for distant buyers, factories centralized some manufacturing, and wage labor grew. These changes expanded opportunity and consumption but made households more vulnerable to market prices, unemployment, and financial panics.

Capitalism did not displace slavery during this expansion. The [[Cotton Kingdom]] grew as international textile demand made enslaved labor and land increasingly profitable. Enslaved people were treated simultaneously as workers, property, and collateral. Northern merchants, insurers, shippers, banks, and manufacturers benefited from slave-produced cotton. The free-labor North and slaveholding South developed different labor ideologies while remaining economically connected.

That relationship explains why capitalism and slavery should neither be treated as identical nor placed in separate stories. Capital accumulation operated through several labor systems, and political conflict centered on which would expand westward.

## [[Industrial Capitalism]]

After the [[Civil War]], railroads created national networks for goods, capital, labor, and information. Corporations mobilized investment on a scale beyond most individual proprietors. Managers integrated production, while new technologies increased output in steel, oil, electricity, and consumer goods. [[Immigration]] and [[Migration|migration]] supplied industrial labor and accelerated urban growth.

Supporters celebrated entrepreneurs and competition. Critics pointed to railroad discrimination, political corruption, dangerous work, long hours, low pay, and the market power of trusts. Workers organized unions and strikes to bargain collectively, but employers used courts, private guards, and government force against many labor actions. Race, gender, and immigration status divided labor markets and often weakened solidarity.

Farmers also confronted railroad rates, debt, and falling commodity prices. The [[Populism|Populist movement]] demanded currency reform, railroad regulation, and greater democratic control over economic institutions. These conflicts show that industrial capitalism reorganized political power as well as production.

## Reform, Crisis, and the State

Progressive reformers accepted much industrial development but sought rules for its abuses. Antitrust laws, railroad regulation, food and drug standards, workplace protections, and income taxation expanded public oversight. Courts sometimes limited regulation by treating freedom of contract as a constitutional right, producing debate over whose economic liberty the law protected.

The [[Great Depression]] created the system's deepest crisis. Bank failures, unemployment, and collapsed demand discredited limited federal responses. The [[New Deal]] regulated finance, recognized organized labor, provided employment and relief, and established Social Security. It did not replace capitalism; it reshaped the relationship among markets, citizens, and the federal government.

After [[World War II]], mass consumption, union contracts, federal spending, [[Suburbanization|suburbanization]], and global corporate expansion supported broad growth. Since the 1970s, [[Deindustrialization|deindustrialization]], [[Deregulation|deregulation]], declining union membership, finance, technology, and [[Globalization|globalization]] have again altered where jobs and wealth are produced.

## Growth, Power, and Public Regulation

Capitalism is broader than [[Industrialization]], which describes the mechanization and concentration of production. It is also compatible with different degrees of regulation and social provision. The recurring APUSH issue is not simply “markets or government,” because public authority constructs markets through property, contract, currency, infrastructure, and labor law.

Its history combines growth with uneven power. Capitalism created mobility and abundant goods for many Americans while relying at different moments on dispossession, slavery, low-wage labor, and unequal access to credit and property. Reform repeatedly changed the rules without settling the conflict between private accumulation and democratic accountability.
