> **Industrialization** is the transformation of production through machinery, concentrated workplaces, new energy sources, and increasingly specialized [[Wage Labor|wage labor]]. In the United States, it began unevenly in the early nineteenth-century Northeast, accelerated into a national system after the [[Civil War]], and later shifted toward mass production, services, and high technology. Industrialization increased output, lowered the price of many goods, encouraged [[Urbanization|urbanization]], and helped make the United States a major world power. It also produced dangerous work, environmental damage, economic concentration, labor conflict, and regional inequality. For APUSH, industrialization is not merely a sequence of inventions; it is a reorganization of work, capital, communities, government, and the environment.

## APUSH Significance

Industrialization connects economic change to [[Migration|migration]], class, politics, and federal policy:

1. **Technology changed production only when joined to capital and labor.** Machines, factories, transportation, finance, and workers formed an integrated system.
2. **Regions industrialized differently.** The Northeast led early manufacturing, while the South remained tied to slave-grown cotton and the West supplied food and raw materials.
3. **Wage labor created new opportunities and dependencies.** Workers gained income but lost some control over pace, hours, and conditions.
4. **Industrial growth encouraged migration and urbanization.** Immigrants and internal migrants supplied labor and built new communities around workplaces.
5. **Concentrated economic power generated reform.** Unions, farmers, Progressives, and New Dealers demanded regulation, bargaining rights, and social protection.

Industrialization should be distinguished from the broader [[Market Revolution]]. The Market Revolution expanded commercial exchange, transportation, and finance; industrialization more specifically changed how goods were produced.

## Early Manufacturing

Colonial households and artisans produced many goods through skilled hand labor, while Atlantic commerce connected farms and plantations to distant markets. British restrictions and the disruption of the [[War of 1812]] encouraged domestic manufacturing, especially textiles. Francis Cabot Lowell's integrated mills brought spinning and weaving into centralized factories powered by water.

The [[Lowell System]] initially recruited young women from New England farm families and housed them in supervised boardinghouses. Wages offered limited independence, but owners controlled hours, discipline, and machinery. As conditions deteriorated, workers petitioned and struck; later employers increasingly relied on immigrant families.

[[Interchangeable Parts|Interchangeable parts]], machine tools, and standardized production expanded in firearms and other industries. Yet early industrialization remained geographically limited. Most Americans still farmed, and artisans, household labor, and small workshops continued alongside factories.

## Transportation, Cotton, and [[Regional Development]]

Canals, steamboats, railroads, and the [[Telegraph|telegraph]] reduced the cost and time of moving goods and information. The [[Erie Canal]] linked western farms to northeastern markets, while railroads eventually created more flexible national routes. Financial institutions supplied credit, and patent law encouraged invention.

Industrialization tied free and enslaved labor together. Textile mills consumed cotton produced by enslaved workers in the [[Cotton Kingdom]]. Northern merchants, shippers, insurers, and banks participated in this trade. Manufacturing therefore did not automatically weaken [[Slavery]]; before the Civil War, it helped make slave-grown cotton more valuable.

The Civil War accelerated federal support for development through the [[Homestead Act]], [[Pacific Railway Act]], and [[Morrill Land-Grant Act]]. Railroad construction integrated western land and resources into national markets while intensifying Native dispossession.

## The Rise of Industrial [[Capitalism]]

After 1865, [[Industrial Capitalism]] expanded through abundant resources, population growth, investment, patents, and a large national market. Railroads coordinated time zones and connected mines, farms, factories, and cities. Steel, petroleum, electricity, chemicals, and communications created new industries and business organizations.

Corporations raised capital from many investors and separated ownership from professional management. Horizontal and [[Vertical Integration|vertical integration]] allowed firms such as Standard Oil and Carnegie Steel to control competitors, supplies, transportation, and distribution. Supporters praised scale and lower costs; critics attacked trusts, political influence, and inequality.

Industrial labor was divided by skill, race, ethnicity, and gender. Work could offer mobility, but factories and mines imposed long hours, injury, periodic unemployment, and managerial discipline. The [[Knights of Labor]], [[American Federation of Labor]], and industrial unions pursued different organizing strategies. Major strikes often brought injunctions, troops, or police intervention on behalf of employers.

## Reform and Mass Production

Farmers and workers pressed government to regulate railroads and corporations. The [[Interstate Commerce Act]] and [[Sherman Antitrust Act]] created federal authority that initially remained limited. Progressive reform later expanded antitrust action, workplace regulation, and consumer protection, though courts sometimes struck down labor laws.

The assembly line and scientific management increased output in the early twentieth century. Ford's automobiles became symbols of mass production and consumption, while repetitive work reduced worker control. Electricity and consumer credit changed homes and leisure as well as factories.

During the [[Great Depression]], industrial collapse produced mass unemployment. The [[New Deal]] regulated finance, supported collective bargaining, and established social insurance. [[World War II]] mobilization then joined corporations, workers, science, and federal spending on an unprecedented scale, making the United States an industrial superpower.

## [[Deindustrialization]] and Change

After 1945, productivity and consumption rose, but automation and global competition eventually reduced many manufacturing jobs. Factories moved from older northeastern and midwestern centers to suburbs, the South, other countries, or more automated facilities. The decline of unionized employment weakened many industrial communities.

Industrialization did not simply end; it changed form. Computing, logistics, energy, biotechnology, and service industries rely on industrial systems and global supply chains. Environmental movements also exposed long-term costs in pollution, resource extraction, and climate change.

## Productivity, Labor, and Unequal Change

American industrialization combined private enterprise with public action. Governments enforced contracts, supplied land, built or subsidized infrastructure, protected tariffs, educated workers, and regulated markets. The recurring conflict was over which groups would receive the benefits and bear the costs.

Its history is therefore one of growth and unequal power. Industrialization made possible higher output, global influence, and new consumer choices while creating class conflict, regional disruption, and environmental damage. Those consequences explain why technological change repeatedly became a political issue.
