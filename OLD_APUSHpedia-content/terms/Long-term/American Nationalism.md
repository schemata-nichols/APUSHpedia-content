> **American nationalism** is the belief that people of the United States share a distinct political community, identity, and national interest. It grew from the [[American Revolution]], the [[United States Constitution]], territorial expansion, wars, mass culture, and federal institutions, but Americans repeatedly disagreed over who belonged and whether loyalty to nation should outweigh region, state, race, or ethnic identity. In APUSH, nationalism is therefore both a unifying force and a contested claim: it supported independence and national development while also justifying conquest, exclusion, and demands for cultural conformity.

## APUSH Significance

Nationalism helps explain why events that strengthened the Union could also deepen conflict. After the [[War of 1812]], the [[Era of Good Feelings]], [[American System]], and [[Marshall Court]] encouraged national economic and constitutional integration. Yet the same period saw disputes over tariffs, slavery, and federal authority. Expansion through [[Manifest Destiny]] celebrated a national mission while dispossessing Native peoples and intensifying the [[Sectional Crisis]]. During the [[Civil War]], Union victory and emancipation redefined the nation, but [[Reconstruction]] exposed disagreement over whether national citizenship required federal protection of equal rights.

## Changing Meanings

Later nationalism accompanied overseas expansion in the 1890s, mobilization during [[World War I]] and [[World War II]], and Cold War claims that the United States defended democracy. These moments created common purpose but also encouraged coercion, including wartime repression and pressure to assimilate. Civil rights activists appealed to national ideals in the [[Declaration of Independence]] and Constitution to expose unequal citizenship. American nationalism is thus not identical to patriotism or federal power; it is an argument about the nation's people, purposes, and obligations whose meaning changes as excluded groups claim inclusion.

## Symbols, Institutions, and Belonging

National feeling became durable through institutions and repeated public practices. Schools taught national history, elections asked voters to imagine a shared public interest, and newspapers circulated arguments across state lines. The flag, national holidays, monuments, military service, and celebrations of the founding supplied a common symbolic language. The federal government also made nationality tangible through the postal system, courts, currency, military, and infrastructure. These mechanisms never produced agreement automatically. Competing parties and movements instead fought to define which founding principles and historical memories represented the nation.

Belonging was especially contested during periods of mass [[Immigration]]. Nativists sometimes defined the nation as white, Protestant, and culturally Anglo-American, while immigrants combined American citizenship with ethnic, linguistic, or religious identities. Federal policies including Chinese exclusion and wartime restrictions turned narrow definitions of loyalty into law. At the same time, abolitionists, suffragists, civil rights activists, and Indigenous leaders used national promises to expose exclusion. Their claims show that nationalism could sustain hierarchy or become a language for demanding equal membership.

## Nationalism, Section, and the World

Nationalism does not eliminate sectional or group loyalties. Before the Civil War, both Unionists and secessionists appealed to the Revolution, constitutionalism, and self-government while drawing opposite conclusions. The Union victory established that states could not leave the nation unilaterally, and the Reconstruction Amendments made national citizenship a potential basis for federal protection. The retreat from Reconstruction demonstrated that national institutions could still abandon those commitments.

In foreign affairs, nationalism often framed expansion and war as tests of national destiny or honor. It supported the [[Monroe Doctrine]], continental expansion, overseas empire, wartime mobilization, and Cold War leadership. Yet dissenters also described opposition to conquest or repression as fidelity to American principles. The recurring APUSH issue is therefore not whether nationalism existed, but **which people, territory, institutions, and ideals a particular version of the nation privileged**.
