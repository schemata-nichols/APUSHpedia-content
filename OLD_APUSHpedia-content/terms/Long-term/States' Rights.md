> **States' rights** is the argument that state governments retain substantial constitutional authority and may resist or limit federal action. It arises from [[Federalism]], which divides power between state and national governments, but it has never had one fixed policy meaning. Advocates have invoked state authority to oppose national banks, tariffs, civil rights enforcement, and regulatory programs, while also demanding federal power when it served other interests. For APUSH, states' rights should be analyzed as a historically situated constitutional claim, not accepted as a complete explanation without asking which policy, power, and group it protected.

## APUSH Significance

The [[Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions]] argued that states could judge the constitutionality of the Alien and Sedition Acts. South Carolina advanced a stronger doctrine during the [[Nullification Crisis]], and slaveholding states later used state sovereignty to defend slavery and secession. Yet the Confederate government also centralized power during the [[Civil War]], revealing that commitment to state authority yielded when military survival demanded national action. Reconstruction then made federal enforcement of citizenship and equal protection a central conflict between national rights and state control.

## A Flexible Argument

In the twentieth century, defenders of segregation used states' rights against *[[Brown v. Board of Education]]* and federal civil rights laws. At other times, states acted ahead of the federal government on labor standards, environmental regulation, or civil rights. The Constitution does reserve important powers to states, so the phrase is not inherently a code for one cause; its meaning comes from use. The best APUSH analysis identifies the constitutional mechanism—reserved power, concurrent power, nullification, or interposition—and then evaluates how the argument distributed authority and affected individual rights.

## Constitutional Origins and Sectional Uses

The Constitution divided authority without drawing a permanent line between state and national power. Enumerated federal powers, the Necessary and Proper Clause, the Supremacy Clause, and the Tenth Amendment supplied competing textual arguments. During the [[Ratification Debate]], Federalists emphasized the need for an effective Union while Anti-Federalists feared remote consolidated power. Early disputes over the bank and the Alien and Sedition Acts quickly turned those concerns into party conflict.

Sectional crises raised the stakes. South Carolina claimed a state could nullify a federal tariff during the [[Nullification Crisis]], but President Jackson rejected unilateral nullification and Congress paired a Force Bill with compromise. Slaveholding states later demanded strong federal enforcement of the Fugitive Slave Act even while invoking state sovereignty against restrictions on slavery. After Lincoln's election, secessionists claimed a right to leave the Union, and the Civil War settled the practical question of unilateral secession against them.

## Rights, Resistance, and Federal Enforcement

The Reconstruction Amendments expanded national responsibility for citizenship, due process, equal protection, and voting. White southern governments then used states' rights language to resist federal civil rights enforcement and construct the [[Jim Crow System]]. During the twentieth-century civil rights struggle, segregationists again defended “local control,” while activists argued that federal intervention was necessary because states were violating individual constitutional rights.

States have also invoked autonomy for causes unrelated to racial hierarchy. Northern personal-liberty laws resisted federal fugitive-slave enforcement; states have pioneered labor, environmental, welfare, and civil-rights policies beyond national requirements. At other times, reformers have used federalism to defend local experimentation or limit executive and regulatory power. These changes show why states' rights cannot be assigned consistently to conservatism or liberalism.

The phrase can obscure the substantive dispute. States do not possess interests separate from the people and institutions acting through them, and state power can either protect liberty or enforce inequality. A precise APUSH explanation identifies **which state authority, against which federal action, for whose benefit**, then considers how the argument fits the constitutional allocation of power.
