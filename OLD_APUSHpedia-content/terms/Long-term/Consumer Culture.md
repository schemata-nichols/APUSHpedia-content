> **Consumer culture** describes a society in which purchasing goods and services becomes a major way people express status, identity, comfort, and belonging. It developed unevenly as the [[Market Revolution]], mass production, advertising, department stores, installment credit, and rising incomes expanded access to commodities. Consumption could offer convenience and choice, particularly during the [[Roaring Twenties]] and [[Postwar Economic Boom]], but access remained divided by class, race, gender, and geography. For APUSH, consumer culture connects economic growth to changing social identities while revealing the labor and environmental costs hidden behind abundance.

## APUSH Significance

The nineteenth-century [[Market Economy]] moved more household needs into commercial exchange, while industrialization lowered the price of many manufactured goods. By the late nineteenth century, mail-order catalogs and chain stores linked rural and urban consumers to national markets. In the 1920s, automobiles, radios, national advertising, and consumer credit helped create shared mass culture, though farmers and many workers did not share equally in prosperity. The [[Great Depression]] exposed the fragility of consumption based on income and credit, and [[New Deal]] policies strengthened purchasing power and consumer protections without ending inequality.

## Abundance and Its Limits

After [[World War II]], high wages for many unionized workers, suburbanization, federal housing policy, and mass-produced appliances expanded middle-class consumption. Racial discrimination in credit and housing, however, restricted access to that prosperity, while women's paid and unpaid labor remained central to household consumption. From the late twentieth century, globalization made goods cheaper while relocating production and weakening some domestic industries. Consumer culture should therefore be distinguished from capitalism as a whole: it concerns how people use markets and goods to organize everyday life, even as those choices depend on unequal systems of production, finance, and access.

## Building Mass Consumption

Consumer culture required more than factories producing large quantities of goods. Railroads and national distribution networks moved products, urban department stores displayed them, and mail-order firms such as Sears connected isolated households to a common market. Brand names and packaging encouraged buyers to trust unfamiliar producers. Advertising increasingly sold emotional meanings—modernity, respectability, beauty, freedom—alongside practical utility. Installment plans made costly goods immediately available while committing households to future payments.

The automobile demonstrates these connections. Mass production lowered its price, petroleum and road construction supported its use, advertising presented ownership as independence, and credit widened the pool of purchasers. Cars reshaped dating, tourism, suburbs, retailing, and the environment while weakening some older transportation systems. Consumption was consequently a driver of social and geographic change, not merely the final stage of production.

## Identity, Citizenship, and Criticism

Consumption could provide forms of participation to people denied equal political or workplace authority. Women exercised purchasing power for households and became targets of advertising, although marketers often reinforced domestic gender roles. Black consumers and newspapers organized boycotts and “buy Black” campaigns, turning spending into a tool against discrimination. During the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]], selective-buying campaigns pressured segregated businesses by connecting market access to citizenship.

Government helped construct consumer abundance. Wartime rationing defined responsible consumption, New Deal regulation addressed banking and securities, and postwar mortgage, highway, and veterans' policies subsidized particular forms of home and automobile ownership. These benefits were distributed unequally through segregation and discrimination. Since the late twentieth century, credit cards, digital retail, and global supply chains have widened choice while increasing household debt, waste, surveillance, and distance between consumers and producers.

Critics have therefore attacked consumer culture from different directions: reformers condemned deceptive or unsafe products, labor activists exposed working conditions, environmentalists emphasized resource use and disposal, and cultural critics worried that citizenship was being reduced to purchasing. The important distinction is between **greater access to goods**, which can improve material life, and **equal economic power**, which mass consumption has never guaranteed.
