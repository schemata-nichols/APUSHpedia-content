> **Social movements** are sustained collective efforts by people outside or across established institutions to change laws, public policy, culture, or social power. Movements use tactics such as petitions, meetings, boycotts, strikes, litigation, direct action, print, and electoral pressure. They emerge from grievances, but organization, leadership, communication networks, political opportunities, and opposition shape their effects. In APUSH, social movements connect people often excluded from formal authority to institutional change, while showing that participants may share a broad goal yet disagree about strategy, leadership, and the meaning of equality.

## APUSH Significance

The [[Abolitionism|abolitionist movement]], [[Women's Rights|women's-rights movement]], and [[Labor Movement]] built voluntary organizations that challenged slavery, gender inequality, and employer power. They borrowed tactics from one another but also reproduced racial, class, and gender exclusions. In the twentieth century, the [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] combined local organizing, litigation, mass protest, federal lobbying, and media strategy to attack the [[Jim Crow System]]. Its successes helped inspire movements for Chicano, Native, women's, gay and lesbian, disability, and environmental rights.

## Change and Limits

Movements rarely act alone: courts, parties, legislatures, presidents, businesses, and countermovements determine whether demands become enforceable policy. The Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act, for example, required federal power even though grassroots activism made them politically possible. Success can also produce internal division as participants debate reform versus structural transformation. A movement is therefore not identical to a single organization or protest. Its historical importance lies in how networks define a problem, turn private experience into public claims, widen political participation, and sometimes alter institutions even when their full goals remain unmet.

## Organization, Strategy, and Political Opportunity

Grievances alone do not create sustained movements. Activists need organizations, leaders, communication networks, money, meeting places, and shared explanations of what is wrong. Churches, unions, newspapers, colleges, neighborhood groups, and voluntary associations have repeatedly supplied those resources. Movements also frame demands in recognizable language—natural rights, citizenship, religious duty, economic justice, or national ideals—to recruit supporters and pressure institutions.

Strategies reflect both goals and constraints. Petitioning and lobbying seek access to existing authorities; litigation asks courts to reinterpret law; boycotts and strikes impose economic costs; marches and civil disobedience dramatize injustice; electoral organizing attempts to replace officials. Movements frequently combine methods. The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]], for example, joined local organizing, direct action, court challenges, media strategy, voter registration, and federal lobbying rather than advancing through a single protest or leader.

## Coalitions, Opposition, and Outcomes

Movements must build coalitions among people whose interests are not identical. Abolition linked Black activists, religious reformers, formerly enslaved speakers, and antislavery politicians, but conflicts over race, gender, violence, and the Constitution divided participants. Women's-rights movements similarly debated whether to emphasize voting, workplace equality, reproductive freedom, or differences of race and class. Such disagreements are not side stories; they shape which demands and leaders gain visibility.

Opponents organize too. Governments may repress activists, employers may fire organizers, and countermovements may defend established institutions or reverse reforms. Media coverage can broaden sympathy or portray protest as disorder. The surrounding political environment matters: war, economic crisis, party competition, court vacancies, or federalism can open and close opportunities for change.

Success also takes several forms. A movement may win a law, change enforcement, reshape public language, build durable institutions, or inspire later activism. Formal victories rarely settle the underlying conflict: the Reconstruction Amendments did not prevent Jim Crow, and civil rights legislation did not eliminate structural inequality. Social movements demonstrate that outsiders can alter public power, but outcomes usually reflect interaction among grassroots pressure, institutional allies, organized opposition, and the limits of implementation.
