> **Race and Racism** describe the creation of social categories based on supposed inherited differences and the use of those categories to distribute power, rights, resources, and status unequally. Race is not a fixed biological division; in United States history, governments, courts, employers, scientists, and communities repeatedly defined and changed racial boundaries. Racism operated through beliefs and individual actions, but also through [[Slavery|slavery]], conquest, [[Immigration|immigration]] law, [[Segregation|segregation]], housing, education, labor markets, and [[Citizenship|citizenship]] policy. For APUSH, race is not a separate topic added to political or economic history. It is a system that shaped territorial expansion, labor, [[Democracy|democracy]], state power, and the meaning of [[American Identity|American identity]]—and one continually challenged by affected communities.

## APUSH Significance

Race and racism connect social classification to institutions and material interests:

1. **Racial categories developed historically.** Colonial law hardened distinctions among Europeans, Africans, and Native peoples rather than merely recording natural groups.
2. **Race organized labor and land.** Enslavement, dispossession, exclusion, and occupational segregation directed wealth and opportunity toward some groups.
3. **Law both created and challenged hierarchy.** Citizenship statutes, court rulings, segregation, immigration restriction, and civil-rights laws changed racial status.
4. **Racial systems differed across groups.** Black enslavement, Native sovereignty and removal, Asian exclusion, and the changing classification of European and Latino populations were related but not identical.
5. **Resistance shaped every period.** Enslaved people, Native nations, immigrants, and civil-rights organizations contested racial power through culture, law, politics, [[Migration|migration]], labor, and protest.

The essential distinction is between **prejudice**, an attitude, and **racism**, which also concerns power and institutional outcomes. Prejudice can sustain systems, but unequal rules may persist without openly prejudiced language.

## Colonial Construction of Race

European colonizers encountered diverse Native nations with distinct political identities. Colonists nevertheless developed broad racial descriptions that portrayed Native peoples as inferior or uncivilized, helping justify conquest, missionary activity, land seizure, and removal. Native nations negotiated, adapted, allied, and resisted rather than simply accepting colonial categories.

The growth of plantation slavery made race central to labor law. Early colonies used several forms of bound labor, but statutes increasingly made African ancestry associated with permanent, hereditary enslavement. Enslaved status passed through the mother, conversion did not guarantee freedom, and prohibitions on interracial relations helped separate Europeans from Africans.

These laws gave poor and wealthy Europeans a shared legal status as white even when class differences remained substantial. Race therefore developed alongside economic interests and state authority.

## Revolution, Expansion, and Slavery

The Revolution's language of [[Natural Rights|natural rights]] encouraged challenges to slavery, and northern states adopted gradual emancipation. Yet the Constitution protected slaveholding interests through representation, fugitive-slave provisions, and delay of a ban on the international trade. The Naturalization Act of 1790 limited naturalization to free white persons.

Continental expansion joined race to national mission. [[Indian Removal]] presented Native sovereignty as an obstacle to white settlement and the [[Cotton Kingdom]]. War with Mexico brought new land and Mexican residents under United States rule; racial claims shaped conquest and the unequal treatment that followed.

As slavery expanded, defenders increasingly described it as a positive good grounded in white supremacy. In [[Dred Scott v. Sandford]], the Supreme Court denied national citizenship to Black people and protected slaveholders' claims in the territories. Abolitionists and Black activists answered by linking emancipation to equality and citizenship.

## [[Reconstruction]] and Jim Crow

The [[Civil War]] destroyed slavery, and the [[Reconstruction Amendments]] established national citizenship, equal protection, and [[Voting Rights|voting rights]] for Black men. Black political participation and institution-building challenged the racial order. White-supremacist organizations responded with terrorism, while state governments created labor controls and later segregation.

After Reconstruction, the [[Jim Crow System]] used disfranchisement, separate facilities, violence, and custom to enforce white rule. _[[Plessy v. Ferguson]]_ gave constitutional approval to “separate but equal.” Convict leasing and debt arrangements connected racial control to labor exploitation.

Scientific racism and eugenics gave hierarchy a modern vocabulary. Such ideas supported immigration restriction, forced sterilization, segregation, and claims that unequal outcomes reflected inherited capacity rather than policy.

## Immigration and Changing Whiteness

Irish, Italian, Jewish, Slavic, and other European immigrants encountered [[Nativism|nativism]] and were sometimes described as separate or inferior races. Over generations, many gained fuller access to whiteness through citizenship, political incorporation, residential mobility, and distinction from Black and Asian Americans.

Asian immigrants faced exclusion from naturalization, segregated schooling, land ownership, and immigration. The [[Chinese Exclusion Act]] established federal nationality-based restriction, and Japanese American incarceration during [[World War II]] showed how citizenship could fail under racialized security claims.

Mexican Americans occupied a shifting legal position. Many were classified as white in law while experiencing school segregation, labor exploitation, deportation campaigns, and discrimination. These histories demonstrate that racial categories operate through context rather than one uniform rule.

## [[Civil Rights]] and Structural Inequality

The [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] attacked legal segregation through litigation, organizing, direct action, and federal legislation. _[[Brown v. Board of Education]]_, the [[Civil Rights Act of 1964]], and the [[Voting Rights Act of 1965]] dismantled major legal supports of Jim Crow.

Ending explicit discrimination did not erase accumulated inequality. Federal housing policy, restrictive covenants, redlining, suburban development, school boundaries, employment discrimination, and unequal access to credit had shaped wealth and geography. Later formally neutral policies could preserve unequal effects.

Black Power, Native, Chicano, Asian American, and other movements challenged both discrimination and pressures to accept white cultural standards. Their goals included community control, sovereignty, fair labor, political representation, and recognition of distinct histories.

## Constructed Hierarchy and Structural Change

Racial categories changed, but racism showed continuity because institutions repeatedly used perceived ancestry to allocate rights and resources. Explaining that process avoids treating race as either biologically fixed or merely a matter of personal hostility.

American history also contains sustained interracial cooperation and resistance. The central analytical question is how racial ideas interacted with material interests and government power—and how organized people changed those relationships without eliminating every inequality they produced.
