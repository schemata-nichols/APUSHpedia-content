> **Imperialism** is the extension of a nation's political, economic, or military power over other peoples and territories. In United States history, it includes continental conquest as well as the overseas expansion associated with the [[Spanish–American War]], annexation, military occupation, and informal economic influence. Advocates invoked security, commerce, racial hierarchy, and a civilizing mission; opponents argued that ruling people without their consent contradicted republican self-government. For APUSH, imperialism is most useful as a question of continuity: did the overseas empire of the 1890s mark a departure, or did it redirect practices long used in [[Westward Expansion]] and Native dispossession?

## APUSH Significance

The United States displaced Native nations and acquired continental territory through treaties, purchases, settlement, and war before becoming an overseas power. In the 1890s, industrial production, strategic competition, missionary ideas, sensational journalism, and the revolt against Spanish rule helped drive intervention in Cuba. Victory in the Spanish–American War brought Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines under U.S. control, while Hawaiʻi was annexed. The ensuing [[Philippine–American War]] revealed the coercion required to deny independence to people who had also fought Spain.

## Empire and Influence

The [[Anti-Imperialist League]] argued that colonial rule violated the [[Declaration of Independence]], but the United States retained territories and expanded influence through the [[Open Door Policy]], the Panama Canal, and repeated intervention in Latin America. Later decolonization reduced formal empire without ending military bases, territorial possessions, or economic influence. Imperialism differs from [[American Foreign Policy]] generally because it emphasizes unequal control; it also overlaps with [[American Imperialism]], the concentrated late-nineteenth-century phase. Its history connects foreign power to domestic debates over race, citizenship, constitutional rights, and national identity.

## Continental Foundations

Long before 1898, the United States used settlement, war, treaty pressure, and federal administration to subordinate peoples and acquire territory. [[Indian Removal]], the [[Mexican–American War]], and the incorporation of western land created a continental republic while denying Native and Mexican claims to sovereignty and property. Because these acquisitions were often organized as future states rather than permanent colonies, they differed institutionally from overseas empire. They nevertheless established racial and political arguments that expansion represented progress and that stronger powers could govern supposedly “uncivilized” peoples.

The post-1898 empire added a sharper constitutional problem. Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines were governed without a promise of statehood. The Insular Cases allowed the federal government to treat some constitutional protections as not fully applicable in “unincorporated” territories. Empire therefore created categories of people subject to U.S. sovereignty without the political equality associated with state citizenship.

## Formal Rule and Informal Power

Imperial power did not always require annexation. The Platt Amendment limited Cuban independence, Theodore Roosevelt's corollary to the Monroe Doctrine claimed a U.S. policing role in the Western Hemisphere, and “dollar diplomacy” used finance and intervention to protect strategic and commercial interests. Construction and control of the Panama Canal joined military mobility to global trade. These policies often produced resistance because American officials defined order and stability around U.S. priorities.

During the twentieth century, the United States sometimes supported self-determination and decolonization while retaining territories, bases, and extensive economic leverage. Cold War interventions were usually justified as containment rather than colonial acquisition, but critics saw continuities in unequal power and limits on local choice. Not every exercise of influence is imperialism: the term is most precise when a stronger state controls territory, political decisions, security, or economic options without reciprocal accountability.

This history links foreign relations to domestic categories. Courts and lawmakers debated whether colonial subjects were citizens, migrants from U.S. territories moved to the mainland, and racial ideologies developed abroad returned to American politics. Imperialism is consequently not only a story of diplomatic strategy; it is also a struggle over whether republican principles apply beyond the continental states.
