> **Internationalism** is the belief that the United States should cooperate actively with other nations through diplomacy, trade, international law, collective security, or multinational institutions. It does not necessarily reject military power or national interest; instead, it argues that American security and prosperity are connected to events beyond U.S. borders. Internationalism became especially influential during and after [[World War II]], when the United States helped build the United Nations and a new global economic order. In APUSH, the term clarifies that engagement can be cooperative and institutional even when it also produces unequal influence or intervention.

## APUSH Significance

American leaders pursued international cooperation before the twentieth century, but participation remained selective. [[Woodrow Wilson]] made collective security central to his wartime aims and promoted the League of Nations after [[World War I]]. The Senate's rejection of membership showed that the real dispute was not total engagement versus total withdrawal: the United States continued trade, diplomacy, and arms-control efforts while avoiding a binding security commitment. The crises of the 1930s and World War II then persuaded many policymakers that economic instability and aggression abroad directly threatened the United States.

## Institutions and Power

After 1945, the United States joined the United Nations, supported the World Bank and International Monetary Fund, led alliances such as NATO, and promoted trade liberalization. These institutions made internationalism a foundation of [[Cold War]] strategy and later [[Globalization]], but critics challenged their costs, military commitments, and disproportionate American influence. Internationalism should not be treated as the opposite of all unilateral action: administrations often combined alliances with independent intervention. The useful distinction is between sustained cooperation through rules and institutions and policies designed primarily to avoid binding political or military commitments.

## From Selective Cooperation to Global Leadership

Nineteenth-century cooperation included treaties, arbitration, commerce, and international conferences, but distance from Europe and expanding continental markets allowed the United States to avoid permanent security alliances. Growing trade and [[American Imperialism]] made overseas stability more important by the early twentieth century. Wilson's internationalism attempted to unite national self-determination, open diplomacy, and collective security, yet wartime intervention and colonial realities exposed limits in those principles.

The debate over the League of Nations turned especially on Article X, which opponents feared could compromise Congress's authority and force the United States to defend other members. Rejection did not restore an earlier world. American banks, businesses, diplomats, and arms-control negotiators remained deeply involved abroad. This is why the interwar period is better described as selective engagement than complete [[Isolationism]].

## The Postwar Order and Its Contradictions

Officials planning during World War II tried to avoid the economic rivalry and weak collective security associated with the interwar crisis. The United Nations provided a forum for diplomacy, while Bretton Woods institutions sought monetary stability and reconstruction. The Marshall Plan, NATO, and later alliances connected international cooperation to containment of the Soviet Union. U.S. power anchored these institutions, meaning postwar internationalism was both multilateral and a means of American leadership.

The order generated recurring tensions. Alliances could deter attack but entangle the country in distant conflicts. Trade institutions encouraged growth while exposing workers and communities to economic competition. International law supplied standards for human rights, yet powerful states did not apply those rules consistently. The Vietnam War showed how officials could describe intervention as defense of an international system even when domestic opposition questioned its legitimacy and effectiveness.

After the Cold War, multinational cooperation addressed trade, weapons, disease, migration, and climate, but arguments over sovereignty and burden sharing intensified. Internationalism is therefore not a fixed foreign-policy program. It is a recurring claim that rules and institutions can advance national interests through cooperation, always accompanied by disputes over whose rules govern and how costs and authority should be distributed.
