> **Isolationism** describes efforts to avoid binding political or military involvement in conflicts outside the United States, especially in Europe. The term does not mean that the nation literally isolated itself: Americans continually pursued trade, continental expansion, diplomacy, missionary activity, and overseas influence. From Washington's warning against permanent alliances to the neutrality laws of the 1930s, advocates sought freedom of action and protection from foreign wars. For APUSH, the key distinction is between selective non-entanglement and complete separation from the world, which the United States never practiced.

## APUSH Significance

Early leaders feared that European alliances could threaten the new republic, but the United States still fought wars, acquired territory, and asserted regional power through the [[Monroe Doctrine]]. After [[World War I]], the Senate rejected the League of Nations partly because critics feared automatic commitments, yet the country remained economically and diplomatically active. In the 1930s, disillusionment with the war, the [[Great Depression]], and investigations of arms makers helped produce Neutrality Acts intended to keep the nation out of another conflict.

## Limits and Change

German and Japanese expansion made strict neutrality harder to sustain. Franklin Roosevelt gradually aided the Allies before the attack on Pearl Harbor brought entry into [[World War II]]. After 1945, the United States adopted sustained [[Internationalism]] through the United Nations, NATO, economic institutions, and containment, although recurring demands to reduce overseas commitments survived. “Isolationist” can become a misleading label when it erases trade or unilateral power. Historically, arguments usually concerned which regions and commitments mattered, whether cooperation would preserve sovereignty, and how much risk Americans should accept abroad.

## Origins of Non-Entanglement

The weakness of the early republic made avoidance of European wars a practical strategy. George Washington's Farewell Address warned particularly against permanent alliances and intense partisan attachment to foreign powers. Thomas Jefferson later praised “entangling alliances,” yet both administrations used diplomacy, trade restrictions, and military force when leaders believed American interests required them. Non-entanglement sought independence of judgment rather than an end to foreign relations.

Continental expansion further complicates the label. The United States avoided some European commitments while waging wars against Native nations and Mexico, acquiring territory, and announcing the [[Monroe Doctrine]]. In the late nineteenth century it developed an overseas empire and greater influence in Latin America and Asia. A nation could therefore reject one kind of international obligation while aggressively projecting power in another region.

## The Interwar Debate

After World War I, Americans remembered casualties, wartime repression, unpaid European debts, and an unstable peace. Congress's Nye Committee investigated the claim that arms manufacturers and bankers had helped draw the country into war. Neutrality legislation attempted to prevent trade and lending from producing the same pressures during a future conflict. These policies reflected a broad desire to avoid war, although Americans disagreed over whether neutrality would deter aggression or reward it.

As fascist expansion threatened Europe and Asia, Roosevelt argued that American security depended on aiding nations resisting the Axis. Measures such as cash-and-carry, destroyers-for-bases, and Lend-Lease moved the country toward support for Britain without an immediate declaration of war. The America First Committee opposed entry, while interventionists contended that oceans no longer offered adequate protection. Pearl Harbor ended the immediate debate but did not prove that every earlier advocate of restraint favored literal isolation.

Postwar alliance commitments made sustained disengagement unlikely, yet the underlying argument survived in criticism of Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, and other interventions. Calling all restraint “isolationism” can obscure legitimate disputes about strategy. The more useful APUSH questions are what commitments critics opposed, what forms of trade or diplomacy they accepted, and whether reduced involvement protected American autonomy or allowed threats to grow.
