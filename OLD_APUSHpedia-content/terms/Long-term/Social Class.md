> **Social class** describes divisions based on wealth, income, occupation, education, and control over productive resources. Americans have often emphasized equality and mobility, but class differences shaped political influence, working conditions, consumption, housing, and access to security from the colonial era onward. Class interacted with race, gender, legal status, and region rather than replacing those categories. For APUSH, social class exposes the tension between the nation's egalitarian ideals and durable inequalities, while avoiding the false assumption that economic position alone determined every person's identity or politics.

## APUSH Significance

Colonial societies ranged from small-farm communities to plantation regions dominated by wealthy landholders and dependent labor. The [[Market Revolution]] expanded a commercial middle class and wage-earning workforce while reducing many artisans' control over production. After the Civil War, [[Industrial Capitalism]] produced vast corporate fortunes and an insecure industrial working class. The resulting strikes, the [[Labor Movement]], and [[Populism]] turned unequal economic power into a major political issue during the [[Gilded Age]]. Reformers disagreed over whether opportunity, union power, regulation, or redistribution offered the best response.

## Mobility and Inequality

The [[New Deal]], wartime employment, union growth, and federal benefits helped enlarge the mid-twentieth-century middle class, but occupational discrimination and unequal access to housing and education limited gains for many Black and Latino families and for women. Deindustrialization, declining union strength, and expanding finance and technology later changed the geography and distribution of opportunity. Social mobility did occur, but celebrated individual examples could obscure structural barriers and inherited wealth. Class is therefore both a material relationship and a cultural identity, explaining conflicts over who performs work, who receives its rewards, and whether government should reduce economic risk.

## Making and Measuring Class

Class can be measured through income, wealth, occupation, education, or control over productive property, but these measures do not always align. A skilled worker may earn a solid income without accumulating wealth; a landowner may possess valuable property but little cash. Wealth is especially important because it can survive across generations through inheritance, housing, education, and business ownership. Class is therefore both a current economic position and access to long-term security and influence.

Industrialization made class relationships more visible. Large corporations separated owners and managers from a growing wage-earning population, while urban neighborhoods and workplaces concentrated inequality. Workers built unions and political organizations to counter employer power; middle-class reformers promoted education, sobriety, and professional expertise; wealthy industrialists defended fortunes through ideas such as [[Social Darwinism]] or the [[Gospel of Wealth]]. These responses show that class identities are created politically and culturally as well as economically.

## Government, Race, and Mobility

Public policy distributes opportunity. Homestead laws transferred land while excluding people displaced by settlement; schools and colleges built human capital but remained unequal; taxation and social insurance shifted resources; labor law strengthened or weakened bargaining power. New Deal programs reduced insecurity for many families, yet occupational exclusions and local administration denied equal benefits to many Black and agricultural workers. After World War II, federal mortgages and veterans' benefits supported homeownership, but discriminatory lending and segregation limited access and widened racial wealth gaps.

The belief in upward mobility has been central to [[American Identity]], but movement has never depended on effort alone. Economic growth can create new occupations and higher wages, while depression, deindustrialization, illness, discrimination, or inherited disadvantage can block advancement. Race and gender affect class without being reducible to it: people with similar income may face different legal treatment, wealth, safety, or political power.

Class conflict does not always produce a unified class politics. Racial divisions, regional interests, religion, ideas of individualism, and hopes for mobility have often weakened solidarity. Conversely, [[Populism]], the [[Labor Movement]], and welfare-rights movements sometimes built coalitions around shared material concerns. For APUSH, social class works best as an explanation when it identifies a concrete relationship—ownership, wages, debt, taxation, housing, or access—rather than assuming all rich or poor Americans acted alike.
