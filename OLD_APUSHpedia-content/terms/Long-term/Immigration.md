> **Immigration** is the movement of people from other countries into the United States and the political, economic, and cultural process through which newcomers enter American society. Different migrations supplied labor, created communities, reshaped religion and culture, and influenced settlement, cities, industry, and politics. Opportunity, family networks, persecution, war, and economic disruption pushed or pulled migrants, while law determined who could enter, naturalize, remain, or receive protection. Immigration has repeatedly generated [[Nativism]], racial classification, and demands for restriction alongside incorporation and pluralism. For APUSH, immigration is not one continuous wave or a simple story of assimilation; its sources, legal rules, destinations, and meanings changed with the economy and American power.

## APUSH Significance

Immigration connects demographic change to labor, [[Citizenship|citizenship]], politics, and national identity:

1. **Economic development attracted and used migrant labor.** Farms, canals, factories, railroads, mines, and services depended on newcomers with unequal bargaining power.
2. **Law created categories of belonging.** Naturalization, exclusion, quotas, deportation, refugee, and border policies distributed access by race, nationality, family, and skill.
3. **Communities changed American culture.** Immigrants established churches, schools, newspapers, businesses, unions, political organizations, and family networks.
4. **Nativism recurred in new forms.** Economic anxiety, religious prejudice, racism, wartime suspicion, and political competition supported restriction.
5. **Assimilation was selective and mutual.** Newcomers adapted to institutions while also changing language, food, religion, music, politics, and ideas of [[American Identity|American identity]].

Immigration should be distinguished from internal [[Migration]], including forced Native removal, the domestic slave trade, the [[Great Migration]], and movement to suburbs or the [[Sun Belt]]. These processes interacted but had different legal and geographic meanings.

## Colonial and Early National Migration

European colonization brought settlers seeking land, trade, religious communities, or economic advancement. Their migration occurred alongside the forced transportation of enslaved Africans and the displacement of Native peoples. Calling all three processes immigration would obscure the difference between voluntary relocation, forced migration, and invasion.

After independence, the Naturalization Act of 1790 limited naturalization to “free white persons,” establishing a racial boundary around formal membership. The [[Alien and Sedition Acts]] showed that fear of foreign influence could shape party conflict even when the immigrant population was comparatively small.

Territorial expansion encouraged European immigrants and American-born settlers to occupy western land. Federal land policy, transportation development, and military conquest made this mobility possible while overriding Native sovereignty and existing Mexican communities.

## Nineteenth-Century Mass Immigration

In the 1840s and 1850s, Irish people fled famine while Germans left political upheaval and economic disruption. Many settled in northeastern and midwestern cities; others farmed. Irish Catholic immigration especially provoked Protestant fears that newcomers owed loyalty to the pope and threatened jobs or elections. The [[Know-Nothing Party]] tried to restrict immigrant political influence.

Immigrants nevertheless entered urban politics, churches, unions, military service, and community institutions. [[Political Machines|Political machines]] exchanged aid and access for votes, helping some newcomers navigate cities while also reinforcing patronage. Ethnic communities could ease adjustment without preventing later identification as American.

Chinese workers joined western mining, agriculture, services, and construction, including the [[Transcontinental Railroad|transcontinental railroad]]. Racial hostility and labor competition produced local violence and federal restriction. The [[Chinese Exclusion Act]] of 1882 barred most Chinese labor immigration and made nationality-based exclusion a central federal policy.

## Industrial Migration and Restriction

From the 1880s to the 1920s, millions arrived from southern and eastern Europe as well as other regions. Industrial employers recruited labor, while chain migration connected villages to particular American neighborhoods and workplaces. Jewish migrants fled persecution; Italians, Poles, Slavs, Greeks, and others pursued security and income.

Cities grew around immigrant labor and institutions. Settlement-house reformers offered services but sometimes promoted middle-class assimilation. Employers used ethnic divisions to weaken unions, while labor organizers built multilingual coalitions. Immigration thus contributed both to industrial growth and to conflict over wages, housing, education, and urban government.

Nativists described newer migrants as racially inferior, politically radical, or incapable of assimilation. Literacy tests and the national-origins quota system of the 1920s sharply reduced immigration and favored northern and western Europe. Asian exclusion expanded, while Mexican migration remained important to southwestern agriculture and industry because the land border and labor demand operated differently from the quota system.

## War, Depression, and Unequal Belonging

[[World War I]] encouraged “[[Americanization]]” campaigns and suspicion of German culture and political radicalism. During the [[Great Depression]], officials and private groups pressured or removed many people of Mexican descent, including some United States citizens. Economic crisis made belonging vulnerable to racialized claims about employment and public assistance.

[[World War II]] exposed another contradiction. The country mobilized against fascism while incarcerating Japanese Americans, most of them citizens, under Executive Order 9066. At the same time, wartime labor needs supported the [[Bracero Program]] and new migration from Mexico. Security, race, and economic demand pulled policy in different directions.

## The 1965 Framework and [[New Immigration]]

The [[Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965]] abolished the national-origins quota system and emphasized family reunification and employment preferences. Immigration increased especially from Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, and Africa, making the population more diverse and changing metropolitan areas, suburbs, and newer destinations across the country.

Later policy combined legalization with enforcement. The [[Immigration Reform and Control Act]] of 1986 legalized many undocumented residents while penalizing employers who knowingly hired unauthorized workers. Refugee policy, border enforcement, temporary statuses, family separation, and the position of undocumented residents remained politically contested.

## Admission, Belonging, and Exclusion

The common “nation of immigrants” description captures immigration's importance but can hide conquest, forced migration, and exclusion. It can also suggest that every group followed the same path toward assimilation. Legal access, racial classification, labor demand, education, property, and time of arrival produced different outcomes.

The strongest APUSH pattern is a cycle of recruitment and restriction shaped by economic and political conditions. The United States repeatedly depended on immigrant labor and celebrated opportunity while constructing legal barriers around race and nationality. Newcomers did not merely enter an unchanged society; through institutions, activism, work, and culture, they helped remake it.
