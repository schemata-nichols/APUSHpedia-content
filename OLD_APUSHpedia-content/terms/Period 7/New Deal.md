> The **New Deal** was Franklin D. Roosevelt's collection of federal relief, recovery, and reform policies responding to the [[Great Depression]] from 1933 through the late 1930s. Banking measures restored confidence, work programs employed millions, agricultural and industrial policies tried to raise prices and production, and laws strengthened labor rights and social insurance. The New Deal did not end the Depression or distribute benefits equally, but it transformed the relationship among citizens, markets, and national government. For APUSH, its central significance is the creation of a modern federal responsibility for economic security, accompanied by a durable Democratic coalition and continuing conflicts over executive power, race, and welfare.

## APUSH Significance

The New Deal established lasting institutions and political expectations:

1. **Federal relief became direct and national.** Work and assistance programs addressed unemployment beyond local charity.
2. **Government regulated capitalism.** Banking, securities, agriculture, and labor rules sought stability rather than socialism.
3. **Social citizenship expanded.** Pensions, unemployment insurance, and labor protections treated economic security as a public concern.
4. **A new electoral coalition formed.** Workers, immigrants, farmers, white southerners, and many Black voters supported Democrats.
5. **Benefits and protection were unequal.** Program design and southern power excluded or disadvantaged many workers of color and women.

The New Deal was not one coherent master plan. It developed experimentally as officials responded to crisis, court rulings, elections, protest, and incomplete recovery.

## The First New Deal

Roosevelt declared a [[Bank Holiday]] and supported emergency banking legislation. Federal review and deposit guarantees helped restore confidence. The [[Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation]] protected deposits, while the [[Securities and Exchange Commission]] regulated securities markets.

Relief agencies provided funds to states and direct employment. The [[Civilian Conservation Corps]] placed young men in environmental work, while public-works agencies constructed roads, schools, dams, and public buildings. The [[Tennessee Valley Authority]] combined electrification, flood control, navigation, and regional development.

The [[Agricultural Adjustment Act]] paid farmers to reduce production in order to raise prices. Benefits often flowed through landowners, who could displace tenants and sharecroppers. The National Industrial Recovery Act encouraged industry codes and recognized organizing rights, but its administration favored large firms and the Supreme Court invalidated it.

## The Second New Deal

Criticism and Democratic gains encouraged stronger social and labor legislation in 1935. The [[Works Progress Administration]] employed people in construction, arts, writing, education, and public services, leaving extensive physical and cultural infrastructure.

The [[Wagner Act]] protected collective bargaining and created the National Labor Relations Board. Union membership surged, especially through the [[Congress of Industrial Organizations]], which organized mass-production workers across skill lines.

The [[Social Security Act]] established old-age pensions, unemployment insurance, and assistance programs. It created a durable federal commitment but initially excluded many agricultural and domestic workers, disproportionately affecting Black and Mexican American laborers.

## Opposition, Courts, and [[Executive Power]]

Conservative business groups argued that regulation, deficits, and union rights threatened liberty. The Supreme Court invalidated several early programs. Roosevelt's [[Court-Packing Plan]] proposed adding justices for older members who did not retire, provoking bipartisan resistance and concerns about judicial independence.

Critics from the left and populist traditions demanded more. [[Huey Long]] proposed large-scale wealth redistribution, [[Francis Townsend]] promoted old-age pensions, and Father Charles Coughlin attacked financial power before turning toward authoritarian and antisemitic rhetoric.

The Court subsequently became more receptive to regulation, and retirements allowed Roosevelt to appoint new justices. Even without court expansion, constitutional interpretation shifted toward broader federal economic authority.

## Race, Gender, and the New Deal Coalition

Black voters in northern cities increasingly shifted from the [[Republican Party]] to the [[New Deal Coalition]], aided by relief access and advocacy from figures including Eleanor Roosevelt. Yet Roosevelt avoided a strong federal anti-lynching campaign to preserve support from southern Democrats.

Local discrimination shaped program administration, and agricultural and domestic exclusions reinforced inequality. New Deal housing policies helped institutionalize residential segregation through lending standards and redlining.

Women gained visible administrative roles, but programs often assumed a male breadwinner and offered women fewer jobs or lower pay. Indigenous policy under the Indian Reorganization Act moved away from allotment and supported some tribal self-government, though federal paternalism persisted.

## Results and Legacy

The New Deal relieved suffering, stabilized finance, strengthened organized labor, and built public infrastructure. It did not restore full employment; recession returned in 1937 when spending fell, and [[World War II]] mobilization completed economic recovery.

It preserved private ownership and markets while surrounding them with regulation, bargaining rights, and social insurance. Conservatives later challenged its reach, but few could ignore the expectation that Washington should respond to severe economic crisis.

The New Deal therefore represented reform rather than revolution. Its combination of expanded democracy for workers, unequal racial administration, and stronger executive government makes it central to debates over modern [[Federalism]] and liberalism.
