> **American Imperialism** was the late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century expansion of United States political, military, and economic power beyond the continental nation, especially in the Caribbean and Pacific. In the 1890s, strategic competition, overseas trade, missionary ideas, racial ideology, and domestic politics encouraged annexation and intervention. The [[Spanish–American War]] brought Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines under American control while Cuba became formally independent but constrained by U.S. power. For APUSH, imperialism represented both continuity with [[Westward Expansion]] and a change toward overseas possessions whose inhabitants were governed without equal constitutional or political rights.

## APUSH Significance

American imperialism changed the nation's international role:

1. **The United States acquired overseas territory.** Victory over Spain created an empire extending into the Caribbean and Pacific.
2. **Economic and strategic goals reinforced each other.** Policymakers sought markets, naval stations, investment access, and regional security.
3. **Racial and civilizing claims justified rule.** Expansionists portrayed other peoples as incapable of self-government.
4. **Imperialism provoked domestic debate.** Critics argued that colonial rule contradicted consent and republican government.
5. **New power produced repeated intervention.** Presidents used military, financial, and diplomatic pressure throughout Latin America and Asia.

Imperialism was not identical to all [[American Foreign Policy]]. It refers specifically to unequal control over territories or other nations, whether through annexation, occupation, or informal economic dominance.

## Context for Overseas Expansion

The closing of the continental frontier, industrial overproduction, and international competition encouraged interest abroad. Business leaders sought trade and investment, while naval strategist Alfred Thayer Mahan argued that national power required a modern fleet, overseas bases, and a canal through Central America.

Missionaries and expansionists invoked Christianity and a “civilizing mission.” [[Social Darwinism]] and racial hierarchy portrayed Anglo-Saxon rule as natural. These ideas connected overseas empire to earlier Native dispossession and Mexican conquest, although oceans and permanent colonial administration distinguished the new expansion.

The United States annexed Hawaii in 1898 after American planters and business interests had helped overthrow Queen Liliʻuokalani. The islands' location made them valuable for Pacific commerce and naval strategy.

## War with Spain

Cuban rebels fought Spanish rule, attracting American sympathy and investment concerns. [[Yellow Journalism]] publicized Spanish abuses, and the explosion of the [[USS Maine]] in Havana Harbor intensified demands for war despite uncertainty over its cause.

Congress adopted the [[Teller Amendment]], promising not to annex Cuba. The brief 1898 war produced American victories in Cuba and the Philippines. The peace treaty transferred Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines to the United States; Spain relinquished Cuba.

The war demonstrated growing naval capacity and made the United States a more visible world power. It also raised the question of whether liberation from Spain would lead to independence or American control.

## The Philippines and Colonial Rule

Filipino nationalists under Emilio Aguinaldo expected independence after fighting Spain. Instead, American annexation produced the [[Philippine–American War]], a brutal conflict involving guerrilla warfare, civilian displacement, atrocities, and extensive casualties.

American officials established schools, infrastructure, and civil administration while denying immediate self-government. Supporters called this benevolent assimilation; critics emphasized that improvements imposed through conquest did not create consent.

The [[Anti-Imperialist League]], including figures such as Mark Twain and [[Andrew Carnegie]], argued that empire betrayed the [[Declaration of Independence]]. Expansionists replied that withdrawal would invite disorder or rival powers and that the islands offered strategic and commercial advantages.

## Informal Empire and Presidential Diplomacy

The [[Open Door Policy]] sought equal commercial access in China and preservation of Chinese territorial integrity without formal American colonization. It reflected a preference for market access backed by diplomatic power.

In Cuba, the [[Platt Amendment]] authorized American intervention and secured a naval base at Guantánamo Bay. [[Theodore Roosevelt]]'s [[Roosevelt Corollary]] to the [[Monroe Doctrine]] claimed a U.S. policing role in the Western Hemisphere. The [[Panama Canal]], obtained after American support for Panama's separation from Colombia, strengthened naval and commercial reach.

[[Big Stick Diplomacy]], [[Dollar Diplomacy]], and [[Moral Diplomacy]] differed in method but all accepted an unusually broad American role in Latin America. Occupations and financial supervision often protected strategic and business interests while limiting local sovereignty.

## Constitutional and Long-Term Consequences

The Insular Cases held that full constitutional rights did not automatically extend to every acquired territory. This created a category of belonging to the United States without full incorporation, exposing tension between empire and citizenship.

Imperial expansion helped prepare the United States for participation in [[World War I]] and later global leadership. Yet anti-imperial arguments also persisted, and colonized peoples repeatedly asserted independence.

For APUSH comparison, overseas imperialism combined older beliefs in expansion and racial hierarchy with newer naval, commercial, and geopolitical aims. Its central contradiction was a republic claiming to spread liberty while governing people who had not consented to American rule.
