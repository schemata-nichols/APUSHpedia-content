> **World War II** was the global conflict from 1939 to 1945 in which the United States initially aided the Allies while remaining formally neutral, then entered after Japan attacked [[Pearl Harbor]] on December 7, 1941. American industry, armed forces, scientific research, and alliances helped defeat Germany, Italy, and Japan. Mobilization ended mass unemployment, accelerated migration, expanded opportunities for women and minorities, and increased federal power, while [[Japanese American Internment]] exposed severe limits on civil liberties. For APUSH, the war transformed the United States into a global superpower and created domestic and international conditions that shaped civil rights, prosperity, and the [[Cold War]].

## APUSH Significance

World War II marked a decisive transformation in American power:

1. **The United States abandoned formal neutrality.** Aid to the Allies preceded direct entry after Pearl Harbor.
2. **Mobilization ended the Depression.** Federal spending and industrial production created full employment and enormous capacity.
3. **[[Migration]] and work changed society.** Women and minority workers entered defense industries while discrimination persisted.
4. **War expanded federal and military institutions.** Planning, taxation, research, and executive authority reached unprecedented scale.
5. **Victory produced global leadership.** The United States helped construct the [[United Nations]] and emerged in rivalry with the Soviet Union.

World War II differed from [[World War I]] in the scale and duration of American mobilization, the direct attack that triggered entry, and the more durable commitment to postwar international institutions.

## From Neutrality to War

Memories of World War I and disillusionment with arms dealers and debt encouraged Congress to pass the [[Neutrality Acts]]. Many Americans opposed another European war, although [[Isolationism]] did not mean indifference to international events.

As Germany conquered much of Europe, Franklin Roosevelt moved toward aid to Britain. The destroyers-for-bases agreement exchanged ships for base rights, and the [[Lend-Lease Act]] supplied materials to nations considered vital to American defense. The [[Atlantic Charter]] outlined Anglo-American principles for a postwar order.

In Asia, Japan's expansion in China and Southeast Asia threatened regional power and access to resources. American economic sanctions, including restrictions on oil, increased pressure. Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor brought a declaration of war; Germany and Italy then declared war on the United States.

## [[Wartime Mobilization]]

The federal government directed production through contracts, priorities, rationing, and price controls. Automobile factories converted to aircraft and military vehicles, and shipyards produced at remarkable speed. The [[War Industries Board]] belonged to the previous world war; World War II mobilization used newer agencies with greater resources and experience.

Taxes expanded, war bonds absorbed savings, and federal spending ended Depression unemployment. Cooperation with large corporations strengthened a military-industrial relationship that continued after 1945.

Millions served under the draft. On the home front, rationing and recycling tied household consumption to military needs, while propaganda presented the war as a struggle for democracy against fascism.

## Race, Gender, and Migration

[[Rosie the Riveter]] symbolized women's industrial work. Women also served in military auxiliaries and filled clerical and professional roles, though employers often paid less and expected them to leave jobs after the war.

Black activists pursued the [[Double V Campaign]] for victory against fascism abroad and racism at home. A threatened march led Roosevelt to prohibit discrimination in defense employment and create the Fair Employment Practice Committee. The military remained segregated, and racial conflict accompanied migration to defense centers.

The [[Bracero Program]] recruited Mexican laborers, while Mexican American servicemembers fought abroad. Native Americans worked in industry and served in the military, including code talkers. Wartime opportunity accelerated movement toward cities and the West but did not eliminate discrimination.

## Internment and Civil Liberties

Executive Order 9066 led to the forced removal and incarceration of roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast, most of them American citizens. Officials invoked military necessity without demonstrating individual disloyalty, while racism and economic interests shaped the policy.

In [[Korematsu v. United States]], the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion order. German and Italian nationals faced restrictions, but no comparable mass removal occurred. Internment contradicted claims that the war defended freedom and later produced official apology and reparations.

The episode demonstrates how citizenship and constitutional protection can become vulnerable during wartime fear.

## Fighting and Victory

The Allies prioritized defeating Germany. American and British forces fought in North Africa and Italy before the [[D-Day]] invasion opened a western front in France. Soviet armies bore enormous losses while destroying much of German military power. Germany surrendered in May 1945, and the [[Holocaust]] revealed the systematic murder of six million Jews and millions of other victims.

In the Pacific, the United States used [[Island Hopping]] to approach Japan while submarine warfare and bombing weakened its economy. The secret [[Manhattan Project]] developed atomic bombs, which the United States dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945. Japan surrendered soon afterward.

Debate continues over the bombs' necessity, alternatives, expected casualties, Soviet entry into the Pacific war, and the moral status of targeting cities. APUSH analysis should recognize both the military context and the unprecedented destructive power introduced.

## Postwar Consequences

At the [[Yalta Conference]] and [[Potsdam Conference]], Allied leaders addressed occupation, borders, Germany, and the war against Japan while tensions with the Soviet Union grew. Unlike after World War I, the United States joined the United Nations and maintained extensive forces and commitments abroad.

Mobilization left the nation with unmatched industrial strength, advanced technology, a larger federal state, and global military bases. The [[GI Bill]] supported education and homeownership for veterans, though discriminatory administration limited equal access.

World War II expanded opportunity and democratic rhetoric without automatically creating equality. Those contradictions helped energize the postwar [[African American Civil Rights Movement]] even as superpower rivalry reorganized American foreign policy.
