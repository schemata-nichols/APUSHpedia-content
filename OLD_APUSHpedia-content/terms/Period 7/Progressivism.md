> **Progressivism** was a diverse reform impulse from the 1890s through the 1920s that sought to address problems created by industrial corporations, urbanization, immigration, political corruption, and unequal power. Progressives used investigation, professional expertise, citizen activism, and expanded government authority to regulate business, improve public health, democratize politics, and protect workers and consumers. They did not share one ideology: reformers included labor activists, settlement-house workers, journalists, middle-class professionals, politicians, and moral campaigners. For APUSH, Progressivism enlarged government's responsibility for social welfare and economic fairness while frequently preserving racial hierarchy, coercive assimilation, and middle-class control.

## APUSH Significance

Progressivism connected [[Gilded Age]] conflicts to modern regulatory government:

1. **Reformers challenged concentrated power.** Antitrust laws and commissions sought to make corporations accountable to the public.
2. **Cities became centers of innovation.** Public-health campaigns, settlement houses, and municipal reform responded to industrial urban life.
3. **[[Democracy]] expanded procedurally.** Direct primaries, initiative, referendum, recall, and direct election of senators weakened some party organizations.
4. **Government protected consumers and some workers.** Food, drug, safety, and labor laws established new public responsibilities.
5. **Reform had sharp limits.** Many Progressives accepted segregation, exclusion, imperialism, and paternalistic ideas of expertise.

Progressivism overlapped with but differed from [[Populism]]. Populists built a primarily agrarian mass movement, while many Progressives were urban middle-class reformers who trusted administration and experts.

## Origins and Methods

Industrial growth produced national corporations, dangerous workplaces, polluted cities, crowded [[Tenements]], and periodic depressions. Earlier labor, agrarian, women's, and religious reform supplied organizations and ideas. The [[Social Gospel]] applied Protestant ethics to poverty and inequality, while the [[Settlement House Movement]] joined neighborhood service to research and advocacy.

[[Muckrakers]] exposed abuses to a mass reading public. [[Ida Tarbell]] investigated Standard Oil, [[Upton Sinclair]] described the meatpacking industry, and [[Jacob Riis]] photographed urban poverty. Their work did not itself create reform, but it supplied evidence that activists and officials could translate into policy.

Progressives generally rejected both laissez-faire and socialist revolution. They argued that modern conditions required organized public power capable of balancing corporations and correcting social harms.

## Political and Municipal Reform

Reformers attacked patronage and [[Political Machines]], though machines also provided immigrants with services unavailable elsewhere. Cities adopted commissions, city managers, building codes, sanitation systems, and public utilities. These changes could reduce corruption but sometimes shifted authority from working-class voters to professionals.

At the state level, Wisconsin governor Robert La Follette promoted expert-supported regulation. The [[Initiative, Referendum, and Recall]] enabled voters to bypass legislatures, while direct primaries weakened party leaders. The [[Seventeenth Amendment]] established direct election of senators.

The [[Women's Suffrage Movement]] linked political equality to reform. Decades of organizing culminated in the Nineteenth Amendment, although racial barriers continued to exclude many women in practice.

## Corporate Regulation and Consumer Protection

[[Theodore Roosevelt]] distinguished “good” from harmful trusts and used the [[Sherman Antitrust Act]] against combinations he considered contrary to the public interest. Federal mediation in the 1902 coal strike suggested that government need not automatically side with owners.

The Hepburn Act strengthened railroad regulation. The [[Pure Food and Drug Act]] and Meat Inspection Act responded to consumer campaigns and meatpacking revelations. Under [[Woodrow Wilson]], the [[Federal Reserve Act]] reorganized banking, and the [[Clayton Antitrust Act]] strengthened regulation while declaring that labor was not a commodity.

Progressives usually sought controlled corporate capitalism rather than the destruction of large enterprise. Regulatory agencies could be influenced by the industries they supervised, and enforcement varied.

## Labor and Social Welfare

States restricted child labor, regulated hours, and created workers' compensation. The [[Triangle Shirtwaist Factory Fire]] dramatized the costs of unsafe workplaces and strengthened factory regulation in New York.

Protective labor laws often focused on women, justified through maternal roles rather than equal citizenship. Courts sometimes struck down regulation under “liberty of contract,” though _Muller v. Oregon_ upheld limits on women's hours. Workers continued to rely on strikes and unions because reform rarely established broad collective-bargaining rights.

Settlement houses such as [[Hull House]] offered education, childcare, and recreation while supporting labor and health reform. Yet [[Americanization]] campaigns could treat immigrant cultures as problems to be corrected.

## Race, Empire, and Moral Reform

Most white Progressives failed to confront the [[Jim Crow System]], and some promoted segregation or disfranchisement as “good government.” Black activists built their own reform networks, including the NAACP, and challenged lynching and unequal citizenship. Reform was therefore racially divided rather than universally inclusive.

Some Progressives supported [[American Imperialism]], believing expert government should manage overseas peoples. Others joined the [[Anti-Imperialist League]]. [[Prohibition]] similarly combined public-health and family-welfare concerns with hostility toward immigrant and working-class culture.

Progressivism's enduring change was the claim that industrial democracy required government regulation and social protection. Its contradictions show that expanding state capacity does not determine whether that power will promote equality, coercion, or both.
