> The **Great Depression** was the prolonged economic collapse that began in 1929 and dominated the 1930s, producing bank failures, business contraction, mass unemployment, falling prices, homelessness, and severe hardship. The [[Stock Market Crash of 1929]] revealed instability but did not alone cause the crisis; weak banking, unequal income, debt, overproduction, reduced international trade, and policy failures deepened it. President Herbert Hoover relied heavily on voluntary cooperation and limited federal measures before Franklin D. Roosevelt's [[New Deal]] greatly expanded government intervention. For APUSH, the Depression transformed expectations of federal responsibility while exposing racial, regional, and environmental inequalities.

## APUSH Significance

The Great Depression reshaped American political economy:

1. **The crisis had multiple structural causes.** Financial panic interacted with weak demand, debt, banking failures, and global instability.
2. **Mass hardship challenged limited government.** Local charity and private relief could not meet national need.
3. **Political alignments changed.** Voters created the [[New Deal Coalition]] and gave Democrats durable national power.
4. **Federal responsibility expanded.** Relief, recovery, regulation, and social insurance became central government functions.
5. **Recovery remained incomplete before war.** New Deal programs reduced suffering and reformed institutions, but [[World War II]] mobilization ended mass unemployment.

The Depression was more than a stock-market event. The crash was a trigger and symbol within a broader breakdown of production, credit, consumption, and international exchange.

## Causes and Collapse

During the 1920s, productivity rose faster than many wages, limiting mass purchasing power. Consumers and businesses relied on credit, while farmers had faced low prices and debt since the postwar agricultural downturn. Some industries were already weak.

Speculators bought shares on margin, making the market vulnerable to falling prices and forced sales. After the crash, banks failed as loans went unpaid and depositors withdrew funds. Because deposits lacked federal insurance, families lost savings and surviving banks restricted credit.

The money supply contracted, prices and wages fell, and debt became harder to repay. Business failures and layoffs reduced demand further. The Smoot-Hawley Tariff and retaliatory barriers damaged world trade. European war debts, German reparations, and dependence on American loans transmitted crisis internationally.

## Human and Regional Effects

Unemployment reached roughly one-quarter of the labor force at its peak, and many employed people worked reduced hours or wages. Families delayed marriage, doubled up in housing, relied on relief, and searched for work. Breadlines and makeshift “Hoovervilles” became visible symbols.

The [[Dust Bowl]] combined drought with farming practices that left Plains soil vulnerable to erosion. Dust storms destroyed crops and forced many families west, where migrants faced low wages and hostility. The environmental disaster was related to but distinct from the financial causes of depression.

Black workers were often fired first and excluded from equal relief. Mexican Americans and Mexican immigrants faced coerced repatriation campaigns. Women were criticized for holding jobs despite their essential household and wage labor. Hardship followed existing racial and gender hierarchies.

## Hoover's Response

[[Herbert Hoover]] believed voluntary cooperation, local relief, and balanced institutions would preserve individual initiative. He encouraged employers to maintain wages and supported public works, but the scale of collapse overwhelmed these measures.

The [[Reconstruction]] Finance Corporation loaned money to banks, railroads, and other institutions, reflecting a belief that stabilizing credit would support recovery. Critics argued that assistance reached corporations more directly than unemployed people.

The eviction of the [[Bonus Army]], [[World War I]] veterans seeking early payment of a promised bonus, damaged Hoover politically. His response was more active than the image of complete inaction suggests, but he resisted direct, sustained federal relief and failed to restore confidence.

## Roosevelt and Political Realignment

Franklin D. Roosevelt won the 1932 election promising a “new deal.” His administration's bank holiday and public communication sought to stabilize finance and confidence. Congress then enacted emergency relief, public works, agricultural and industrial programs, and financial regulation.

The New Deal drew urban workers, immigrants, white southerners, farmers, intellectuals, and increasingly Black northern voters into a Democratic coalition. This alignment expanded federal authority but depended partly on southern segregationists, limiting civil-rights reform.

Critics came from several directions. Conservatives attacked spending and regulation, while [[Huey Long]], labor organizers, and pension advocates argued that reform did not redistribute enough power or security.

## Recovery and Legacy

Economic indicators improved from their lowest levels, but unemployment remained severe. A renewed downturn in 1937 followed reduced spending and tighter policy, showing the fragility of recovery. Wartime orders, conscription, and industrial mobilization finally restored full employment.

The Depression permanently weakened faith that markets would quickly self-correct without public action. Banking insurance, securities regulation, labor rights, Social Security, and expectations of countercyclical policy outlasted the emergency.

Its APUSH significance lies in continuity and change: poverty and inequality predated 1929, but the crisis made federal management of economic security a lasting feature of American political debate.
