> The **Roaring Twenties** describes the 1920s as an era of economic growth, [[Mass Consumer Culture]], automobiles, radio, advertising, and changing social behavior after [[World War I]]. Productivity and credit expanded access to goods, while urban entertainment, the [[Harlem Renaissance]], and new roles for some women challenged older conventions. Yet prosperity was uneven: farmers struggled, unions weakened, and Black Americans faced segregation and racial violence. [[Nativism]], immigration quotas, the Second [[Ku Klux Klan]], and cultural conflict accompanied modernity. For APUSH, the decade joined innovation and reaction, making it neither a simple celebration nor merely a prelude to the [[Great Depression]].

## APUSH Significance

The Roaring Twenties reveals contradictions in modern American society:

1. **Consumer capitalism reshaped daily life.** Mass production, advertising, and credit connected identity to purchased goods.
2. **New media created national culture.** [[Radio]], film, popular music, and spectator sports crossed regional boundaries.
3. **Cultural freedom expanded unevenly.** Women and Black artists gained visibility while legal and social discrimination endured.
4. **Reactionary politics intensified.** [[Immigration]] restriction, antiradicalism, religious fundamentalism, and the Klan defended older hierarchies.
5. **Prosperity contained weaknesses.** Unequal income, speculation, weak farm markets, and installment debt made growth less secure.

The term emphasizes urban consumption and culture, but it should not erase rural hardship or groups excluded from prosperity.

## Consumer Growth and Business Politics

Manufacturers adapted wartime productive capacity to consumer goods. Henry Ford's assembly-line methods lowered automobile costs, while cars stimulated steel, rubber, oil, road construction, suburbs, tourism, and roadside businesses. [[Automobile Culture]] expanded personal mobility and changed courtship and leisure.

Advertising and national brands encouraged consumers to associate products with status and modern identity. [[Installment Buying]] allowed households to acquire cars and appliances before paying the full price. Electricity increased demand for radios, refrigerators, and labor-saving devices, though rural areas and poor households had less access.

Republican administrations promoted low taxes, protective tariffs, and cooperation between government and business. The [[Teapot Dome Scandal]] exposed corruption under Warren Harding, but pro-business policy remained popular during much of the expansion.

## Mass Culture and Social Change

Commercial radio brought news, music, advertising, and entertainment into homes. Hollywood films and national magazines helped standardize popular culture. Celebrity athletes and movie stars became mass-market figures.

Young urban women associated with [[Flappers]] symbolized shorter skirts, public leisure, and sexual independence. The image represented real changes for some women but not the experience of most. Women still faced occupational segregation, unequal pay, and expectations centered on marriage and domestic work.

The Nineteenth Amendment expanded formal voting rights through the [[Women's Suffrage Movement]], but women did not vote as one bloc. Black women in the South remained constrained by the [[Jim Crow System]], and Native and Asian American citizenship restrictions also limited political inclusion.

## Harlem Renaissance and Black Politics

The [[Great Migration]] created large Black urban communities whose institutions supported artistic and political expression. The Harlem Renaissance included writers, artists, musicians, and intellectuals who examined race, migration, history, and modern identity. [[Langston Hughes]] and others rejected demands that Black culture conform to white expectations.

Jazz and blues reached national audiences while white businesses often profited from segregated venues. [[Marcus Garvey]] built a mass movement promoting Black pride, economic independence, and global African solidarity. The NAACP pursued legal challenges and anti-lynching advocacy.

These developments represented cultural self-definition, not the end of racism. Housing discrimination, employment barriers, riots, and lynching continued in the North and South.

## Nativism and Cultural Conflict

The [[First Red Scare]] associated labor activism, immigrants, and dissent with revolution. The [[Palmer Raids]] violated civil liberties through arrests and deportations. The execution of [[Sacco and Vanzetti]] became an international symbol of prejudice and contested justice.

The [[Immigration Act of 1924]] created national-origins quotas favoring northern and western Europeans and excluded immigration by people barred from citizenship. The [[Second Ku Klux Klan]] targeted Black Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and perceived moral disorder while gaining political influence beyond the South.

[[Prohibition]] attempted to suppress alcohol through constitutional authority but encouraged illegal markets and uneven enforcement. The [[Scopes Trial]] dramatized conflict between religious fundamentalism and modern science, though the underlying debate involved education, authority, and cultural change.

## Uneven Prosperity and Interpretation

Farmers faced low prices and debt after wartime demand collapsed. Coal, textiles, and railroads also experienced weakness, while anti-union employers promoted the open shop. Mexican and Black workers often filled low-wage jobs while encountering discrimination.

Stock speculation, buying on margin, unequal purchasing power, and a vulnerable banking system accompanied genuine productivity growth. These weaknesses did not alone make depression inevitable, but they shaped the collapse after the [[Stock Market Crash of 1929]].

The decade's central pattern was simultaneous modernization and resistance. New technologies and cultural expression widened possibilities, while political movements used race, religion, and nationality to restrict who could fully belong in modern America.
