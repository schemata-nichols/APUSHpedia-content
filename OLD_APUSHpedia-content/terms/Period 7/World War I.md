> **World War I** was the global conflict from 1914 to 1918 in which the United States remained officially neutral until entering on the Allied side in April 1917. German [[Unrestricted Submarine Warfare]], economic and cultural ties to the Allies, and the [[Zimmermann Telegram]] helped overcome neutrality. American troops, credit, supplies, and industrial capacity strengthened the Allied war effort, while mobilization expanded federal management of the economy and restricted dissent at home. For APUSH, the war accelerated state power, migration, and disputes over civil liberties, but the Senate's rejection of the [[League of Nations]] revealed limits to [[Woodrow Wilson]]'s internationalist vision.

## APUSH Significance

World War I linked foreign policy to major domestic changes:

1. **Neutrality gave way to intervention.** Trade, finance, submarine warfare, and security concerns drew the United States toward the Allies.
2. **Mobilization expanded federal authority.** Agencies coordinated production, labor, transportation, food, and public opinion.
3. **War transformed society.** Military service and industrial demand accelerated the [[Great Migration]] and women's wage work.
4. **Civil liberties narrowed.** The government prosecuted antiwar speech and radical activism.
5. **Peace exposed political limits.** Wilson shaped the settlement but could not secure Senate approval of the treaty.

The war did not make the United States a world power from nothing; [[American Imperialism]] had already expanded its reach. It did make American finance, military resources, and diplomacy decisive in a European war.

## Neutrality and the Road to War

When war began in Europe, Woodrow Wilson proclaimed neutrality. Americans were divided by ethnic loyalties, pacifism, preparedness, and economic interests. British naval power limited trade with Germany, while American banks and exporters developed strong ties to Britain and France.

German submarine warfare challenged neutral shipping. The 1915 sinking of the [[Lusitania]] killed Americans and increased public anger, though Germany temporarily restricted attacks after diplomatic pressure. Wilson won reelection in 1916 while emphasizing that he had kept the country out of war.

Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare in 1917, gambling that it could defeat Britain before American power arrived. The Zimmermann Telegram proposed a German-Mexican alliance against the United States. Wilson asked Congress for war to defend neutral rights and make the world “safe for democracy.”

## Mobilizing People and Production

The [[Selective Service Act]] created a national draft, and millions served in the armed forces. The American Expeditionary Forces under John J. Pershing entered major combat in 1918. Their arrival reinforced exhausted Allied armies and helped defeat Germany, although European forces bore most of the war's casualties.

The [[War Industries Board]] coordinated raw materials and production. The government managed railroads, encouraged food and fuel conservation, sold Liberty Bonds, and increased taxes. Cooperation with large corporations accelerated organizational capacity while generating profits and labor conflict.

The [[Committee on Public Information]] used posters, films, speeches, and news to promote the war. Propaganda mobilized participation but also encouraged intolerance toward German Americans and critics.

## Labor, [[Migration]], and Race

War production created factory jobs and strengthened workers' bargaining leverage. Federal boards sometimes supported the eight-hour day and union recognition to prevent strikes, though employers and workers still clashed.

Reduced European immigration and industrial demand accelerated Black migration from the rural South to northern and midwestern cities. The Great Migration offered higher wages and political opportunities while producing competition, residential segregation, and racial violence. The East St. Louis riot of 1917 and the Red Summer after the war exposed national racism.

Women entered industrial and clerical employment and served as nurses, but many were displaced from wartime jobs after demobilization. Their public service strengthened arguments for the [[Women's Suffrage Movement]], though suffrage resulted from decades of organizing rather than the war alone.

## Repression and Civil Liberties

The [[Espionage and Sedition Acts]] criminalized interference with the draft and broad criticism of the government or war. Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs received a prison sentence for an antiwar speech. Officials targeted radicals, labor organizers, and German-language culture.

In [[Schenck v. United States]], the Supreme Court upheld a conviction for distributing antidraft leaflets and accepted restrictions on speech presenting a “clear and present danger.” Wartime repression and fear of revolution fed the postwar [[First Red Scare]] and [[Palmer Raids]].

These actions show how mobilization could expand national unity while narrowing the boundaries of acceptable political participation.

## Wilsonian Peace and Rejection

Wilson's [[Fourteen Points]] called for open diplomacy, national self-determination, freer trade, arms reduction, and a League of Nations. The [[Treaty of Versailles]] included the League but also territorial settlements, reparations, and compromises with Allied demands.

Republican senators objected especially to the League's collective-security obligations. Wilson refused major reservations and undertook an unsuccessful speaking tour after which he suffered a severe stroke. The Senate rejected the treaty, and the United States made a separate peace with Germany.

Rejection did not produce complete [[Isolationism]]: the United States remained financially and diplomatically involved abroad. The war instead created an unstable combination of economic internationalism, political reluctance toward collective commitments, and unresolved tensions that shaped the interwar era.
