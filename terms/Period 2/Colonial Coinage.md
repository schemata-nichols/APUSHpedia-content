## Section 1

If you lived in colonial America in the 1600s or 1700s and wanted to buy something at a local store, pulling out standard dollar bills or quarters wasn't an option. Great Britain strictly banned the Thirteen Colonies from minting their own official silver or gold coins because, under mercantilism, all the real money was supposed to flow straight back to England. This created a massive cash shortage in the colonies, forcing people to get creative with how they paid for everyday items.

## Section 2

Instead of official British money, colonists used a wild mix of alternative currencies. In New England, colonists regularly traded using Native American wampum —polished beads carefully crafted from purple and white clam shells. In southern colonies like Virginia and Maryland, dried tobacco leaves were officially accepted as legal tender. You could literally pay your local taxes or buy groceries with bundles of cured tobacco.

## Section 3

When people actually got their hands on real metal coins, it was almost always foreign money brought in through overseas trade or privateers. The most popular coin was the Spanish Silver Dollar (famously known as "Pieces of Eight"). Because small change was so rare, colonists would take heavy shears and cut a single Spanish coin into eight pie-slice wedges called "bits." If something cost a fraction of a dollar, you handed over physical chunks of a chopped-up silver coin.

## Section 4

Massachusetts even tried to fix the coin shortage by secretly running an illegal mint in Boston starting in 1652. They stamped silver coins with a drawing of a pine tree on them, known as "Pine Tree Shillings." To avoid getting shut down by the King of England, who was the only person legally allowed to produce money—the mint stamped the exact date "1652" on every single coin they made for over thirty years. That way, if royal inspectors asked questions, the colonists could just claim all the coins were old leftovers from a single batch made decades prior.
