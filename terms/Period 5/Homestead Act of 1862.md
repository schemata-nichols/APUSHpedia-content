> The Homestead Act of 1862 was a law that provided government-owned land to settlers, known as homesteaders, who wanted to establish farms. This law, signed by Abraham Lincoln on May 20, 1862, was intended to encourage settlement and farming in the West. The law offered eligible settlers 160 acres of federal land for just a small filing fee. Under the provisions of the law, qualified people had to be the head of a household or 21 years of age. The settlers were required to live on the land for five years, build a home on it, and farm the land. If, after five years, they met the requirements, they could receive the deed to the land. For those that could not meet the requirements, often because of the hardships of frontier life, the land would revert back to the government. This law remained in effect for a long time, taking effect January 1, 1863, the same day as the Emancipation Proclamation, being repealed in 1976 for all states but Alaska. It resulted in the settlement of approximately ten percent of U.S. land.

## Section 1

The Homestead Act had a signficant impact on the development of the United States. It encouraged increased settlement in the West, which contributed to national development and economic growth during and after the Civil War. The law not only helped to encourage westward migration and agricultural development, but also promoted independent landownership for settlers from various backgrounds. This also facilitated the development of the Great Plains region, increasing the population and expanding farming communities. However, westward expansion also came with negative consequences. In order to make way for the homesteaders, tensions over land increased between Native American nations and the settlers. As conflicts worsened, large numbers of Native American tribes were removed from their ancestral lands and placed on Indian reservations.
